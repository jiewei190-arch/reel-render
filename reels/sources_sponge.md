Every page below was fetched and the quoted sentence checked.

|---|---|---|

| Most sponges today are synthetic: polyester, polyurethane, or cellulose | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bath_sponge | "Synthetic sponges can be made of polyester, polyurethane, or vegetable cellulose." / "most commonly made from synthetic materials today." |
| Touch one to a puddle, and the water climbs up into it | https://www.physicsclassroom.com/Chemistry-Tutorial/Solids-Liquids-and-Intermolecular-Forces/Capillary-Action | "A puddle of water on the kitchen countertop can be quickly absorbed by a sponge or paper towel." / adhesion "exert a pull upon water" |
| Capillary action: liquid flowing through narrow spaces, all by itself | same | "Capillary action is the tendency of a liquid to flow through narrow spaces, pores, or narrow columns ... without the assistance of external forces" |
| Each opening acts like a tiny tube | same | "Each hole or opening acts as a tiny capillary tube through which a liquid can pass." |
| Water clings to the sponge's walls: adhesion | same | "Adhesive forces between water molecules and the particles of the sponge or paper towel exert a pull upon water" |
| They cling to each other: cohesion | same | "Because of cohesive forces, the water molecules stick together." |
| Pulled up the walls, they drag their neighbours along | same | "as water molecules are pulled upward by adhesion to glass, they pull surrounding water molecules" |
| A maze of dead ends and narrow channels slows the water as it drains | https://www.sciencefocus.com/science/how-is-a-sponge-able-to-hold-so-much-water/ | "complex labyrinth of dead ends and narrow channels slows the water as it tries to drain away." |
| Squeeze: channels close, extra pressure forces water out faster | same | "compresses the channels and the extra pressure helps to force the water out more quickly." |
| Water alone won't grab grease | https://www.explainthatstuff.com/detergents.html | "Water alone can't clean clothes because it won't attach to molecules of grease and dirt." |
| Water molecules stick together in drops | same | "Water molecules prefer their own company so they tend to stick together in drops." |
| Surfactants: one end loves water, the other grease | same | "One end is strongly attracted to water; the other is attracted to oily substances like grease." |
| Grease end grabs dirt, water pulls it away | same | "The grease-loving ends of the surfactant molecules start to attach themselves to the dirt" / "The water molecules pull the surfactant and dirt away" |

Notes
- The capillary-rise mechanism (adhesion pulling, cohesion dragging neighbours) is stated for a glass tube on the Physics Classroom page and applied there to sponges and paper towels; the script applies it the same way.
- The detergent page's example is laundry; "water alone won't attach to grease" is a general property and is used generally.
