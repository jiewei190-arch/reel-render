# Sources: parachute (how a parachute slows you down)

Both pages fetched with WebFetch on 2026-10-27; every claim below is quoted or directly derived from them.

| Line | Claim | Source (fetched) |
|---|---|---|
| 0, 8 | Belly-to-Earth free fall is "about 122 miles per hour" (hook says about 120); head-down with limbs pulled in reaches "close to 200 miles per hour" | https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/sports-and-leisure/mathematics-skydiving |
| 1, 2 | Two forces on a falling object: gravity, "expressed as the weight of the object", and "the air resistance, or drag" | https://www.grc.nasa.gov/WWW/k-12/VirtualAero/BottleRocket/airplane/termvr.html |
| 3, 4 | Drag D = Cd · r · V² · A / 2: drag depends on "the square of the air velocity" | NASA page above |
| 5 | Double speed → about four times the drag (arithmetic from the V² term) | NASA page above |
| 6, 7, 14 | "When drag is equal to weight, there is no net external force", so the object falls at a constant velocity, terminal velocity | NASA page above |
| 10 | "an object with a large cross-sectional area or a high drag coefficient will fall slower" | NASA page above |
| 11 | "The parachute reduces the terminal velocity to the five to 10 miles per hour range" | EBSCO page above |
| 12 | The Army's "round 35-foot diameter parachute" gives "a terminal velocity of about 15 feet per second" | EBSCO page above |
| 13 | Weight unchanged; the canopy raises drag (area and drag coefficient) | NASA + EBSCO pages above |
