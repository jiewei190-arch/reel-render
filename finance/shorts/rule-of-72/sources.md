# Sources and maths: rule-of-72

**The rule:** years to double ≈ 72 ÷ (annual rate in percent). It is an approximation of the exact compound-growth
answer, years = ln 2 ÷ ln(1 + r).
- Investor.gov (SEC) glossary, "Rule of 72": https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/rule-72
- Investor.gov (SEC), "Compound interest": https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/compound-interest
- Secondary explainer: Bankrate, "Rule of 72: What it is and how to use it": https://www.bankrate.com/investing/what-is-the-rule-of-72/

| VO | rule of 72 | exact, annual compounding: ln2 / ln(1+r) |
|---|---|---|
| "Six percent? About twelve years." | 72 / 6 = 12 | 11.90 years |
| "Eight percent? About nine." | 72 / 8 = 9 | 9.01 years |
| "Twelve percent? About six." | 72 / 12 = 6 | 6.12 years |
| "A card charging twenty-four percent can double what you owe in about three years." | 72 / 24 = 3 | 3.22 years (annual); 2.92 years if the 24% APR compounds monthly at 2%/month: ln2 / ln(1.02) / 12 |

The card line assumes **no payments and no new charges**, interest compounding on the unpaid balance. "Can double" is
hedged because real balances depend on payments, fees and how the issuer compounds.
24% is an illustrative APR, not an average; for context the Federal Reserve's G.19 shows the average rate on
credit-card accounts assessed interest at about 22% (see minimum-payment-trap/sources.md):
https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/current/default.htm

Hedge said in the VO: "It's a shortcut, not a promise. Real returns go up and down." The percentages are example rates,
not expected returns from anything.
