A judge sued his dry cleaner for $54 million. Over one pair of pants.
Not a typo. Fifty-four million dollars, for trousers that went missing after a simple alteration.
He went to trial. He cried on the stand. And a small family business spent years defending a sign in its window.
How does a man who judges cases for a living end up here?
It became one of the most talked-about lawsuits in America.
This is the story of Pearson versus Chung.
In 2005, Roy Pearson worked as an administrative law judge in Washington, D.C.
That spring, he brought a pair of suit pants to Custom Cleaners, a small family shop near his home.
The job was simple: a $10.50 alteration.
The owners, Jin Nam Chung, Soo Chung and their son Ki, tagged the pants and got to work.
Then the pants went missing.
A week later, the family found a pair of pants with his ticket. He looked at them and said, "These are not my pants."
So what would it take to make this right?
The Chungs tried to settle. They offered $3,000. Then $4,600. Then $12,000.
For a pair of pants. He said no.
Instead, he filed a lawsuit asking for $67 million.
His case rested on two signs in the shop window: Satisfaction Guaranteed, and Same Day Service.
To him, those signs were a promise, and the promise had been broken.
He charged $1,500 per violation, per day, for each of three owners.
Before trial, he cut the demand to $54 million. Still a number no dry cleaner could ever pay.
But would a court agree that a sign is a promise?
The trial began in June 2007.
Pearson argued his own case. On the stand, talking about the pants, he cried.
On June 25, 2007, Judge Judith Bartnoff ruled.
Her ruling said no reasonable customer reads Satisfaction Guaranteed as a promise to meet unreasonable demands.
He demanded $54 million. He was awarded nothing.
He appealed. In 2008, he lost again.
One pair of pants, years in court, and a sign that never meant what he said it meant.
