## The model: four "view jails"
| Jail | Typical views | What's wrong | Escape route |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0-view** | 0–~100 | Reposted or mass-produced AI content. Example: a 169K-sub all-AI channel whose last five uploads got 0–2 views | Make original videos. The AI-slop path also risks demonetization [0:41–1:55] |
| **1K** | 300–1,000 | The seed audience doesn't take to it. Posting *more of the same* doesn't help | Change something real: the **format** (how it's presented), the **topic/premise** (what the channel is about), or both [2:04–4:56] |
| **10K** | 3K–10K | The video works for your niche, but the wider audience it reaches next doesn't know or care about the niche | Make it **relatable to outsiders**: give a reason to care up front, then a story that's hard to swipe away from. Fix the middle using the retention graph [5:04–10:15] |
| **30K** | 20K–40K every upload | Topic and format are proven; the problem is a "leak" at an exact second | **Tighten the pacing.** Find the second people leave (start, middle or end) and cut it. End abruptly [10:27–12:30] |

## Tactics, in order
1. **[0:41] Don't mass-produce AI content.** The video's examples:
   - an AI-only channel at 169K subs now getting 0–2 views;
   - "Creatures 101", AI cat videos stuck under 100 views.

   YouTube allows AI content, but warns against "mass-produced" uploads that aren't really yours. The risk is demonetization even after a channel grows. *(Relevant to us: see the takeaways.)*
2. **[2:11] Jails are ranges, not exact numbers.** "1K jail" covers anything from 300 to 900 views. Posting more is the obvious reaction, and it's the wrong one.
3. **[2:29] Change the format: add motion.** C Jedi Knight reviewed games standing in front of a still Steam store page and got 248–553 views. One Short put **moving gameplay behind her while she talked** and broke out. Same topic, different presentation.
4. **[3:20] Change the premise: one clear subject.** Ealanation TV jumped between Filipino combat sports, pop culture and different personalities. After a year away it came back focused on one rising tennis player, and Shorts went to 1.2K, 3K and 19K views. The editing didn't change. **A viewer can now tell at a glance what the channel is for.**
5. **[4:18] How Shorts spread** (Todd Sherman, head of YouTube Shorts). The algorithm finds a **seed audience** first. Depending on how they react, the video either spreads further or tapers off.
6. **[4:41] Pivot, don't spam.** If original Shorts keep stalling at ~1K, change the topic, the format or both.
7. **[5:27] Why videos stall at 10K.** Each wider ring of viewers knows less about you and your niche. Videos made for insiders "fall apart" at that point.
8. **[5:59] Effort isn't enough; storytelling is.** Smashed Brothers Gaming's biggest Short:
   - a thrift store has a donated VHS collection;
   - twist: it's covered in mold;
   - he restores a tape with a strange machine;
   - payoff: the tape plays.

   You don't need to care what's on the tapes to want to watch.
9. **[6:55] Compare: niche framing loses outsiders.** "I finally completed my collection of this forgotten Ghostbusters series" only works for collectors. Their suggested rewrite: *"I just completed one of the rarest cartoon collections you've probably never heard of."* Same video with a wider way in.
10. **[7:46] No slow intros.** "What's up guys, it's a Sunday night…" lost the viewer before a long-delayed horror-movie review. The channel's biggest Short instead **ranks every movie in a new horror box set**. It's clear right away, has structure, and is worth something to anyone thinking of buying the set.
11. **[8:42] Nostalgia widens the audience.** xrevains posts generic Call of Duty trick shots that get a few thousand views. "We have to bring these back on BO2" got 29K, and a nostalgia Short got 700K+. The pull was the memory of playing Black Ops 2, not the trick shot.
12. **[9:23] Ask: does a viewer have to be deep in the niche to care?** If yes, give them something to grab first:
    - a **mystery**;
    - a **problem to solve**;
    - a **decision to make**;
    - a **memory**;
    - something **relatable**.
13. **[9:48] Use the retention graph.** If viewers leave halfway, the hook was fine and the middle got boring.
14. **[10:31] At 30K, look for the leak.** You don't need a new idea, just tighter pacing. Their example is Youthful Angel F's 3D Kirby meme animations:
    - with no face or personality on screen, pacing is all that holds viewers;
    - one second too long matters a lot in a ~15 s Short;
    - viewers who "get the joke" swipe away before the end.

    "One extra second can cost you potentially hundreds of thousands of views."
15. **[12:05] Read the drop-off.**
    - Drop right at the start: the opening leaks.
    - Drop after the hook: the middle leaks.
    - Drop at the end: you signalled that the video was ending.
16. **[12:13] End abruptly.** Don't say "thanks for watching", don't ask for a subscribe, don't push long-form. "Just get out of there."
17. **[12:38] Views ≠ revenue.** YouTube recently changed how it counts Shorts views, so more views may not bring more watch time or revenue. They point to another vidIQ video on this; it's not covered here.

## Monetization
This video says little directly about money. What it does say:
- Mass-produced or reposted AI content risks **demonetization**, even if the channel already has subscribers [1:40].
- **Views no longer equal watch time or revenue** after YouTube's change to how Shorts views are counted [12:38]. Judge Shorts by engaged views and retention, not the raw view count.

## Takeaways for Justin clips and the reel studio (my reading)
- **Where we are:** most of the 18 public Shorts sit at **1.2K–1.5K views**. Two "what if" Shorts reached 2.7K and one sits at 13. By this model that is the **top of 1K jail**.
  - The fix is a real change in format or premise, not more volume. Our 14 scheduled Shorts all use one format, so their numbers will test whether that format can get past 1K.
- **AI-content risk** (point 1):
  - Our reels are AI-assisted: AI voice, generated visuals, "altered/synthetic = Yes".
  - To stay clear of "mass-produced" we need to keep visible **original work** in each one: researched facts, sources, our own art and kit, a consistent host or character.
  - We should not post near-duplicates (the held v2 re-cuts of topics already public, e.g. Coca-Cola).
- **Clear premise** (point 4): the Justin clips feed mixes stickman "what if", history oddities, animal facts and an Nvidia/tech Short. One stated premise would help viewers recognise the channel, e.g. *"weird true history you never heard of"*.
- **Wide way in, not niche** (points 9–12): open with the most relatable framing. Example: "Napoleon was attacked by rabbits", not "the 1807 hunting incident".
- **Hooks** (point 12): each topic should open with a mystery, problem or decision.
- **Drop the closing beat** (point 16): our templates shouldn't end on a CTA or a "follow for more" card; cut on the payoff. Check the current template endings.
- **Check retention per Short** (points 13–15): the pending channel scan (stayed-to-watch % plus each retention graph) is how we'd find leaks. That's another reason to run it once the scheduled Shorts have data.
