# icecream: sources

1. Hotelling, H. (1929), "Stability in Competition", The Economic Journal 39 (153): 41-57.
   - Coins the "undue tendency for competitors to imitate each other in quality of goods, in location, and in other essential ways" (p. 41).
   - On two-party politics: "each party strives to make its platform as much like the other's as possible" (p. 54).
2. Ridley, D. B., "Hotelling's Law", Palgrave Encyclopedia of Strategic Management (draft, Duke University): https://people.duke.edu/~dbr1/research/Hotellings-Law.pdf
   - "the most profitable location is next to a competitor in the middle of a geographic or product space."
   - "The classic example is ice-cream vendors locating near one another on a beach."
   - The model: consumers "evenly dispersed along a line" buy "from the nearest firm"; "The two firms choose to locate at the mid-point of the line. A firm that unilaterally moves away from the mid-point loses market share and profit."
   - It "explains why retailers and restaurants so often locate near one another"; it also covers politicians.
   - Caveats: with a third firm there is no equilibrium (Chamberlin 1933); when firms also choose prices, they move apart (d'Aspremont, Gabszewicz & Thisse 1979, Econometrica 47: 1145-1150).
3. Wikipedia, "Hotelling's law" (the ice-cream beach example; the quarter / three-quarter spots as the "socially optimal" split): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotelling%27s_law

Wording notes:
- "same ice cream, same price" is stated on purpose: the side-by-side result is for fixed, equal prices (with price competition they can move apart, source 2).
- Quarter and three-quarter spots: each cart's customers walk at most a quarter of the beach. Side by side in the middle: people at the far ends walk up to half the beach, "twice as far" (simple geometry of the model).
- "It's why shops, and even politicians, so often look alike": Hotelling's own extension (sources 1 and 2). Real clustering has other causes too (source 2 lists them).
- "STEPS APART" on the sign is a story prop, not a measurement.
