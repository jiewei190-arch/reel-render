# Sources: newroad (the town that built a new road and traffic got worse: Braess's paradox)

- Primary: D. Braess, "Über ein Paradoxon aus der Verkehrsplanung", Unternehmensforschung 12:258-268 (1968); English translation: D. Braess, A. Nagurney, T. Wakolbinger, "On a paradox of traffic planning", Transportation Science 39(4):446-450 (2005).
- University: Anna Nagurney, UMass Amherst Isenberg School (Virtual Center for Supernetworks), "The Braess Paradox": https://supernet.isenberg.umass.edu/facts/braess.html
- The worked example (4,000 drivers, a 45-minute fixed road and a crowd-dependent T/100 road on each route, 65 vs 80 minutes): the standard textbook illustration of Braess's network, as given at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braess%27s_paradox
- Stuttgart: Gina Kolata, "What if They Closed 42d Street and Nobody Noticed?", The New York Times, Dec 25, 1990 (reprint: https://www.tampabay.com/archive/1990/12/25/the-solution-to-traffic-jams-close-down-a-street), citing a 1969 German study of Stuttgart; also UMass (above).
- Seoul: S. Chung, K. Hwang, H. Bae, "The effects of removing the Cheonggyecheon elevated expressway…", Transport Policy 21:165-178 (2012), https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/trapol/v21y2012icp165-178.html ; Global Designing Cities Initiative, "Case Study: Cheonggyecheon, Seoul": https://globaldesigningcities.org/publication/global-street-design-guide/streets/special-conditions/elevated-structure-removal/case-study-cheonggyecheon-seoul-korea/

| Claim | Source |
|---|---|
| A new road can make traffic worse for everyone (Braess's paradox) | Braess 1968; UMass: "when a new link is added, the change in equilibrium flows might result in a higher cost, implying that users were better off without that link" |
| 4,000 drivers, two routes, each a crowd-dependent narrow street plus a 45-minute highway; split evenly, 65 minutes each way | The standard worked example (T/100 + 45 with 2,000 drivers each: 20 + 45 = 65) |
| A free shortcut joining the two narrow streets; every driver takes narrow street, shortcut, narrow street; 80 minutes for everyone | Same example: with a zero-cost link all 4,000 take it, 4000/100 + 0 + 4000/100 = 80 |
| When everyone picks their own best route, the whole town can lose | Braess 1968 (user-optimised equilibrium vs the system optimum); UMass |
| Stuttgart opened a new road in 1969, traffic got worse, so they closed it again | Kolata 1990 (NYT), citing the 1969 Stuttgart study; UMass: "when a new road was constructed in Stuttgart, Germany, traffic flow worsened and only improved after the road was torn up" |
| Seoul tore down a downtown highway, and more people rode the bus and subway | Chung, Hwang & Bae 2012 (more bus and subway riders, fewer car trips into downtown after the Cheonggyecheon expressway was removed); GDCI: +15.1% bus and +3.3% subway ridership 2003-2008 |

Softened on purpose: no claim that Seoul's driving got faster (the 2012 study found slower surface-road speeds at first); the town, the mayor and the numbers are the textbook example, not a real town.
