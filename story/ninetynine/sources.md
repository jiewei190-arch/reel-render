# Sources: "Why do prices end in .99?" (ninetynine)

Claims in the narration, and how each one is hedged.

| Line | Claim | Source |
|---|---|---|
| s5-s7 | People read prices left to right and anchor on the first digit, so $2.99 can feel like "two-something". Researchers call it the left-digit effect. | Manoj Thomas & Vicki Morwitz, "Penny Wise and Pound Foolish: The Left-Digit Effect in Price Cognition", *Journal of Consumer Research* 32(1), 54-64, 2005. doi:10.1086/429600. Summary: ScienceDaily, "Why $2.99 Seems So Much Cheaper Than $3.00" (2005). |
| s8 | The effect mostly works when the first digit changes ($3.00 to $2.99, not $3.60 to $3.59). Narration says "mostly". | Thomas & Morwitz 2005 (same paper): the effect appeared when the left-most digit changed and not when it stayed the same. |
| s9 | In field tests with a clothing catalog, prices ending in 9 sold more. | Eric T. Anderson & Duncan I. Simester, "Effects of $9 Price Endings on Retail Sales: Evidence from Field Experiments", *Quantitative Marketing and Economics* 1, 93-110, 2003. doi:10.1023/A:1023581927405. Three experiments with a women's clothing catalog; 9-endings increased demand. |
| s10 | A 9 can also signal "this is a deal" (hedged: "can"). | Anderson & Simester 2003 discuss 9-endings as a cue that a price is low or discounted; also Schindler & Kibarian, "Increased consumer sales response through use of 99-ending prices", *Journal of Retailing* 72(2), 1996. |
| s11 | Legend: odd prices forced cashiers to open the register to give change, which stopped theft. Narration says "Nobody's proven it." | The story is widely repeated without primary evidence; see Steven Landsburg, *The Armchair Economist* (1993), which discusses it as a folk explanation. Presented as an unproven legend only. |

Not used: a widely shared "$34 vs $39 dress" figure (reported in a Harvard Business Review piece) because the original could not be checked here (paywalled).
