# Sources — The Ice Aircraft Carrier (Project Habakkuk)

Checked 20 Nov 2026 against the owner's card source: Wikipedia, "Project Habakkuk" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Habakkuk
(fetched and read in full this session). Quotes are from that article.

| Claim in the video | What the article says |
|---|---|
| Britain planned an aircraft carrier made of ice, to fight U-boats | "a plan by the British during the Second World War to construct an aircraft carrier out of pykrete, a mixture of wood pulp and ice", "for use against German U-boats in the mid-Atlantic, which were beyond the flight range of land-based planes at that time." |
| U-boats were hunting convoys where planes couldn't reach | Same lead line; Pyke "had been considering the problem of how to protect seaborne landings and Atlantic convoys out of reach of aircraft cover." |
| Inventor Geoffrey Pyke's fix: a floating airfield made of ice | Pyke "decided that the answer was ice"; the material was "named after Pyke". |
| Pykrete = wood pulp mixed with water, then frozen; not plain ice | "a mixture of water and woodpulp that when frozen was stronger than plain ice"; optimum "14 per cent wood pulp and 86 per cent water". |
| Code name Habakkuk; 600 metres long; would have been the biggest ship ever | "what would have been the largest ship ever at 600 metres (1,969 ft) long". (Requirements: deck "2,000 ft (610 m) long".) |
| 1943 conference, Mountbatten **reportedly** fired his pistol at a block of it; the bullet bounced off and ricocheted around the room | "According to some accounts, at the Quebec Conference in 1943 Lord Louis Mountbatten brought a block of pykrete along…"; "Next he fired at the pykrete…"; "The bullet ricocheted off the block, grazing the trouser leg of Admiral Ernest King, and ended up in the wall." Alan Brooke's diaries support the account: "the bullet rebounded out of the block and buzzed round our legs like an angry bee." |
| A prototype was built on Patricia Lake, Canada, chilled by a one-horsepower motor | "a small prototype was constructed at Patricia Lake, Alberta, measuring 60 by 30 feet (18 metres by 9 metres), weighing 1,000 tons and kept frozen by a one-horsepower motor." |
| Costs ballooned | "The cost was estimated at £700,000." … "This caused the cost estimate to grow to £2.5 million." |
| Longer-range planes closed the gap | "the availability of longer-range aircraft and escort carriers which closed the Mid-Atlantic gap that the project was intended to address"; "The introduction of long-range fuel tanks allowed British-based aircraft extra patrol time over the Atlantic." |
| By the end of 1943 it was shelved | "The final meeting of the Habakkuk board took place in December 1943." Conclusion quoted: "found to be impractical because of the enormous production resources required and technical difficulties involved." |
| The real ship was never built | The lead: "would have been the largest ship ever"; the project ended before construction (card: "killed the project before construction ever began"). |
| The prototype took three summers to melt | "It took three hot summers to completely melt the prototype constructed in Canada." |

## Hedges kept / notes
- **"Reportedly"** on the pistol story, as on the owner's card: the article opens it with "According to some accounts" and notes
  a conflicting account (Max Perutz: a demonstration at COHQ by another officer, with the bullet hitting Alan Brooke). The video
  names no one the bullet hit and shows a cartoon tracer only.
- **"Ricocheted around the room"** is the card's wording; the article's sources say it grazed King's trouser leg / "buzzed round our
  legs" and ended in the wall. Kept as a light paraphrase.
- **"At a 1943 conference"**: the article says the Quebec Conference, 1943 (per some accounts). The card says the demonstration was to
  "Churchill and Roosevelt's staffs"; the video just says "a 1943 conference" (no wording beyond the article).
- **Cost figures** are not spoken (the £ tag is a cartoon); the article's £700,000 → £2.5 million is the basis for "ballooned".
- **"Biggest ship ever"** is said as "would have been".
- **Prototype year** is not stated (the article gives 1943 testing in one place and "built in 1944" in another).

the generals' poses, the bullet's zig-zag path, the folder, the three suns. Characters are generic beans, not likenesses.
