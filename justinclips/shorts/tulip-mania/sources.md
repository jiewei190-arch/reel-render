# Sources: Tulip mania (1630s)

Checked Nov 16 2026 (WebSearch + WebFetch of Wikipedia and Smithsonian). Quotes are verbatim from the pages listed.

## Every claim, sourced
- **"A flower once cost more than a house."** Smithsonian Magazine, "There Never Was a Real Tulip Fever" (2017): https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/there-never-was-real-tulip-fever-180964915/ says the Semper Augustus "sold for more than the cost of a mansion in a fashionable Amsterdam neighborhood." Wikipedia, "Tulip mania": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulip_mania says "certain tulip bulbs sold for more than 10 times the annual income of a skilled artisan." The hook states the traditional claim. Line 5 carries the hedge ("reportedly"), and the payoff gives the modern correction.
- **Holland, the 1630s; tulips were in huge demand.** Wikipedia: "The major acceleration started in 1634 and then dramatically collapsed in February 1637"; "Tulip mania reached its peak during the winter of 1636–37." ("Holland" is the popular name. The trade centred on Holland's towns, e.g. Haarlem and Amsterdam, in the Dutch Republic.)
- **The rarest had flame stripes caused by a virus.** Wikipedia: "this effect is produced when the bulbs are infected with a type of tulip-specific mosaic virus." Smithsonian: "the result of a mosaic virus." The on-screen "(harmless to people)" follows from Wikipedia's "tulip-specific".
- **Bulbs traded while still in the ground, on paper, in taverns.** Wikipedia: florists "signed forward contracts before a notary to buy tulips at the end of the season"; "Traders met in 'college' at taverns"; the trade was called "windhandel ('air trade'), because no bulbs were actually changing hands."
- **One bulb reportedly sold for 10,000 guilders.** Wikipedia: "The Semper Augustus was the most expensive tulip during the mania" and "at its peak sold for ƒ10,000". HEDGED with "reportedly". The figure rests on old accounts and is not confirmed by official records.
- **A craftsman made about 300 a year.** Wikipedia: "A skilled artisan at the time earned about ƒ300 per year." The on-screen "33 YEARS OF PAY" is 10,000 ÷ 300 ≈ 33.
- **February 1637, the market crashed.** Wikipedia ("dramatically collapsed in February 1637"); Smithsonian ("by February 1637, the bottom fell out of the market").
- **A historian who checked the records couldn't find anyone who went bankrupt.** Smithsonian, quoting historian Anne Goldgar (author of *Tulipmania: Money, Honor, and Knowledge in the Dutch Golden Age*, 2007): "I couldn't find anybody that went bankrupt." Wikipedia: "Many modern scholars believe that the mania was not as destructive as [Mackay] describes"; "It had no critical influence on the prosperity of the Dutch Republic." Economist Peter Garber also argued the speculation "was not obvious madness" (Smithsonian).

## Kept hedges (brief: "recent historians say the crash was less dramatic")
- "reportedly" on the 10,000-guilder sale (VO + on-screen "REPORTEDLY").
- The payoff IS the hedge. The legend of ruin comes from Charles Mackay's 1841 *Extraordinary Popular Delusions*, and the historian who checked the archives (Goldgar) found no bankruptcies. She is named on screen.
- The famous anecdote of a sailor eating a bulb he took for an onion is left out on purpose. Smithsonian: "none of these stories are true."

## Dramatised (comedy, not claimed as fact)
The giant balance scale, the coin bursts, the tulip stall crowd and hearts, the cute virus with a paintbrush, the SOLD sign over the soil, the price chart, the calendar flip. All characters are original toons, not likenesses (the historian is a generic modern toon, not a portrait of Anne Goldgar).
