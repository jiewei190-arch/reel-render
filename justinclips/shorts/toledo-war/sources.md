| 1 | Ohio and Michigan nearly went to war over Toledo | W1: "The Toledo War (1835–1836) ... was a boundary dispute between the state of Ohio and the adjoining Michigan Territory over the Toledo Strip." | 2 | A bad map; a strip 5 to 8 miles wide | W1: "Poor geographical understanding of the Great Lakes helped to produce conflicting state and federal legislation"; the Mitchell Map "showed the southerly extreme of Lake Michigan at a latitude north of the mouth of the Detroit River"; the Toledo Strip "spanned a region five to eight miles (8 to 13 km) wide". LOC: "Militias were mustered in both states and sent to the border." TA: "Governor Mason moved 1,000 Michigan soldiers further south into Toledo." / "Ohio forces, now stationed just south of Toledo in Perrysburg." |
| 4 | The one battle: reportedly shots into the air, nobody hit | W1: "The single military confrontation of the war ended with a report of shots being fired into the air, incurring no casualties." | 5 | The war's only injury: a deputy, hurt with a penknife | W1: "The only blood spilled was the non-fatal stabbing of a law enforcement officer." LOC: "the only blood shed ... was that of Michigan deputy sheriff called Wood, who was stabbed (non-fatally) with a pen knife". |
| 6 | By Two Stickney; his brother reportedly named One | W1 names "Two Stickney" (see line 5). TA: "Two Stickney, son of Major Benjamin Stickney and brother of One Stickney, stabbed Monroe County Deputy Sheriff Joseph Wood". WA: "an eccentric who had numbered his sons One and Two rather than naming them." (One's name is not in W1 → "Reportedly".) |
| 7 | Congress stepped in: Ohio got Toledo; Michigan got statehood + the Upper Peninsula | W1: "the United States Congress proposed a compromise whereby Michigan gave up its claim to the strip in exchange for its statehood and the remaining three-quarters of the Upper Peninsula." "On January 26, 1837, Michigan was admitted to the Union as the 26th state, without the Toledo Strip but with the entire Upper Peninsula." TA: "the only way Michigan could gain statehood was to end the border conflict, turn over the Toledo Strip, and accept most of what is now the Upper Peninsula." |
| 8 | A consolation prize almost everyone called a worthless wilderness | W1: "the compromise was considered a poor deal for Michigan at the time"; "The Upper Peninsula was considered a worthless wilderness by almost all familiar with the area, valuable only for timber and fur trapping." W2: "Michigan was considered the losing party in the Toledo compromise." MTU: "most people in 1835 viewed the Upper Peninsula as a barren wasteland". WA: "the UP was widely written off as a frozen, unfarmable wilderness". |
| 9a | In the 1840s they found copper and iron | W1: "the discovery of copper in the Keweenaw Peninsula and iron in the Central Upper Peninsula in the 1840s led to a mining boom". | 9b | By the 1860s it supplied 90% of America's copper | W2: "The Upper Peninsula supplied 90% of America's copper by the 1860s."
