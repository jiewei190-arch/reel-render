# Sources: What If You Went to Space Without a Suit?

| Line / claim | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| "You have about ten seconds" / "about ten seconds in, your brain runs out of oxygen" / "Lights out" | NASA *Bioastronautics Data Book*, 2nd ed., NASA SP-3006 (1973), ch. "Barometric pressure", quoted in G. A. Landis (NASA Glenn Research Center), "Human Exposure to Vacuum": http://www.geoffreylandis.com/vacuum.html : "Some degree of consciousness will probably be retained for 9 to 11 seconds". FAA Advisory Circular 61-107: time of useful consciousness above 50,000 ft is 9-12 s (same page). Wikipedia, "Effect of spaceflight on the human body": "After 9 to 12 seconds, the deoxygenated blood reaches the brain, and it results in the loss of consciousness." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effect_of_spaceflight_on_the_human_body ; BBC Sky at Night: "may remain conscious for up to 12 seconds" https://www.skyatnightmagazine.com/space-missions/astronaut-take-off-helmet-in-space | Softened to "about ten seconds". |
| "But you don't explode. That's a movie thing." | BBC Sky at Night Magazine (above): "contrary to vintage sci-fi, the astronaut won't immediately die, explode or freeze." Landis (above): "you wouldn't explode". | Visuals show no swelling (the real effect, ebullism, is not depicted). |
| "You don't freeze solid either. Space is basically a giant thermos." | Landis (above), "Would You Freeze? No. ... What space is, though, is a very good insulator. (In fact, vacuum is the secret behind thermos bottles.)" ; Sky at Night (above): won't immediately "freeze". | |
| "The spit on your tongue, though? That boils." | Water boils at body temperature at near-vacuum pressure (Armstrong limit, ~6.3 kPa): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armstrong_limit ; NASA SP-3006 via Landis: water vapour forms in the soft tissues; Sky at Night: the 1966 engineer's last sensation was "the [saliva] on his tongue boiling away". | Exposed saliva is the visual; shown as cartoon fizz bubbles. |
| "In 1966, a NASA test volunteer felt it bubbling... right before he blacked out." | Guinness World Records, "Highest equivalent altitude exposure survived" (14 Dec 1966, Houston, NASA spacesuit test in a vacuum chamber; a hose disconnected; ~120,000 ft equivalent; "recalled the sensation of feeling his own saliva boiling off his tongue just before he passed out"): https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/447311-highest-equivalent-altitude-exposure-survived ; Wikipedia (above): "I could feel the saliva on my tongue starting to bubble just before I went unconscious"; conscious for about 14 s. | Topic bank rule: the volunteer is not named. His suit leaked inside a test chamber (shown as a chamber test, not open space). |
| "They pumped the air back in, in under ninety seconds." | Guinness (above): "It took 87 seconds for the chamber to be repressurised to 14,000 ft equivalent altitude, at which point [he] regained consciousness." Wikipedia (above): "The chamber was repressurized in 1 minute instead of the normal 30 minutes." | 87 s < 90 s; both sources fit. Chip shows "87 s". |
| "He woke up fine." | Guinness (above): "He suffered no long-term effects." Wikipedia (above): "recovered almost immediately with just an earache and no permanent damage." | |
