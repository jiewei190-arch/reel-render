# D.B. Cooper (1971) - sourced claims for a 70-second reel

Source key (URLs used):
- [NA] National Archives, Seattle: https://www.archives.gov/seattle/d-b-cooper
- [WIKI] Wikipedia, D. B. Cooper: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D._B._Cooper
- [HL] HistoryLink essay 23059: https://www.historylink.org/file/23059
- [NPR] NPR, "FBI is giving up on solving the mystery of D.B. Cooper" (July 13, 2016): https://www.npr.org/sections/thetwo-way/2016/07/13/485839902/fbi-is-giving-up-on-solving-the-mystery-of-d-b-cooper
- [BRIT] Encyclopaedia Britannica, D. B. Cooper: https://britannica.com/biography/D-B-Cooper
- [USPA] USPA, "The Secrets of D.B. Cooper, Part One: Notorious Flight 305": https://www.uspa.org/the-secrets-of-db-cooper-part-one-notorious-flight-305
- [HN] HistoryNet, "Legend of D.B. Cooper": https://historynet.com/legend-of-d-b-cooper-what-happened-to-historys-most-famous-hijacker/

Note on primary sources: the fbi.gov case pages returned client errors when fetched, so no FBI page is quoted directly. The FBI's own words come through NPR (a spokesperson quote) and the FBI statement as reported in [NPR] and [WIKI]. The National Archives page is the primary-archive source, but it gives only the year 1971.

## Claims

1. On November 24, 1971, a man hijacked Northwest Orient Flight 305 on a Portland-to-Seattle trip. (17 words)
   - Sources: [BRIT], [HL], [USPA], [WIKI]; [NA] confirms the flight and airline but gives only the year.
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

2. He demanded $200,000 and four parachutes in exchange for the passengers' safe return. (14 words)
   - Sources: [NA] (ransom for passengers), [BRIT] ($200,000), [HL] (four parachutes), [USPA] (four parachutes).
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

3. He received about 10,000 unmarked $20 bills, and the FBI had already recorded their serial numbers. (16 words)
   - Sources: [WIKI] (10,000 unmarked $20 bills), [HL] (serial numbers recorded on microfilm beforehand), [USPA] (serial numbers already recorded).
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

4. After the passengers were freed in Seattle, the plane flew on, and he jumped between Seattle and Reno, Nevada. (19 words)
   - Sources: [NA] (jumped between Seattle and Reno), [BRIT] (released passengers in Seattle; jump between Seattle and Reno).
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

5. Around 8 p.m., he lowered the plane's rear stairs and jumped out into rain and darkness over Washington state. (19 words)
   - Sources: [BRIT] (around 8 p.m., rear steps), [HL] (shortly after 8 p.m.), [USPA] (rain and snow flurries; exact time given as 8:15 p.m.).
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources
   - Note: the exact minute varies by source (8:12 in [HN], 8:15 in [USPA]). Use "around 8 p.m."

6. Early searches centered on Ariel, Washington, and the FBI later moved its estimate toward the Washougal River area. (19 words)
   - Sources: [USPA] (Ariel first, then about 40 miles east near Washougal), [WIKI] (Lake Merwin/Ariel area, then Washougal River watershed).
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

7. The exact landing spot was never found, and no direct evidence from the jump has ever been recovered. (18 words)
   - Sources: [WIKI] (no direct evidence from the jump), [HN] (exact jump and landing location unknown), [BRIT] (exact landing or jump location unknown).
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

8. One of the four parachutes he took was a non-working training dummy, and one reserve was found opened. (19 words)
   - Sources: [WIKI] (one reserve was a non-functional training "dummy" chute; agents recovered one opened reserve), [HL] (one parachute sewn shut), [USPA] (dummy reserve).
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources
   - Note: the [USPA] text does not clearly say which reserve was opened. Keep the claim at "one was a dummy" for the reel.

9. On February 10, 1980, an eight-year-old boy found about $5,800 in decayed $20 bills on a Columbia River sandbar. (22 words)
   - Sources: [WIKI] (about $5,800, February 10, 1980), [HL] (three packets, February 10, 1980, eight-year-old finder), [BRIT] ($5,800, boy, Columbia River), [HN] (February 10, 1980, $5,800).
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources
   - Note: the finder's name is in [HL] and [WIKI]. It is left out of this file because he is a private individual who was a child at the time.

10. The riverbar, called Tena Bar (also spelled Tina Bar), sits on the Washington side of the Columbia, downstream from Vancouver. (20 words)
    - Sources: [HL] (Tena Bar, Washington side, about 10 miles northwest of Vancouver), [WIKI] (Tina Bar, about 9 miles downstream from Vancouver).
    - Confidence: verified by 2 sources
    - Note: the spelling is Tena in [HL] and Tina in [WIKI]. Use "a riverbar on the Columbia River" in narration to avoid the spelling issue.

11. The 1980 find is the only known recovery of the ransom money, and the rest has never been found. (19 words)
    - Sources: [HL] (the boy was the first and only known person to find any of it), [BRIT] (a later search turned up nothing else), [WIKI] (the rest of the ransom was never recovered).
    - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

12. Investigators found a black clip-on tie that the hijacker left behind, and DNA from it did not identify him. (20 words)
    - Sources: [WIKI] (black clip-on tie, found in seat 18-E; partial DNA profile could not be linked to Cooper), [BRIT] (DNA recovered from a tie the hijacker removed), [HL] (photo caption: black tie with gold pin, left behind on November 24, 1971).
    - Confidence: verified by 2 sources
    - Note: [BRIT] does not say where or when the tie was found. [HN] gives seat 18E for Cooper's seat, not for the tie.

13. In July 2016, the FBI stopped actively investigating, saying it had redirected resources to other priorities. (18 words)
    - Sources: [NPR] (FBI spokesperson quote on redirected resources), [HL] (closed July 11, 2016), [WIKI] (suspended active investigation, July 8, 2016), [BRIT] (closed in 2016).
    - Confidence: verified by 2 sources
    - Note: the exact day conflicts between [HL] (July 11) and [WIKI] (July 8). Use "July 2016" in the reel unless a source confirms the day.

14. The FBI calls the name "D.B. Cooper" a press myth, and the hijacker's real identity is still unknown. (18 words)
    - Sources: [NPR] (FBI statement that the name is "apparently, a myth created by the press"), [BRIT] ("Dan Cooper" was an alias; true identity unknown), [HN] ("Dan Cooper" alias), [USPA] (the name given was "Dan Cooper").
    - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

15. Whether he survived the jump is unknown, and no suspect has been definitively identified. (14 words)
    - Sources: [BRIT] (whether he survived is unknown; true identity unknown), [HL] (whether he survived the jump is unknown), [NPR] (FBI: no definitive identification of the hijacker).
    - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

## Left out (could not verify to 2 sources)

- The FBI case code name "NORJACK" is in [HL] only.

## Best hook (3 lines)

In 1971 a man got $200,000, jumped out of a plane over the Northwest, and was never found.
The FBI closed the case in 2016, and still cannot say who he was.
Hook line: "Nobody knows his name, and the name we all use, D.B. Cooper, is a press myth, per the FBI."
