# Sources: How Electricity Really Works (70-second reel)

Confidence tags: "verified by 2 sources" = two independent pages state the same fact or number. "single source" = one page only.
Wikipedia is used only as a second source where noted. Primary anchors are EIA (a DOE agency), University of Illinois, Georgia State HyperPhysics, and University of Calgary Energy Education.

## Electron drift speed

1. At 1 ampere in a 2 mm copper wire, electrons drift at about 23 micrometers per second (0.023 mm/s).
   - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drift_velocity
   - Cross-check: https://hyperphysics.gsu.edu/hbase/electric/ohmmic.html (gives 0.00028 m/s for 3 A in a 1 mm wire)
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources. Both use the same formula (v = I / nqA); the 23 micrometer figure matches my arithmetic check (about 2.3 x 10^-5 m/s).

2. For ordinary currents, electrons in a copper wire drift only on the order of millimeters per second.
   - Source: https://hyperphysics.gsu.edu/hbase/electric/ohmmic.html
   - Corroboration: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drift_velocity (drift "in the order of 10^-3 meters per second")
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

3. Copper has about 8.5 x 10^28 free electrons per cubic metre, roughly one per atom.
   - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drift_velocity
   - Confidence: single source

4. The random thermal motion of electrons in a wire is about 10^6 metres per second, far faster than their drift.
   - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drift_velocity
   - Confidence: single source

## Signal and energy flow

5. The electrical signal that travels down a wire moves at roughly the speed of light.
   - Source: https://hyperphysics.gsu.edu/hbase/electric/ohmmic.html ("on the order of the speed of light, about 300 million m/s")
   - Confidence: single source (the page is about signal transmission, not the electric field by name)

6. In a circuit, energy flows through the space around the wires, not only inside them.
   - Source 1: https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/20654 ("the energy flow starts in the battery, goes out through the space, and then into the wires")
   - Source 2: https://arxiv.org/abs/1207.2173 ("the Poynting vector is nonzero outside as well as inside the circuit")
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

7. Right next to a current-carrying wire, the energy flow points inward toward the wire.
   - Source: https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/20654
   - Confidence: single source

## Power plants and generators

8. Most power plants burn fuel to heat water into steam, and the steam spins a turbine.
   - Source 1: https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/electricity/how-electricity-is-generated.php ("Most steam turbines have a boiler where fuel is burned to produce hot water and steam")
   - Source 2: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_generator (lists steam turbines as a source of mechanical energy for generators)
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

9. A generator converts the turbine's mechanical energy into electricity; it does not create energy from nothing.
   - Source 1: https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/electricity/how-electricity-is-generated.php
   - Source 2: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_generator ("an electromechanical device that converts mechanical energy to electrical energy")
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

10. Inside a generator, spinning a magnet past coils of wire makes an electric current flow through the wire.
    - Source: https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/electricity/how-electricity-is-generated.php ("moving a magnet inside a coil of wire makes (induces) an electric current flow")
    - Confidence: single source

11. In 2022, steam turbines accounted for about 42% of US electricity generation.
    - Source: https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/electricity/how-electricity-is-generated.php
    - Confidence: single source (a dated statistic; check for a newer year before the reel is published)

## Why power lines use high voltage

12. For a fixed amount of power, raising the voltage lowers the current in proportion.
    - Source 1: https://energyeducation.ca/encyclopedia/Electrical_transmission ("As the voltage gets higher, the current must decrease in proportion because the power is remaining constant")
    - Source 2: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_power_transmission ("For a given amount of power, a higher voltage reduces the current")
    - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

13. Power lost as heat in a wire depends on the square of the current (P = I^2 R).
    - Source 1: https://energyeducation.ca/encyclopedia/Electrical_transmission (gives "Plost = I^2 x R")
    - Source 2: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_power_transmission ("energy losses are proportional to the square of the current")
    - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

14. Step-up transformers raise the voltage for long-distance lines, so lower current means less heat lost in the wires.
    - Source 1: https://energyeducation.ca/encyclopedia/Electrical_transmission ("Usually, this electricity is alternating current so step-up transformers can increase the voltage")
    - Source 2: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_power_transmission ("The reduced current reduces heating losses")
    - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

15. About 6% of US electricity is lost in transmission and distribution.
    - Source: https://energyeducation.ca/encyclopedia/Electrical_transmission (cites EIA estimate of about 6%)
    - Note: Wikipedia gives 6.6% for 1997, so use "about 6%" and cite the EIA figure rather than a single exact number.
    - Confidence: single source

## Best hook (3 lines)

Electrons in your wire crawl along at about 23 micrometers per second, less than a fingertip's width per minute.
Yet a light switch responds almost instantly, because the signal and the energy travel through the space around the wire.
That fact opens the reel: the wire carries the energy in the fields around it, not the electrons themselves.
