# Sources: How a Nuclear Power Plant Works (70-second reel)

Research date: 2026-10-26. Quotes come from fetched pages; re-check exact wording on each page before on-screen use.

## Claims

1. Fission splits a large atom, such as uranium-235, into smaller atoms and releases neutrons and energy.
   - https://world-nuclear.org/nuclear-essentials/how-does-a-nuclear-reactor-work
   - https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/nuclear-101-how-does-nuclear-reactor-work
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

2. Released neutrons can split other atoms, which keeps a chain reaction going.
   - https://world-nuclear.org/nuclear-essentials/how-does-a-nuclear-reactor-work
   - https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/nuclear-101-how-does-nuclear-reactor-work
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

3. Fission heat boils water into steam, and the steam spins a turbine that generates electricity.
   - https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/nuclear-101-how-does-nuclear-reactor-work
   - https://world-nuclear.org/nuclear-essentials/how-does-a-nuclear-reactor-work
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

4. In most reactors, a circulating fluid, usually water, carries the heat away from the core.
   - https://world-nuclear.org/nuclear-essentials/how-does-a-nuclear-reactor-work (water as coolant, "more than 90%" water-based)
   - https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/nuclear-101-how-does-nuclear-reactor-work (water as coolant and moderator)
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

5. A moderator, such as light water, slows the neutrons from fission so the chain reaction continues.
   - https://www.enec.ae/discover/how-nuclear-energy-works/how-do-operators-control-the-nuclear-reaction/ ("The use of a moderator (light water, heavy water or graphite) slows down neutrons.")
   - https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/nuclear-101-how-does-nuclear-reactor-work ("The moderator slows the neutrons released by fission so the chain reaction continues.")
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

6. Control rods contain neutron-absorbing material; lowering them slows or stops the reaction, and raising them speeds it up.
   - https://world-nuclear.org/nuclear-essentials/how-does-a-nuclear-reactor-work (rods "typically made of neutron-absorbing materials such as silver and boron")
   - https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/nuclear-101-how-does-nuclear-reactor-work (inserted to slow, withdrawn to speed up)
   - https://www.enec.ae/discover/how-nuclear-energy-works/how-do-operators-control-the-nuclear-reaction/ (boron absorbs neutrons)
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources (3 listed)

7. Reactor fuel is uranium shaped into small ceramic pellets, sealed in metal rods, and grouped into assemblies.
   - https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/nuclear-101-how-does-nuclear-reactor-work (pellets, sealed metal tubes, "Over 200 rods" per assembly)
   - https://world-nuclear.org/nuclear-essentials/how-does-a-nuclear-reactor-work (assemblies, "thousands of small uranium pellets")
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

8. Reactor fuel is enriched to 3 to 5 percent uranium-235, while natural uranium contains less than 1 percent.
   - https://www.nrc.gov/regulations-legislation/fact-sheets-brochures/backgrounder-on-uranium-enrichment (3 to 5 percent; natural uranium "less than 1 percent")
   - https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/safety-and-security/safety-of-plants/safety-of-nuclear-power-reactors (fuel "not enriched beyond about 5%"; supports the upper bound only)
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources (WNA confirms only the roughly 5 percent ceiling)

9. A commercial power reactor cannot explode like a nuclear bomb, because its fuel is far below bomb-level enrichment.
   - https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/safety-and-security/safety-of-plants/safety-of-nuclear-power-reactors ("simply cannot under any circumstances explode like a nuclear bomb"; "much higher enrichment" needed for explosives)
   - Confidence: single source. NRC and DOE pages on this point were not reachable. Check before publishing.

10. Nuclear power supplied about 9 percent of the world's electricity in 2025.
    - https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/current-and-future-generation/nuclear-power-in-the-world-today.aspx ("about 9%")
    - https://world-nuclear.org/our-association/publications/world-nuclear-outlook-report/global-highlights-for-2025-world-nuclear-outlook-report-2026 ("9% of the world's electricity in 2025")
    - Confidence: single source (World Nuclear Association, two pages). No independent source confirmed the figure.

11. Thirty-one countries generate electricity from nuclear energy.
    - https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/current-and-future-generation/nuclear-power-in-the-world-today.aspx ("31 countries generate electricity from nuclear energy")
    - Confidence: single source. Taiwan's last reactor shut down in May 2025 per the same page.

12. Most spent fuel is kept in water-filled pools that cool it and shield radiation.
    - https://www.cameco.com/uranium_101/spent-fuel-management/spent-fuel/ (large water-filled pools; water shields workers from radiation; pumps carry away decay heat)
    - https://www.iaea.org/newscenter/multimedia/photogallery/spentfuel ("Water shields the radiation and absorbs the heat." Published 2009)
    - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

13. After three to five years in pools, spent fuel is cool enough to move to dry storage casks.
    - https://www.cameco.com/uranium_101/spent-fuel-management/spent-fuel/ ("After 3 to 5 years, the fuel is cool enough to move to dry storage.")
    - Confidence: single source

14. Most spent fuel is stored at or near the reactor where it was used.
    - https://www.iaea.org/newscenter/multimedia/photogallery/spentfuel ("engineered storage pond at or near the reactor site," in most countries)
    - https://www.cameco.com/uranium_101/spent-fuel-management/spent-fuel/ (about 270,000 tonnes in storage, "mostly at reactor sites")
    - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

15. Worldwide, nuclear plants produce roughly 10,000 to 12,000 tonnes of spent fuel per year.
    - https://www.iaea.org/newscenter/multimedia/photogallery/spentfuel ("More than 10,000 tonnes" per year; published 2009)
    - https://www.cameco.com/uranium_101/spent-fuel-management/spent-fuel/ ("about 12,000 tonnes" per year)
    - Confidence: verified by 2 sources, but the figures differ and the IAEA number is from 2009. Use the range with a date, not a single exact figure.

## Notes on excluded or unverified items

- Reactor count: WNA cites "about 440" in one place and 443 operable reactors in its table. Not used as an exact figure.
- U.S. nuclear share, about 18 percent of U.S. utility-scale generation in 2025 (EIA electricity page): single source and not the world figure. Not used.
- One fuel pellet holds energy equal to one tonne of coal, and a reactor uses about 27 tonnes of fuel per year (WNA): single source, not second-verified. Excluded.
- Roughly one-third of spent fuel reprocessed (IAEA 2009): single and dated. Excluded.
- 90 percent of spent fuel in ponds (Cameco): single source. Excluded.
- "94 U.S. reactors" appears in the DOE page, but no second source was confirmed. Excluded.
- Moderator-free design comparisons and reactor-type percentages (DOE: more than 65 percent PWRs): single source. Excluded.
- Unreachable pages: NRC reading room, NRC fuel cycle, DOE nuclear reactor technology, IAEA "nuclear power in the world today" factsheet, WNA "how is nuclear waste managed," WNA "could a nuclear reactor explode."

## Best hook (3 lines)

Every nuclear plant is basically a very fancy kettle.
It splits atoms on purpose, and that heat boils water to spin a turbine.
And it cannot explode like a bomb, because its fuel is only about 5 percent enriched.
