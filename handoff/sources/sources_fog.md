# Sources: fog (How spooky fog really works)

Checked Oct 27, 2026. Only what these pages support is in the script.

## Dry ice fog
- Rutgers University Chemistry, Lecture Demonstration Facility, "Fog Formation" demo: https://chem.rutgers.edu/cldf-demos/1029-cldf-demo-fog-formation
  - Dry ice is solid CO2 at about -78.5 C (-109 F). It sublimes and releases cold CO2 gas.
  - "Fog forms when water vapor in the air condenses into tiny suspended droplets": the warm, humid air over the (hot) water is cooled by the cold gas.
  - Once the cold CO2 has cooled the air enough, it becomes denser than the surrounding air, so the fog sinks, flows over the rim and spreads across the bench and floor.
- Cornell University EHS, "Dry Ice Tip Sheet": https://ehs.cornell.edu/research-safety/chemical-safety/specific-chemical-hazards/cryogenic-material-safety/dry-ice-tip-sheet
  - About -109 F (-78.5 C); turns directly from solid to gas (sublimation).
  - Never place dry ice in a sealed container (pressure can rupture it). CO2 can build up in enclosed or low areas.
  - Never handle with bare hands; use tongs or dry, loose, insulated gloves; thin nitrile/latex gloves are not adequate.
- University of Utah Institutional Biosafety Committee, "Work Safely With Dry Ice": https://ibc.utah.edu/library/working-safely-with-dry-ice.php
  - CO2 gas can displace oxygen; store only in well-ventilated areas. A closed container can pressurize and explode. Never handle with bare hands; use loose, thermally insulated gloves.

## Fog machines
- MIT OpenCourseWare 21M.735 (Technical Design: Scenery, Mechanisms and Special Effects), technical note on fog: https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/21m-735-technical-design-scenery-mechanisms-and-special-effects-spring-2004/5b8cc420e2d0d167b6643f64f723c3d8_drkp_tn10.pdf
  - Glycol/glycerol foggers heat and vaporize a water-based fluid; the output forms an opaque aerosol when it mixes with the surrounding air. The typical effect is a rising, smoke-like cloud; chiller units can chill the output to make low-lying fog.
- Alliance Chemical blog, "Brewing a spooky atmosphere: propylene glycol and glycerin in fog generation" (commercial source, used only for the step order): https://alliancechemical.com/blogs/blog/brewing-a-spooky-atmosphere-the-role-of-propylene-glycol-and-glycerin-in-fog-generation
  - A pump pushes fluid (water with propylene glycol and/or glycerin) into an electrically heated block; it vaporizes and leaves the nozzle; in cooler air the vapor condenses into fine droplets, which are the visible fog.
