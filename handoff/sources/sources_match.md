# Sources: match (cel-boil style)
- Fire triangle = heat, fuel, oxygen, needed for a fire to start: AIChE CCPS glossary (aiche.org/ccps/resources/glossary/process-safety-glossary/fire-triangle). The page does not say what happens if one is removed, so the reel doesn't claim it.
- Head: potassium chlorate (oxidizer, releases oxygen when heated) + antimony(III) sulfide / sulfur (fuel); striking strip: red phosphorus + powdered glass; friction turns a trace of red into white phosphorus, which ignites in air, which decomposes the chlorate, releasing oxygen: University of Washington Chemistry "Match Head Reaction"; RSC (edu.rsc.org/download?ac=11207); Compound Interest "matches" (compoundchem.com/2014/11/20/matches/); Britannica match article (print/article/368955).
- Wood stick soaked in ammonium phosphate (flame retardant, so it burns slowly): RSC. Compound Interest says it prevents afterglow.
- John Walker's first friction matches sold 1827 (ledger dated April 7, 1827): Britannica. Compound Interest says "1826" for development; the reel uses the sale date, 1827.
- White phosphorus added in the early 1830s (Sauria) and caused "phossy jaw" in workers; later banned nearly everywhere: Britannica (match tinder / match), Compound Interest.
- Red phosphorus discovered by Anton von Schrotter, 1845; nontoxic, doesn't ignite spontaneously, made the safety match possible; Lundstrom patented the safety match 1855: Britannica.

## Caveats / own wording
- "A fire needs all three to start": AIChE states the three elements are needed for a fire to start.
- "Half of the chemistry is on the box": own summary of Britannica's description that safety-match ingredients are split between head and striking surface; not a measured fraction.
- Strike-anywhere matches (phosphorus sesquisulfide in the head) are not covered; the reel is about safety matches.
- Head ingredient lists vary by maker (Britannica: "typically"); sulfur + antimony sulfide are shown as fuel, as in RSC / Compound Interest.
- Scene art (faces on matches and flame, bubbles) is illustration, not to scale.
