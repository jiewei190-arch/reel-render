# Sources: How Airplanes Really Fly (70-second reel)

Page numbers for FAA PHAK are from section markers in the PDF text and are approximate where a section spans a page break.

## Claims

1. Lift happens when a solid object turns a moving flow of air.
   - Sources: https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/what-is-lift/ ; https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/06_phak_ch4_0.pdf (flow turning, p. 4-9)
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

2. A wing's shape deflects air downward as the wing moves through it.
   - Sources: https://www.grc.nasa.gov/WWW/K-12/Summer_Training/Elementary97/planearticle.html ; https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/06_phak_ch4_0.pdf (p. 4-7)
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

3. Newton's third law: the air pushed downward pushes the wing upward.
   - Sources: https://www.grc.nasa.gov/WWW/K-12/Summer_Training/Elementary97/planearticle.html ("In response to the force of moving the air down, the air pushes the airplane upward.") ; https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/06_phak_ch4_0.pdf (p. 4-7, "forces air downward, which provides an equal reaction from the air")
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

4. Faster-moving air has lower pressure, so the pressure on a lifting wing's top is lower than on its bottom.
   - Sources: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/06_phak_ch4_0.pdf (Bernoulli, p. 4-6) ; https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/07_phak_ch5_0.pdf (p. 5-7, "the pressure on the lower surface of it is greater than that on the upper surface") ; https://www.grc.nasa.gov/WWW/k-12/airplane/right1.html (pressure related to velocity through Bernoulli's equation)
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources (FAA chapters 4 and 5 are one publication; NASA Glenn adds the Bernoulli relation)

5. The equal transit time theory says air over the top must reach the back edge of the wing at the same time as air underneath.
   - Sources: https://www.grc.nasa.gov/WWW/k-12/VirtualAero/BottleRocket/airplane/wrong1.html ("molecules going over the top of the wing must travel faster than the molecules moving under the wing") ; https://aip.brightspotcdn.com/PTO.v61.i9.8_3.online.pdf (Physics Today, 8 Sep 2008, Evan Jones letter: "the dictum of equal time")
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

6. The equal transit time idea is false: air over a lifting wing's top reaches the back edge sooner than air underneath.
   - Sources: https://www.grc.nasa.gov/WWW/k-12/VirtualAero/BottleRocket/airplane/wrong1.html ("particles moving over the top arrive at the trailing edge before particles moving under the airfoil") ; https://aip.brightspotcdn.com/PTO.v61.i9.8_3.online.pdf (Anderson reply: "The flow over the 'top' is faster and arrives at the end of the foil sooner")
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

7. Many lifting wings, including symmetrical ones, do not have a longer top surface.
   - Sources: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/06_phak_ch4_0.pdf (p. 4-8, "many lifting airfoils do not have an upper surface longer than the bottom, as in the case of symmetrical airfoils") ; https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/foilinc (symmetric airfoil has zero lift at zero angle of attack, so lift comes from the angle, not shape length)
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

8. Lift acts perpendicular to the direction of the airflow.
   - Sources: https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/what-is-lift/ ("directed perpendicular to the flow direction") ; https://www.grc.nasa.gov/www/k-12/airplane/right1.html ("The component of the net force perpendicular to the flow direction is the lift.")
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

9. Drag is the force along the airflow direction that resists the aircraft's motion.
   - Sources: https://www.grc.nasa.gov/www/k-12/airplane/right1.html ("The component of the net force along the flow direction is the drag.") ; https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/07_phak_ch5_0.pdf (p. 5-1/5-2, "a rearward, retarding force caused by disruption of airflow")
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

10. Thrust is the forward force produced by the engine or propeller.
    - Sources: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/07_phak_ch5_0.pdf (p. 5-1/5-2, "the forward force produced by the powerplant/propeller or rotor") ; https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/06_phak_ch4_0.pdf (p. 4-6, propeller pushes the airplane forward)
    - Confidence: verified by 2 sources (same FAA publication, two chapters)

11. Weight is the combined load of the aircraft, crew, fuel and cargo, pulled down by gravity.
    - Sources: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/07_phak_ch5_0.pdf (p. 5-1/5-2, weight definition) ; https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/06_phak_ch4_0.pdf (p. 4-5, "the force of gravity")
    - Confidence: verified by 2 sources (same FAA publication, two chapters)

12. An aircraft can fly when its wing's lift is greater than the aircraft's weight.
    - Sources: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/06_phak_ch4_0.pdf (p. 4-5, "Lift from the wing that is greater than the force of gravity ... enables an aircraft to fly") ; same file (p. 4-7, "If a wing ... causes a lift force greater than the weight of the aircraft, the aircraft will fly")
    - Confidence: verified by 2 passages (same FAA publication)

13. Angle of attack is the angle between the wing's chord line and the oncoming airflow.
    - Sources: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/07_phak_ch5_0.pdf (p. 5-2, "The AOA is defined as the acute angle between the chord line of the airfoil and the direction of the relative wind.") ; https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/foilinc ("The angle between the chord line and the flight direction is called the angle of attack")
    - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

14. At small angles, lift rises roughly in direct proportion to angle of attack.
    - Sources: https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/foilinc ("the lift is directly proportional to the angle of attack for small angles")
    - Confidence: single source

15. Past a critical angle, airflow separates from the wing and lift drops suddenly, which is a stall.
    - Sources: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/07_phak_ch5_0.pdf (p. 5-26, "A rapid decrease in lift caused by the separation of airflow from the wing's surface") ; https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/foilinc ("aircraft wings will abruptly lose lift at high angles to the flow")
    - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

## Contested or excluded

- Physics Today (2008) letter by Evan Jones argues that Bernoulli's principle is not the cause of the pressure difference and that deflection is the right cause. Anderson replies that cause versus consequence is "a matter of semantics." Claims 4 and 8 avoid taking a side on cause order. Keep the reel's wording at the level of "pressure is lower on top" and "the wing pushes air down."
- FAA PHAK ch. 4 (p. 4-8) states that the pressure difference alone does not account for lift and that Bernoulli and Newton's laws both operate. Not used as a numbered claim.
- Excluded for being single-source and not needed: NASA's statement that equal-transit predicts far too little lift (wrong1), its statement that equal-transit fails for upside-down flight (wrong1), and NASA's "faster plane generates more lift" (planearticle).

## Best hook (3 lines)

Everyone learned that planes fly because air races over a longer top surface.
That's wrong: the air over the top actually reaches the back edge sooner than the air underneath.
A plane flies because its wing shoves air down, and the air shoves the plane up.
