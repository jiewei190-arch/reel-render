# Sources: How a bicycle really stays upright (risograph style)
| Claim | Source |
|---|---|
| A moving bike that leans makes its front wheel turn automatically; "bicycle leaning automatically causes steering" (Ruina), which can bring the wheels back under a falling bike | Cornell/ScienceDaily: https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/04/110414141356.htm ; BikeRadar: https://www.bikeradar.com/features/your-bikes-secret-to-staying-upright-is-actually-a-mystery |
| If the bike is knocked to one side it straightens itself back upright | Cornell/ScienceDaily (above) |
| Old explanation 1: the gyroscopic effect (a spinning wheel resists tilting) | BikeRadar (above) |
| Old explanation 2: trail/caster, the offset between the steering axis and the wheel's ground contact point, like a shopping-cart caster | BikeRadar (above) |
| 2011 test bike: riderless, two small wheels each paired with a counter-rotating disk (cancels gyro effect), front contact point slightly ahead of the steering axis (negative caster); launched above ~5 mph it still balanced itself | Cornell/ScienceDaily (above) |
| Gyroscopic effects and trail help but are not essential | TU Delft via IamExpat: https://www.iamexpat.nl/expat-info/dutch-news/tu-delft-why-does-moving-bicycle-not-fall-over ; Cornell (above) |
| Self-stability happens above a certain forward speed | TU Delft via IamExpat (above) |
| Mass distribution matters, especially the center of mass of the front/steering assembly | Cornell (above); TU Delft via IamExpat (above) |
| Almost any self-stable bike can be made unstable by misadjusting trail, front-wheel gyro or front-assembly center of mass | Cornell (above) |
| NOT CLAIMED (unverified): exact speed range for ordinary bikes, rider effects, 1910 history, any detail of "why" beyond the sources |
