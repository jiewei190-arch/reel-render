# Sources: How the Sun Works (70-second explainer reel)

Research date: 2026-10-26. Each claim is one plain sentence (under 25 words) with its source URL and confidence tag.

## Numbered claims

1. The Sun's core reaches about 15 million degrees Celsius, hot enough for nuclear fusion.
   - Source: https://pwg.gsfc.nasa.gov/istp/outreach/workshop/thompson/facts.html (NASA, table lists 15,000,000 K)
   - Source: https://www.spaceweather.gov/sites/default/files/images/u2/Chapter_2_mod2-FINAL.pdf (NOAA SWPC, p. 7 and Table 2-1: "about 15,000,000 K"; original link swpc.noaa.gov redirects here)
   - Also: https://science.nasa.gov/sun/facts/ (NASA: core "top 27 million F (15 million C)")
   - Range: NASA's NSSDC model gives a central temperature of 1.571 x 10^7 K (about 15.7 million K), so use "about 15 million".
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

2. Fusion in the core joins hydrogen nuclei into helium, and that is what powers the Sun.
   - Source: https://science.nasa.gov/sun/facts/ (NASA: hydrogen fuses into helium in the core)
   - Source: https://www.spaceweather.gov/sites/default/files/images/u2/Chapter_2_mod2-FINAL.pdf (NOAA, p. 8: hydrogen fused into helium)
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

3. Every second, the Sun converts about 4.3 million metric tons of its mass into energy.
   - Source: https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/sunfact.html (NASA NSSDC fact sheet: mass conversion rate 4260 x 10^6 kg/s, i.e. 4.26 billion kg/s = 4.26 million metric tons/s)
   - Source: https://web.pa.msu.edu/courses/2009spring/ISP205/sec-2/03-17.pdf (MSU lecture: "The sun loses 4 million tons of mass every second")
   - Range: NOAA (https://www.spaceweather.gov/sites/default/files/images/u2/Chapter_2_mod2-FINAL.pdf, p. 8) states 5 million tons per second. Quote the NASA figure and say "about 4 million".
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources (range 4 to 5 million tons/s across sources)

4. Each second, about 700 million tons of hydrogen fuse into about 695 million tons of helium.
   - Source: https://www.spaceweather.gov/sites/default/files/images/u2/Chapter_2_mod2-FINAL.pdf (NOAA SWPC, p. 8)
   - Note: the UNT page (https://solar.unt.edu/TheSun.html) says "700 billion tons" per second, which conflicts with NOAA's 700 million. Excluded as an error.
   - Confidence: single source

5. The fusion reaction turns about 0.7 percent of the fused mass into energy.
   - Source: https://www.spaceweather.gov/sites/default/files/images/u2/Chapter_2_mod2-FINAL.pdf (NOAA, p. 8: "0.7 percent of the mass disappears")
   - Source: https://www.as.utexas.edu/astronomy/education/spring06/komatsu/secure/lecture05.pdf (UT Austin lecture: four-proton mass difference is 0.7% of the original mass)
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

6. The Sun radiates energy at about 3.8 x 10^26 watts.
   - Source: https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/sunfact.html (NASA NSSDC: luminosity 3.828 x 10^26 W)
   - Source: https://www.as.utexas.edu/astronomy/education/spring06/komatsu/secure/lecture05.pdf (UT Austin: "3.8 x 10^26 Watts")
   - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

7. Gravity pulls the Sun inward, and pressure from the hot core pushes outward to balance it.
   - Source: https://www.as.utexas.edu/astronomy/education/spring06/komatsu/secure/lecture05.pdf (UT Austin: "Pressure force balances gravitational force"; "High temperature is necessary to balance enormous gravity")
   - Note: the CfA page (https://lweb.cfa.harvard.edu/~ejchaisson/cosmic_evolution/docs/text/text_stel_4.html) names the term "hydrostatic equilibrium" but does not explain it. Neither source explicitly says "does not explode".
   - Confidence: single source

8. Energy from the core escapes slowly because photons are absorbed and re-emitted again and again, a random walk.
   - Source: https://www.spaceweather.gov/sites/default/files/images/u2/Chapter_2_mod2-FINAL.pdf (NOAA, p. 8: "Photons do a random walk")
   - Source: https://lweb.cfa.harvard.edu/~ejchaisson/cosmic_evolution/docs/text/text_stel_4.html (CfA: radiation "scatters around (or random walks) in the solar interior")
   - Source: https://www.as.utexas.edu/astronomy/education/spring06/komatsu/secure/lecture05.pdf (UT Austin: photons scattered by electrons, random walk)
   - Confidence: verified by 3 sources

9. The energy's trip from the core to the surface is estimated at about 100,000 to 1 million years.
   - Source: https://pwg.gsfc.nasa.gov/istp/outreach/workshop/thompson/facts.html (NASA: "takes a million years to reach the surface")
   - Source: https://lweb.cfa.harvard.edu/~ejchaisson/cosmic_evolution/docs/text/text_stel_4.html (CfA: "about a million years")
   - Source: https://www.as.utexas.edu/astronomy/education/spring06/komatsu/secure/lecture05.pdf (UT Austin: "about a million years from the core to the surface")
   - Source: https://solar.unt.edu/TheSun.html (UNT: "around 170,000 years" to the top of the convection zone; a different endpoint)
   - Source: https://www.universetoday.com/articles/what-would-happen-if-the-sun-stopped-part-3-the-photon-traffic-jam (Universe Today: "around 100,000 years", no cited source)
   - Note: the user's 100,000-year figure has only an uncited popular source. The best-supported figure is about 1 million years. The 50-million-year figure in NOAA's text refers to the radiative layer only and is an outlier, so it is excluded.
   - Confidence: verified by 3+ sources (range is contested; state the range, not one number)

10. Neutrinos made in the core reach the surface in about 2 seconds, far faster than light.
    - Source: https://lweb.cfa.harvard.edu/~ejchaisson/cosmic_evolution/docs/text/text_stel_4.html (CfA: "reach the surface in about 2 seconds")
    - Confidence: single source

11. Sunlight takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to reach Earth.
    - Source: https://howthingsfly.si.edu/ask-an-explainer/how-long-does-it-take-light-sun-get-earth (Smithsonian: "about 8 minutes and 20 seconds", 150 million km)
    - Source: https://www.universetoday.com/articles/how-long-does-it-take-sunlight-to-reach-the-earth (Universe Today: 8 min 20 s, citing NASA)
    - Range: the distance varies from about 147 to 152 million km over Earth's orbit, so the time varies a few seconds.
    - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

12. The Sun has about 10 billion years of hydrogen burning in total as a main-sequence star.
    - Source: https://lweb.cfa.harvard.edu/~ejchaisson/cosmic_evolution/docs/text/text_stel_4.html (CfA: "~10 billion years")
    - Source: https://www.as.utexas.edu/astronomy/education/spring06/komatsu/secure/lecture05.pdf (UT Austin: "about 10 billion years")
    - Source: https://www.spaceweather.gov/sites/default/files/images/u2/Chapter_2_mod2-FINAL.pdf (NOAA: "Lifetime is 10Byr")
    - Confidence: verified by 3 sources

13. The Sun will keep producing energy at its current rate for about 5 billion more years.
    - Source: https://science.nasa.gov/sun/facts/ (NASA: "another 5 billion years or so")
    - Source: https://www.spaceweather.gov/sites/default/files/images/u2/Chapter_2_mod2-FINAL.pdf (NOAA, p. 7: "another 5 billion years")
    - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

14. The Sun is roughly 4.5 to 5 billion years old.
    - Source: https://www.spaceweather.gov/sites/default/files/images/u2/Chapter_2_mod2-FINAL.pdf (NOAA, Table 2-1: "at least 4.5 billion years")
    - Source: https://www.as.utexas.edu/astronomy/education/spring06/komatsu/secure/lecture05.pdf (UT Austin: "about 5 billion years old")
    - Confidence: verified by 2 sources

## Excluded or unverified

- Universe Today's 100,000-year figure has no cited source. It is used only inside claim 9 as a range end.
- UNT's "700 billion tons" per second is contradicted by NOAA's 700 million. Excluded.
- NOAA's "as long as 50 million years" for the radiative layer is inconsistent with other sources. Excluded.
- The Universe Today article ("around 100,000 years") is a popular article; no primary study was found for the exact 100,000 value.
- Mass-loss: NOAA gives 5 million tons/s; NASA NSSDC gives 4.26 million metric tons/s. Use "about 4 million".

## Best hook (3 lines)

1. The sunlight on your face was made in the Sun's core, where it is about 15 million degrees.
2. It spent somewhere between 100,000 and a million years escaping, then only 8 minutes 20 seconds crossing to you.
3. Say "a million years" or "a range" on screen, because the 100,000 figure is contested.
