# Sources: How a tsunami really works (ukiyo-e style)
Only claims listed here are in the script. Retrieved Oct 26 2026.

| Claim used | Source |
|---|---|
| A tsunami is a series of waves caused by a large, sudden displacement of the ocean; most are triggered by large earthquakes below or near the seafloor | NOAA, US Tsunami Warning System explainer: https://www.noaa.gov/explainers/us-tsunami-warning-system |
| Needs a quake strong enough to suddenly displace a large volume of water | National Geographic, Tsunami facts: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/tsunami-facts-warning-signs-safety-tips |
| In deep water tsunamis move over 500 mph (800 km/h), as fast as a jet plane | NOAA JetStream, tsunami propagation: https://noaa.gov/jetstream/tsunamis/tsunami-propagation |
| Deeper water, faster; speed depends on water depth (gravity and depth) | NOAA JetStream (above) |
| Wavelengths are hundreds of miles | NOAA JetStream (above) |
| In the deep ocean rarely more than ~3 ft (1 m) high; ships normally don't notice one passing | NOAA JetStream (above); NOAA explainer (above) |
| Approaching shore: slow down, wavelengths decrease, waves grow in height, currents intensify; near shore ~20-30 mph (30-50 km/h) | NOAA JetStream (above) |
| Before the surge the sea may retreat suddenly, showing the ocean floor like a very low, low tide | NOAA explainer (above); NatGeo (rapid drop in sea level) |
| Local tsunamis can arrive just minutes after generation; waves may keep flooding and receding for hours | NOAA explainer (above) |
| A tsunami is a wave train; the first wave isn't necessarily the most dangerous; surges may come minutes to an hour apart | National Geographic (above) |
| After a strong earthquake near the coast: go to high ground immediately, don't wait for an official warning | National Geographic (above, 2007 article) |
| DART buoys give early detection, measurement and real-time reporting of tsunamis in the open ocean | NOAA explainer (above) |

NOT CLAIMED (no source found): "the whole water column rises", exact wave period, any specific historical event or death figure.
