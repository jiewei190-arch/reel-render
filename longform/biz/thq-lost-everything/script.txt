In January twenty thirteen, a video game company that had once been worth more than two billion dollars was put up for auction, one piece at a time.
The bidding ran for twenty-two hours. Ten bidders took part.
One studio sold for twenty-six point six million dollars. One famous game sold for about half a million.
And one studio, the team behind a series called Darksiders, got no bids at all.
That day one of its lead designers posted a goodbye online. He wrote, "The walls look bare. It's quiet. The seats are empty."
When it was over, the whole auction had raised about seventy-two million dollars. The shareholders got nothing.
And the strangest part is what pushed it over the edge. Not a rival. Not a scandal. A plastic drawing tablet for children.
This is how THQ went from two billion dollars to an auction block, and the one yes that cost it everything.
The name gives away where it started. THQ was short for Toy Headquarters.
It was founded in April nineteen ninety by a toy executive named Jack Friedman, with a reported one million dollars, in Calabasas, California.
It sold dolls and board games, but mostly it sold cartridges. By the end of nineteen ninety-one, Nintendo games were about eighty percent of its sales.
That summer, the company hired a new chief financial officer. His name was Brian Farrell. He was an accountant who had spent seven years running the finances of a hotel company.
By January nineteen ninety-five, the accountant was the chief executive. He would hold that job for the next eighteen years, to the very last day.
And Farrell's THQ ran on one simple idea. Don't invent the characters. Rent them.
In nineteen ninety-eight, THQ and a partner signed a ten-year deal to make wrestling games for the World Wrestling Federation.
In two thousand, it locked up the video game rights to a new cartoon about a sponge who lives in a pineapple.
Then came the biggest name in animation. Pixar. Under that deal, THQ could make games for the studio's next four films.
Here's why that worked. A parent in a store does not read game reviews. A parent sees a character their kid already loves, and buys the box.
The game based on the film Cars shipped nearly eight million copies. A single wrestling game shipped four million in one year.
So in the year that ended in March two thousand seven, THQ crossed a line most publishers never reach. One billion dollars in sales. Sixty-eight million dollars in profit.
It had sixteen development studios and about two thousand employees.
That spring, its shares traded above thirty-six dollars. Multiply that by the share count in its own annual report, and the company was worth about two point four billion dollars.
But the same annual report held one sentence that should have worried everybody.
Three partners, Pixar, Nickelodeon and wrestling, made up fifty-four percent of everything THQ sold.
More than half the company was built on characters it did not own, and on customers who were about to grow up.
The first crack showed up within a year. THQ went from a sixty-eight million dollar profit to a thirty-five million dollar loss.
Then the floor gave way. In the year ending March two thousand nine, THQ lost four hundred and thirty-one million dollars. More than six times the profit of its best year.
So what happened? The company explained it in its own filing.
Kids' tastes, it wrote, had shifted away from games based on movies and television shows, toward original games made by Nintendo itself.
And the old console those kids' games sold on was fading. Sales on that one machine fell by a hundred and sixty million dollars in a single year.
Farrell would later put it in one line. The see the movie, play the game experience, he told investors, did not seem to be working anymore.
In November two thousand eight, THQ closed five studios in a single announcement and cut about two hundred and fifty developers.
By the next spring, the total was about six hundred jobs. Nearly a quarter of the company.
The stock had fallen eighty-six percent in a year. In March two thousand nine, the Los Angeles Times put THQ's entire market value at one hundred and seventy-three million dollars.
From two point four billion to a hundred and seventy-three million, in under two years.
And one analyst said out loud what people were thinking. He told a Los Angeles business paper that the odds of THQ going bankrupt were fifty-fifty.
Farrell had an answer for that. When the stock price is depressed, he said, the naysayers can have their day in the sunshine.
He wasn't bluffing. He had a plan. Fewer games. Better games. Games THQ would own.
All he needed was someone who knew how to make a blockbuster.
The man he picked did not come from the toy aisle. Danny Bilson was a screenwriter who had moved into games.
He joined THQ in February two thousand eight, and within about sixteen months he was running everything the company made for grown-up players.
He was not shy about what he wanted to change. I'm bringing a totally new culture for this company, he told a reporter. We're not making manifolds. We're making products to entertain people.
He compared it to Hollywood in the seventies. The artist and the content come first, he said, before the marketing.
And to be fair, the logic was sound. The games earning real money by then were big, original shooters and open worlds. THQ had to own one or stay a renter forever.
The flagship of the new THQ was a shooter called Homefront, set in an America under occupation.
It was reported to be the most expensive game THQ had ever made. Thirty-five to fifty million dollars to produce, with tens of millions more to advertise.
A company executive said it had to sell two million copies just to break even.
At a games conference in San Francisco, the marketing team released ten thousand red balloons into the sky. The stunt drew complaints in the city, and the marketing firm was later fined seven thousand dollars.
Homefront came out on March fifteenth, twenty eleven. That morning, the review scores arrived. The average was seventy-two out of a hundred.
For a game meant to stand beside the biggest shooters in the world, seventy-two was not enough.
THQ's stock fell twenty-one percent that day. At one point it was down by about a quarter.
Here's the twist. Homefront actually sold. THQ said it shipped two point six million copies within weeks.
But it wasn't the five-million seller the plan required, and Farrell had admitted that same month that THQ had never had one.
In June, the New York studio that built Homefront was closed.
So the kids' business was dying, and the blockbuster business had just stumbled.
Except there was one thing inside THQ that was working. And it came from the side of the company everyone had written off.
It was called the uDraw GameTablet. A white plastic slate with a stylus on a cord, that plugged into Nintendo's Wii.
Kids drew on the tablet, and the picture appeared on the television. THQ announced it in August twenty ten at sixty-nine dollars and ninety-nine cents, with an art game in the box.
The executive in charge of it ran THQ's kids and family division. His name was Martin Good, and he said the potential was limitless.
For one Christmas, it looked like he was right.
THQ had hoped to move a million tablets in the first year. It shipped one point two million in the first holiday quarter alone.
By the end of March, that number was one point seven million. Farrell called it well in excess of the target.
Think about where the company was. Losses. Layoffs. A blockbuster that had just cost it a fifth of its value in a day. And here was a surprise hit, from the division it was trying to escape.
So on May third, twenty eleven, in the same announcement as a hundred and thirty-six million dollar annual loss, THQ said yes to more.
It would build brand new uDraw tablets for the other two consoles. The Xbox three sixty and the PlayStation three.
Good's reasoning was on the record. Those consoles were expected to reach a critical mass of family owners within two years. It was the same idea, on twice as many machines.
But there was a problem hiding in that logic. The Wii was the console many families had bought for young children. The other two were better known for shooters and sports games.
And there was a second problem, one that makes a toy different from a game. A game is a disc. A tablet is a factory order.
You have to guess how many to build months ahead, pay for them, ship them, and hope.
THQ guessed big. On November second, Farrell told investors the coming holiday quarter would be the largest in the company's history, in sales and in earnings. He named three reasons. One of them was uDraw.
Five weeks later, he took it back. On December seventh, THQ cut its sales forecast for the quarter by about a quarter. Sales of the new tablets, he said, were weaker than expected.
Two days after that, a filing disclosed that Martin Good's employment had been terminated. It gave no reason.
The full count came on February second, twenty twelve.
One point four million tablets were still sitting unsold.
Revenue came in about a hundred million dollars short. The tablet alone wiped more than thirty million dollars off the quarter's operating results.
THQ lost fifty-five point nine million dollars in what was supposed to be the best quarter it ever had. It said it had no plans to manufacture the tablet again.
On the call, Farrell did not hide. Unfortunately, he said, we were wrong.
He said the tablet was meant to be a bridge to the company's future. Instead, in his words, it turned out to be a plank that we walked off of.
And here is the part that stings. In that very same quarter, THQ finally got the hit it had been chasing for three years.
A game it owned outright, Saints Row The Third, shipped three point eight million copies. It was the biggest launch of an original series in the company's history.
The strategy had worked. Farrell said so himself. Without the tablet, he told investors, the company would have doubled its profitability in the quarter.
The blockbuster arrived on time. The toy ate it.
What came next happened fast.
Days before that call, THQ had announced it was leaving the kids' licensed business that had built it, and cutting up to two hundred and forty more jobs.
Farrell cut his own salary in half for a year, from about seven hundred and eighteen thousand dollars to about three hundred and fifty-nine thousand.
The stock exchange sent a warning. THQ's shares had spent too long below one dollar, and it had until July to fix that or lose its listing.
By April, a share of THQ closed at forty-five cents. Five years earlier, it had been thirty-six dollars.
In May, Farrell brought in help. Jason Rubin, who had co-founded one of the most admired studios in games, became president. Danny Bilson was out.
Then the selling began. THQ handed its mixed martial arts license to a much larger rival, Electronic Arts, for an undisclosed cash payment.
It cut loose a horror project with a famous film director. It dropped an action game from a celebrated Japanese designer. A giant online game set in a famous science-fiction universe had already been scaled back, with more than a hundred layoffs.
In July, to stay listed, the company swapped every ten shares for one.
One game still had to carry the year. Darksiders Two, from the studio in Austin. THQ shipped one point four million copies.
Rubin told investors plainly that it had not performed to expectations.
By the end of September, THQ had thirty-six million dollars in cash. In November it delayed its three biggest upcoming games, and stopped giving forecasts at all.
A week later, it disclosed that it had defaulted on its fifty million dollar credit line. The stock fell by about half in a week.
And then THQ did something no big publisher had done before. It put its own games in a pay-what-you-want charity bundle on the internet.
Nearly nine hundred thousand bundles sold, for about five million dollars. The company's own president chipped in ten thousand dollars himself.
It was a remarkable show of affection from players. It was also nowhere near enough.
The lender agreed to wait, but only until January fifteenth. The chief financial officer resigned.
THQ had weeks. And it had found exactly one buyer.
On December nineteenth, twenty twelve, THQ filed for bankruptcy protection in Delaware.
It listed about two hundred and five million dollars in assets and about two hundred and forty-eight million in debts.
But it arrived with a deal already signed. A private investment firm called Clearlake would buy essentially the whole company, for about sixty million dollars.
Every studio, every game, kept together. Farrell called it a necessary step to complete the company's transformation. Rubin told fans it was a new start.
There was one condition. THQ asked the court to finish the entire sale in about thirty days.
The people THQ owed money to read that plan very differently.
A group of lenders told the court the process was designed to keep competing bidders away. They also alleged it was built so that current management would keep their jobs. That was their claim, and it was never proven.
The government's bankruptcy watchdog objected too, to the speed and to the fees the buyer would collect.
On January fourth, Judge Mary Walrath heard them out. And she said no.
I am not convinced, she said, that we are under the gun to have a sale process by the fifteenth.
She pointed out that THQ had never even told the public it was for sale. And she added one more thing. Individual titles, she said, may have substantial value.
So the rules changed. The sixty million dollar offer became an opening bid. Anyone could now bid for the whole company, or for any piece of it.
Which brings us back to that room.
The auction began on the afternoon of January twenty-second and ran into the next day. Twenty-two hours.
The strategy studio in Canada went to Sega, for twenty-six point six million dollars.
The studio behind Saints Row went to a European publisher, Koch Media, for twenty-two point three million. The same buyer took a shooter called Metro for five point nine million.
A game still in development, called Evolve, drew a bid from the very studio that was making it. They offered two hundred and fifty thousand dollars for their own work. A larger publisher took it for ten point nine million.
The Montreal studio and a South Park game went to a French publisher. And Homefront, the most expensive game THQ had ever made, sold for about half a million.
Added together, the pieces came to about seventy-two million dollars. About twelve million more than the offer for the whole.
That was the math that ended THQ. It was worth more in parts.
And one part had no price at all. Nobody bid on the Darksiders studio. Rubin said its next game was simply too far from finished.
In a letter to staff that day, Farrell and Rubin wrote that if you worked for a part of the company that was not sold, we regret that your position will end.
A week later, both men were formally let go themselves.
The leftovers were sold that spring. Darksiders, the series nobody had bid on, went in a bundle with several others to a small publisher called Nordic Games, for four point nine million dollars.
The wrestling license, the oldest pillar of the company, moved to another publisher as part of a settlement of claims.
In July twenty thirteen, the court approved THQ's liquidation plan. Every share of THQ stock was cancelled.
Lenders and suppliers without collateral were expected to recover somewhere between twenty and fifty-two cents on the dollar, according to press reports at the time.
A shareholder lawsuit over the tablet forecasts was dismissed, revived on appeal, and reportedly settled in twenty seventeen for two point six million dollars.
And the games? Many of them did fine without THQ.
Saints Row Four reportedly sold more than a million copies in its first week under its new owner. The South Park game came out in twenty fourteen. Company of Heroes Two and Metro both shipped within months.
Even the name survived. The publisher that bought Darksiders later bought the THQ trademark, and in twenty sixteen it renamed itself after the company it had picked through.
Jason Rubin said afterward that THQ had every chance to survive, had it not made massive mistakes. He named two. Staying in children's games long after phones and tablets had taken that business. And the incredible losses attached to uDraw.
But look at how that last decision was actually made.
Nobody at THQ did anything reckless. They had one product that was beating its forecast, in a year when nothing else was. So they made more of it, for more customers.
The mistake was not the tablet. The first one worked. The mistake was reading one good Christmas, on one console, as proof about every family, everywhere.
THQ spent three years learning to build the hit that could save it. Then it bet the same quarter on a toy.
A surprise success tells you something worked once. It does not tell you why.
