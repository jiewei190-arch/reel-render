On November nineteenth, twenty twenty-six, a video game went on sale. By the end of that first day, according to the company that publishes it, people had bought three point seven nine billion dollars' worth.
That is more, in one day, than the highest-grossing films ever made took in during their entire runs in cinemas.
The same series held the old record. Eight hundred million dollars. This was nearly five times that.
The game is Grand Theft Auto Six. And almost every number around it is public. The price. The pre-orders. The launch.
Except one. Nobody outside the company knows what it cost to make.
You have probably heard a figure. Two billion dollars. It is in headlines and thumbnails everywhere.
The earliest trace of that number is not a filing or an executive. It is a claim attributed to an anonymous hacker.
So this is the real math of the biggest bet in entertainment, using only the numbers somebody was willing to sign.
To see why this game mattered so much, go back to September seventeenth, twenty thirteen. The day Grand Theft Auto Five came out.
Its publisher, a New York company called Take-Two, said the game sold more than eight hundred million dollars' worth on day one. And more than one billion dollars in three days.
The company said it believed that was the fastest any entertainment property had ever reached a billion.
And what did that game cost? Here's the first surprise. Nobody official ever said.
The number you see quoted, about two hundred and sixty-five million dollars, came from a newspaper report in Scotland. The studio never confirmed it.
Earlier that year, one analyst had estimated development alone at about a hundred and thirty-seven million. His method was simple. Two hundred and fifty people, for five years.
Hold on to that method. It comes back.
Then Grand Theft Auto Five did something no one planned for. It never stopped selling.
It was re-released on the next generation of consoles, then the one after that. Its online mode kept players spending for years.
As of this month, the company says it has shipped nearly two hundred and thirty-five million copies.
In the year ending March twenty sixteen, Grand Theft Auto products were fifty-four point five percent of Take-Two's revenue.
So picture the position. More than half your company is one game. And its sequel has no release date.
The studio behind it, Rockstar, did not go quiet. In October twenty eighteen it released a western, Red Dead Redemption Two, which did seven hundred and twenty-five million dollars in its first three days.
But the next Grand Theft Auto? Years of silence.
The first official word came on February fourth, twenty twenty-two. One sentence. Active development for the next entry in the series, Rockstar wrote, is well underway.
So when did it actually start? Even that depends on who you ask.
Bloomberg has reported that the project existed in some form as far back as twenty fourteen.
Take-Two's chief executive, Strauss Zelnick, has put it differently. He told investors that development began in earnest in twenty twenty.
Both can be true. An idea in twenty fourteen. A full production from twenty twenty.
That matters for the math. Thirteen years passed between the two games. But by the chief executive's own account, the heavy spending covers about six of them.
And reporting at the time gave reasons for the pace. The pandemic. And a studio trying to move away from the brutal overtime it had been criticised for on earlier games.
In other words, part of the delay was a choice to stop burning people out. That choice costs time, and time costs money.
Then, seven months after the game was confirmed, somebody else showed it to the world first.
In September twenty twenty-two, dozens of clips of the unfinished game appeared on a fan forum. The BBC counted more than ninety videos and images.
Rockstar confirmed it. An unauthorized third party, it said, had illegally accessed and downloaded confidential information, including early development footage.
The trail led to a teenager in Britain. A jury later found that he had carried out the hack, and in December twenty twenty-three a London court ordered him held indefinitely in a secure hospital.
That order was later lifted. And this month, three days before the game came out, he pleaded guilty.
At that hearing, the court was told what the break-in cost Rockstar. Five million dollars to recover, plus thousands of hours of staff time.
That is a real number, given in court. Remember how small it is.
Because in the same weeks, another number started to travel. Gaming sites reported that the hacker appeared to have claimed, in online messages, that more than two billion dollars had already been spent on the game.
An unnamed intruder. An unverified message. Reported with the words appeared to have claimed.
And then watch what happens to it.
In twenty twenty-three, a major broadcaster writes that some speculate the cost could be in the two billion dollar range. No source named.
In twenty twenty-five, a Hollywood trade paper writes that the company is believed to have spent north of one billion. No source named.
Other outlets cite industry analysts at one to one and a half billion. The analysts are anonymous.
One well-known analyst did put his name to a figure, reportedly about one and a half billion dollars including marketing. That is an estimate, and he does not have the invoices either.
And the company? Earlier this year, Zelnick would only tell one magazine that the game was expensive.
Then came one more clue. Last month, in a Bloomberg interview, Rockstar's head of publishing was reportedly asked whether the game cost more than the world's tallest building, about one and a half billion dollars. She said no.
So on this month's earnings call, an analyst asked Zelnick about it directly.
His answer began with seven words. We haven't spoken about budgets at all.
Then he added a warning. Someone saying something is not more than something, he said, does not mean it's close to something. Half of something. Or a tenth of something.
In plain terms, do not do math with that answer.
That is everything on the record. A claim from a hacker, guesses from unnamed analysts, and a company that will not say.
Except companies have to file accounts. And accounts leave fingerprints.
Here is the part almost nobody reads. When a game company pays people to build a game, it does not always count that money as an expense right away.
Once a project is past a certain technical milestone, the cost gets stored on the balance sheet, like a building under construction. It only hits the profit line when the game comes out.
Take-Two reports that stored-up cost every quarter. It is one line, called software development costs and licenses.
At the end of March twenty twenty, the year the chief executive says full production began, that line stood at about four hundred and forty-two million dollars.
Two years later, eight hundred and thirty-seven million.
By March twenty twenty-four, one and a half billion.
By March twenty twenty-six, two point three five billion.
And at the end of September, seven weeks before launch, it stood at about two point five eight billion dollars.
Nearly six times where it started.
Now, the honest limits. That line covers every unreleased game across the whole company, not one title. And it leaves out early-stage work and all the marketing.
So it is not the budget of Grand Theft Auto Six. It is the shadow of it.
There is a second fingerprint. The same analyst method from twenty thirteen. People, multiplied by years.
Take-Two's last annual report counts almost ten thousand employees working in product development, company-wide. Its yearly research and development bill has passed one billion dollars.
And reports based on British company filings say Rockstar's British arm alone averaged more than seventeen hundred staff. That is one country. Rockstar reportedly has about a dozen offices.
So what did it cost? The only truthful answer is that nobody outside the company can tell you. But every fingerprint points the same direction. Well past anything made before it.
For scale, the most expensive films ever documented cost in the region of five to six hundred million dollars each, going by reports on their studios' own filings.
The priciest games with paperwork behind them? One shooter cost over seven hundred million across its lifetime, according to a court filing. One role-playing game cost about three hundred and sixteen million, according to its own maker.
Even the most cautious estimate for Grand Theft Auto Six is nearly double the costliest film on record.
But the budget was never the expensive part. The waiting was.
In May twenty twenty-three, Zelnick made investors a promise. The year ending March twenty twenty-five would be a new phase of growth. More than eight billion dollars in bookings. More than one billion in cash flow.
He did not name the game. He did not have to.
That December, the first trailer went up. It drew over ninety-three million views in twenty-four hours, a record for a video that was not a music video. It made one promise. Twenty twenty-five.
Then the calendar started to slip.
In May twenty twenty-four, the window narrowed to the fall of twenty twenty-five, and the forecast for that promised year dropped to about five and a half billion.
On May second, twenty twenty-five, the fall date was gone too. The new date was May twenty-sixth, twenty twenty-six. We are very sorry, Rockstar wrote, that this is later than you expected.
Take-Two's shares fell as much as ten percent that morning and closed down nearly seven.
Six months later, on November sixth, it happened again. The date moved to November nineteenth, twenty twenty-six.
The next day the stock closed down eight point one percent, which Bloomberg called its worst day in more than a year.
On the call, Zelnick said this. It is always painful when we move a date. We have done so occasionally in the past, and we have never regretted it in retrospect.
And while it waited, the rest of the company was not having an easy time.
In twenty twenty-two, Take-Two had bought a mobile games company in a deal valued at about twelve point seven billion dollars.
In fiscal twenty twenty-four and twenty twenty-five it reported net losses of three point seven four billion and four point four eight billion dollars, driven largely by writing down the value of goodwill on its books.
In April twenty twenty-four, it said it would cut about five percent of its workforce and scrap several projects.
So a company that had promised eight billion dollars was instead writing off billions, and telling the market, twice, to wait another six months.
Everything now rested on one day in November.
The price came first. In June, Rockstar set the standard edition at seventy-nine dollars and ninety-nine cents, and a premium edition at ninety-nine ninety-nine.
Pre-orders opened the next day. Research firms raced to guess the total. One projected about four and a half billion dollars in sales by the end of launch week.
On November ninth, ten days before release, Zelnick told investors that pre-orders were tracking far in excess of the company's initial expectations.
But he did not raise the forecast. We tend to avoid, he said, claiming success before it occurs.
And look at what that forecast was. Eight to eight point two billion dollars in bookings for the year. The same eight billion he had promised in twenty twenty-three. Arriving two years late.
Then came the nineteenth.

A few cautions belong next to that figure. It is the company's own estimate. And it is the value of sales at retail, not the revenue Take-Two itself keeps.
An outside tracker, counting digital stores only, estimated about two point three six billion.
Still, the company calls it the biggest entertainment launch in history, and nobody has seriously argued.

Nobody can say for certain why a stock moves on a given day, and this is not advice about that one or any other.
But some facts were already on the table. A record launch was built into the forecast. The shares had been higher in August.

So the launch is over. The story of what this game earns has barely started.
Go back to the question. What did Grand Theft Auto Six cost?
We know what the hack cost, because it was said in court. Five million dollars.
We know what the delays cost on the days they were announced, because the market priced them in public.
We know that the pile of unfinished game costs on Take-Two's books grew by more than two billion dollars in six years.
We know the first day's sales, by the company's estimate, were larger than every budget guess in this video.
What we do not have is the budget itself. Not from the publisher. Not from the studio. Not from any document.
And maybe that is the most interesting fact in the whole story.
