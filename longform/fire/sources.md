# Sources: How Did Cavemen Make Fire?
Line numbers = narration line order in script.json (= shot order in shots.json). 20 lines, 167 words.

- L1-2, 9-15 (Barnham, Suffolk, England: earliest evidence of MAKING fire, ~400,000 years; baked clay patch, heat-shattered flint handaxes, repeated burning in the same spot, >700 C; two pyrite fragments; pyrite not naturally present in that landscape, so brought in to make fire; makers probably early Neanderthals): Davis (Rob, British Museum) et al., incl. Nick Ashton, 2025, "Earliest evidence of making fire", Nature (published online 10 Dec 2025), doi:10.1038/s41586-025-09855-6. Natural History Museum: https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/news/2025/december/earliest-fire-making-dating-back-400-000-years-unearthed-in-suffolk-england.html (Parfitt: "Because pyrite doesn't occur naturally in that landscape, its presence shows they had the ability to make fire at will"; Stringer: "pretty certainly" early Neanderthals, but no human fossils at Barnham) ; AP: https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2025/dec/10/study-humans-making-fire-400000-years-ago-far-earlier-thought/ ; https://www.sci.news/archaeology/earliest-evidence-fire-making-14416.html . Hence "probably brought it" (L14), "Experts think early Neanderthals" (L15). "Seven hundred degrees" = Celsius.
- L12 (flint struck on pyrite makes sparks): NHM article above; Sorensen et al. 2018 (below).
- L5-6 (Wonderwerk Cave, South Africa, ash and burned bone ~1 million years ago, burning inside the cave): Berna et al. 2012, "Microstratigraphic evidence of in situ fire in the Acheulean strata of Wonderwerk Cave, Northern Cape province, South Africa", PNAS 109(20): E1215-E1220, doi:10.1073/pnas.1117620109 ; ScienceDaily: https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/04/120402162548.htm ; Science News: https://www.sciencenews.org/article/ashes-oldest-controlled-fire .
- L7-8 (using is not making; fire may have been collected from wildfires): Barnham paper/NHM (before the pyrite, natural and deliberate fire were "effectively impossible" to tell apart; earlier fire use = use/maintenance, not proven making). Opportunistic use of natural fire is a standard hypothesis (Roebroeks & Villa 2011, PNAS 108(13): 5209-5214; Sandgathe et al. 2011). Hedged with "Maybe".
- L16-17 (Neanderthals in France, ~50,000 years: microwear on bifaces matches experimentally striking pyrite = strike-a-lights): Sorensen, Claud & Soressi 2018, "Neandertal fire-making technology inferred from microwear analysis", Scientific Reports 8: 10065, doi:10.1038/s41598-018-28342-9, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6053370 ; Leiden release: https://www.universiteitleiden.nl/en/news/2018/07/neanderthals-could-make-fire . Traces were "probable or possible"; some researchers unconvinced -> "suggest".
- L18 (rubbing sticks / wood rots): wooden fire drills are perishable; no Palaeolithic friction fire-making kit has been found (the oldest known fire-making evidence is the Barnham pyrite and the French strike-a-light wear above). Stated as "no proof that old", "Maybe".
- L3-4, 19-20: question / Moss / joke / outro; no new claims.

## Softened
- Makers of the Barnham fires: "Experts think early Neanderthals" (no human fossils at the site).
- Neanderthal strike-a-lights: "suggest" (probable/possible wear traces, debated).
- Wildfire-collecting: "Maybe" (hypothesis).
- Wonderwerk: "ash and burnt bone" only; not claimed as a hearth or as fire-making.

## Dropped
- Bow drill: no Stone Age evidence for its use at these dates; not mentioned.
- ~1.5-million-year fire claims (Koobi Fora, Chesowanja): contested; omitted.
- "350,000 vs 360,000 years older than previous record" figure (outlets differ); omitted.
