# Sources: How Did Ancient Humans Actually Sleep?
Line numbers = narration line order in script.json (= shot order in shots.json).

- L1-3, 44-53, 89-92 (Hadza, 18 minutes, sentinel idea): Samson, Crittenden, Mabulla, Mabulla & Nunn 2017, "Chronotype variation drives night-time sentinel-like behaviour in hunter-gatherers", Proc. R. Soc. B 284: 20170967, doi:10.1098/rspb.2017.0967. Abstract: all subjects simultaneously scored asleep for only 18 min over 20 days; one or more awake/light sleep in 99.8% of epochs; chronotype covaries with age. Duke/ScienceDaily release https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/07/170711215825.htm : 33 men and women aged 20-60, watch-like device 20 days; groups of 20-30 sleeping by the hearth or in grass/branch huts; older participants (50s-60s) went to bed earlier; Hadza "generally don't post sentinels". Data: https://datadryad.org/dataset/doi:10.5061/dryad.jd651
- L10-11 (chimp nests): each weaned chimpanzee builds a new nest most evenings, takes a few minutes, occasionally reuses one: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/chimpanzees-are-extremely-picky-about-where-they-sleep-180951128/ ; https://pages.ucsd.edu/~jmoore/publications/HernandezNestingPatterns13.pdf ; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10560204/
- L18-19 (Wonderwerk ~1 Myr fire): Berna et al. 2012, PNAS 109(20): E1215-E1220, doi:10.1073/pnas.1117620109, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3356665
- L20 (warmth/light/predators): general; predator deterrence stated as "may have".
- L21-22 (evenings, firelight talk): Wiessner 2014, "Embers of society: Firelight talk among the Ju/'hoansi Bushmen", PNAS 111(39):14027; Yetish quoted on trading sleep for social time by the fire (2015 coverage, e.g. https://neurosciencenews.com/evolution-sleep-neuroscience-2902/).
- L25-36 (Border Cave): Wadley, Esteban, de la Pena, Wojcieszak, Stratford, Lennox, d'Errico, Rosso, Orange, Backwell & Sievers 2020, "Fire and grass-bedding construction 200 thousand years ago at Border Cave, South Africa", Science 369: 863-866, doi:10.1126/science.abc7239. Panicoideae (broad-leafed) grass on ash, near back of cave close to hearths; ash from burned older bedding (cleaning/pests); ash as insect deterrent ("we speculate"); camphor bush (Tarchonanthus) in bedding; predates Sibudu by >100,000 yrs. https://www.sciencealert.com/early-humans-were-making-surprisingly-sophisticated-beds-200-000-years-ago ; https://insidescience.org/news/200000-years-ago-humans-created-beds-grass-and-ash-sleep
- L37-41 (Sibudu): Wadley, Sievers, Bamford, Goldberg, Berna & Miller 2011, "Middle Stone Age bedding construction and settlement patterns at Sibudu, South Africa", Science 334: 1388-1391, doi:10.1126/science.1213317. Sedge/rush bedding from ~77,000 yrs, at least 15 layers (77-38 ka), 1 to 3 m2 exposed, used for sleeping and working, from the uThongathi River; Cryptocarya woodii (River Wild-quince) leaves with insecticidal chemicals "suitable for repelling mosquitoes"; regular burning after 73 ka. https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/12/111208151220.htm
- L59-70, 76 (Hadza, San, Tsimane): Yetish, Kaplan, Gurven, Wood, Pontzer, Manger, Wilson, McGregor & Siegel 2015, "Natural sleep and its seasonal variations in three pre-industrial societies", Current Biology 25: 2862-2868, doi:10.1016/j.cub.2015.09.046 (PDF https://gurven.anth.ucsb.edu/sites/secure.lsit.ucsb.edu.anth.d7_gurven/files/sitefiles/papers/yetishetal2015.pdf). Sleep 5.7-7.1 h, "near the low end of industrial societies"; onset 2.5-4.4 h after sunset (mean 3.3 h); waking usually before sunrise (except San in summer), during falling temperature / near the daily low; "Neither group [Tsimane, San] has a word for insomnia"; 1.5% / 2.5% had problems more than once a year vs 10-30% chronic insomnia in industrial societies; nocturnal awakenings infrequent.
- L71-77 (first/second sleep): Ekirch 2001, "Sleep We Have Lost: Pre-industrial Slumber in the British Isles", American Historical Review 106(2):343-386; Ekirch 2005, At Day's Close (500+ references). Yetish et al. 2015 (above): bimodal pattern "not present in traditional equatorial groups today", "may have been a consequence of longer winter nights in higher latitudes". Debate summary: https://www.bbc.com/news/magazine-16964783 ; https://sleepreviewmag.com/sleep-disorders/insomnia/sleeping-in-two-shifts-history-of-biphasic-sleep/
- L78-83 (hibernation): Bartsiokas & Arsuaga 2020, "Hibernation in hominins from Atapuerca, Spain half a million years ago", L'Anthropologie 124(5):102797 (Sima de los Huesos, ~430,000 yrs). Criticism (other causes for lesions; large mammals cannot drop body temperature enough for true hibernation; not persuading most anthropologists): https://archaeology.org/news/2020/12/22/201223-spain-neanderthal-hibernation/ ; https://www.sciencealert.com/?p=45981

## Softened / dropped
- Sentinel: NOT presented as people deliberately "taking turns keeping watch" (the Hadza generally don't post lookouts); framed as natural chronotype variation, "the sentinel idea", and "if the Hadza are any guide".
- Hadza/San/Tsimane presented as modern people who give clues, not as Stone Age replicas.
- "No word for insomnia": limited to the San and Tsimane (the paper's wording), not all three groups.
- Insomnia rate given loosely as "a few people in every hundred" (1.5-2.5%).
- Border Cave: ash as bug barrier = "scientists think"; burning old bedding = "seem to have"; makers not named as a species. "Oldest known grass beds".
- Fire keeping predators away = "may have"; "evenings" = "scientists think".
- Segmented sleep: limited to the last few centuries, mostly Europe; "jury is still out" for the Stone Age.
- Hibernation (Sima de los Huesos): included only as a controversial idea, explicitly doubted ("fun, but probably not").
- Dropped: stone/wooden pillows (Mesopotamia/Egypt, far later, off-topic); human-vs-primate sleep-duration numbers (Samson & Nunn 2015; figures inconsistent across coverage); claims that hunter-gatherers sleep in total darkness (they often had small fires).
- Ending line about a noise waking you at 3 a.m. is a playful joke, not a scientific claim.
