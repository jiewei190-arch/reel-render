A judge is on the witness stand, crying, over a pair of pants.
He wants fifty-four million dollars for them.
He is not the judge in this courtroom. He is the customer, and he says his dry cleaner lost his suit pants.
The family that owns the shop offered him twelve thousand dollars to end it. He said no.
So hold on to one question. How much does the court give him?
That is case number one. Before it come thirteen more real lawsuits that sounded like jokes, until a judge had to rule.
Number fourteen. A man mailed a soft drink company a check for seven hundred thousand dollars, and asked for his fighter jet.
Seattle, nineteen ninety-six. A Pepsi commercial shows a teenager landing a Harrier jet at school. On screen: seven million Pepsi Points.
John Leonard reads the catalog. Extra points cost ten cents each.
So he mails in fifteen real points and a check, for a jet worth more than twenty million dollars.
Pepsi sends the check back. The jet, it says, was a joke. He sues.
The verdict, from a federal judge in New York: no objective person could reasonably have concluded that the commercial actually offered a Harrier jet.
An appeals court agreed. No jet.
That one ended in New York. The next one reached the Supreme Court.
Number thirteen. The United States Supreme Court once had to decide whether a tomato is a fruit.
New York, eighteen eighty-six. Imported vegetables are taxed at ten percent. Fruit comes in free.
Importers named Nix bring in tomatoes from the West Indies, pay the tax under protest, and sue to get it back.
Their evidence at trial: dictionaries, read out loud.
Eighteen ninety-three. The court agrees with the science. Botanically, a tomato is the fruit of a vine.
Then it rules against them anyway. People serve tomatoes at dinner, not as dessert. For the tax law, a tomato is a vegetable.
They lost over one word. The next company lost over its own advertisement.
Number twelve. A company promised one hundred pounds to anyone who caught the flu while using its product. Then a customer caught the flu.
Victorian England. The Carbolic Smoke Ball Company runs a newspaper ad. Use the smoke ball three times a day for two weeks. Catch influenza anyway, and collect.
And it says one thousand pounds is sitting in a bank, to show the company is sincere.
Mrs. Carlill uses it as directed. She gets the flu. The company refuses to pay.
Its defense: the ad was a mere puff. A promise to nobody in particular.
Eighteen ninety-two. Three appeal judges point at that bank deposit. It showed the company meant it.
The verdict: the company must perform its promise.
The next plaintiff could not read an ad. He was a monkey.
Number eleven. A monkey sued a photographer for copyright infringement, in federal court.
Indonesia, two thousand eleven. Wildlife photographer David Slater leaves a camera unattended in a reserve.
A crested macaque named Naruto allegedly takes several photos of himself. The selfies end up in Slater's book.
In twenty fifteen, the animal rights group PETA sues in the monkey's name. Its claim: Naruto owns the copyright.
A trial court dismisses it. PETA appeals, then settles with the photographer. The monkey, the court notes, was not a party to that settlement.
April, twenty eighteen. The appeals court rules anyway. Animals, since they are not human, cannot sue under the Copyright Act.
The next man knew exactly what he was signing.
Number ten. A man sold his farm on the back of a restaurant check, said it was a joke, and learned the joke was binding.
Virginia, December, nineteen fifty-two. W. O. Lucy walks into A. H. Zehmer's restaurant with a bottle of whiskey and an offer: fifty thousand dollars for the Ferguson farm.
Zehmer writes it on a guest check. We hereby agree to sell. He and his wife sign.
By their account, he whispers to her that it is only a joke.
Lucy hires a lawyer to check the title.
In court, Zehmer testifies he was high as a Georgia pine.
Nineteen fifty-four. Virginia's highest court does not buy it. What counts is what you say and do, not a secret intention. It orders the sale.
He said he never meant it. The next company said its own heroes were not people.
Number nine. A toy company went to federal court to prove its superheroes were not human.
Nineteen ninety-four. Toy Biz imports action figures from China, including the X-Men. Customs calls them dolls.
Dolls are taxed at twelve percent. Toys of non-human creatures, like robots and monsters: six point eight.
And under the tariff, a doll has to represent a human being.
So the company points to the claws. The wings. The tentacles.
January, two thousand three. Judge Judith Barzilay, at the Court of International Trade, has the samples in front of her.
Her ruling: these figures do not represent human beings. The company gets the lower tax.
The next court ruled on something even less human. A ghost.
Number eight. A New York court ruled that a house was haunted. As a matter of law.
Nyack, New York, nineteen eighty-nine. Jeffrey Stambovsky agrees to buy a riverfront Victorian for six hundred fifty thousand dollars.
Then he learns that the seller, Helen Ackley, had told Reader's Digest and the local paper that the house had poltergeists.
He wants out. The first court says buyer beware.
Nineteen ninety-one. The appeals court splits three to two. She told the public the house was haunted, so she cannot deny it now. As a matter of law, the house is haunted.
He may seek to cancel the deal and recover his down payment. On his fraud claim, the judge writes, he hasn't a ghost of a chance.
The court never said ghosts are real. It said her own words were. The next judge had to define lunch.
Number seven. A judge had to rule on whether a burrito is a sandwich.
Shrewsbury, Massachusetts, two thousand six. A Panera cafe has a clause in its lease. The shopping center will not rent to another restaurant that makes more than ten percent of its sales from sandwiches.
Then the landlord signs a Qdoba. Burritos, tacos, quesadillas.
Panera's operator says those count as sandwiches. The lease never defines the word.
So Judge Jeffrey Locke opens a dictionary. A sandwich: two thin pieces of bread, with a layer spread between them.
A burrito is typically made with a single tortilla. Not a sandwich. The court refuses to block the burrito shop.
A dictionary settled that one. No dictionary could help with the next defendant.
Number six. A man asked a federal court for permission to sue Satan.
Pennsylvania, nineteen seventy-one. Gerald Mayo files a civil rights complaint against Satan and his staff.
He alleges that Satan has caused him misery, placed deliberate obstacles in his path, and caused his downfall. He asks to file without paying the fees.
Judge Weber does not wave it away. He writes an order.
First problem: the complaint does not say the defendant lives in the district. Second: this might have to be a class action.
Third: the plaintiff gave no instructions for how a United States Marshal should serve the papers.
Permission denied.
The Devil stayed out of court. The next defendant did not. It was a pile of money.
Number five. The defendant was one hundred twenty-four thousand, seven hundred dollars. Not a person holding the money. The money.
Nebraska, two thousand three. A state trooper stops a rented car for speeding.
In a cooler: bundles of cash, wrapped in foil.
The driver first says he has no cash. At trial he says it was pooled to buy a truck for a produce business.
A drug dog alerts to the money. No drugs are found. No drug charges are filed.
So the government sues the cash itself. The trial judge sides with the owners.
Two thousand six. An appeals court reverses, two to one. The dissent points out: no drugs, no drug records, no drug conviction.
The case went back down. What finally happened to that money, we could not confirm.
And it was not the first time the government took an object to court. Once, it was a rooster.
Number four. The United States government seized a solid gold rooster, and put it on trial.
Sparks, Nevada. Casino owner Richard Graves runs a fried chicken dining room called the Golden Rooster Room.
Eighteen karat gold. About fourteen pounds of it.
One problem. Under a nineteen thirty-four law, with a few exceptions, private gold is contraband. One exception: gold made for a customary artistic use.
The Mint's position: a solid gold rooster is not customary. By nineteen sixty, the bird is in a United States Marshal's custody.
The case is titled United States versus One Solid Gold Object in Form of a Rooster.
It goes to a jury. Even the government concedes the rooster is a work of art.
The jury sides with its owner. In nineteen sixty-two, the judge refuses to overturn the verdict.
Three cases left. And in these, people got hurt.
Number three. A man broke into a farmhouse to steal old jars, was shot by a trap, sued the owners, and won.
Iowa, nineteen sixty-seven. The house has stood empty for ten years. It has been broken into again and again.
The Brineys board up the windows. They post no trespass signs.
Then Edward Briney wires a twenty-gauge shotgun to a bedroom door. At his wife's suggestion, he lowers the aim to the legs.
Five weeks later, Marvin Katko opens that door. He spends forty days in the hospital.
He pleads guilty to larceny. His fine: fifty dollars. Then he sues.
A jury awards him thirty thousand dollars.
Nineteen seventy-one. Iowa's Supreme Court upholds it, with one dissent. Its reason: the law has always placed a higher value on human safety than on property.
Most people tell the next one wrong.
Number two. A woman spilled coffee on herself, sued McDonald's, and a jury awarded her millions. True. And most of the story is missing.
Albuquerque, nineteen ninety-two. Stella Liebeck is seventy-nine. She is not driving. She is the passenger, and the car is parked.
She holds the cup between her knees to add cream and sugar. It spills.
The coffee was kept at one hundred eighty to one hundred ninety degrees. She suffers third-degree burns. Eight days in the hospital. Skin grafts.
She asks McDonald's for about twenty thousand dollars. The offer: eight hundred.
At trial, the jury hears about roughly seven hundred earlier burn reports. McDonald's side: she spilled it herself, and customers want their coffee hot.
The jury puts twenty percent of the blame on her. It awards one hundred sixty thousand dollars in damages, plus two point seven million to punish the company. About two days of its coffee sales.
The judge cuts that to four hundred eighty thousand. Then both sides settle, for an amount never made public. Reportedly, less than six hundred thousand dollars.
Not millions. Which brings us back to the man who asked for fifty-four of them.
Number one. The fifty-four million dollar pants.
Washington, D.C., two thousand five. Roy Pearson, an administrative law judge, drops off suit pants for a ten dollar and fifty cent alteration.
The pants go missing. The Chung family, who own the shop, later hand him a pair. He says: these are not my pants.
They offer three thousand dollars. Then four thousand six hundred. Then twelve thousand. No.
He sues over two signs on the wall. Satisfaction Guaranteed. Same Day Service.
His math: fifteen hundred dollars per violation, per day, for each of three owners. About sixty-seven million dollars. Before trial, he trims it to fifty-four.
June, two thousand seven. He takes the stand, and he cries.
Judge Judith Bartnoff's answer: a reasonable customer does not read Satisfaction Guaranteed as a promise to satisfy unreasonable demands.
He gets zero dollars. He is ordered to pay the Chungs' court costs, about one thousand dollars.
In two thousand eight, an appeals court upholds it. And the city does not reappoint him as a judge.
Fourteen lawsuits that sounded like jokes. Every one made a real judge write a real answer.
