# Sources: What Did Ancient Humans Do When They Got Sick?
Line numbers = narration line order in script.json (= shot order in shots.json). 44 scenes / 91 lines / ~1,093 words.

- L1-2, 42-56, 87-88 (Liang Tebo amputation, Borneo): Maloney, Dilkes-Hall, Vlok, Oktaviana, Setiawan, Priyatno, Ririmasse, Geria, Effendy, Istiawan, Atmoko, Adhityatama, Moffat, Joannes-Boyau, Brumm & Aubert 2022, "Surgical amputation of a limb 31,000 years ago in Borneo", Nature 609: 547-552, doi:10.1038/s41586-022-05160-8, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9477728/ . Young adult (~19-20 at death); distal third of left lower leg surgically amputated, probably in childhood; survived 6-9 years; no major infection; not accident/animal attack; burial dated 31,000-30,000 yrs; deliberate grave with limestone markers above head and arms; steep karst rainforest terrain; "a high degree of community care", "intensive post-operative nursing"; authors suggest locally available medicinal plants may have been used; previous oldest = ~7,000-yr-old forearm amputation, Buthiers-Boulancourt, France (pushed back ~24,000 yrs). Found 2020. Press: https://www.uwa.edu.au/news/article/2022/september/earliest-known-evidence-of-surgical-amputation-uncovered-in-borneo
- L9-15 (Mahale chimpanzee, Vernonia amygdalina): Huffman & Seifu 1989, "Observations on the illness and consumption of a possibly medicinal plant Vernonia amygdalina by a wild chimpanzee in the Mahale Mountains National Park, Tanzania", Primates 30: 51-63 (observation 1987; female Chausiku; peeled bark/leaves, chewed bitter pith; plant rarely eaten; recovered by the next day); Huffman, Gotoh, Izutsu, Koshimizu & Kalunde 1993, African Study Monographs 14(4): 227-240, https://repository.kulib.kyoto-u.ac.jp/dspace/bitstream/2433/68112/1/ASM_14_227.pdf ; Tongwe people use the plant for malarial fever, stomach upset and intestinal parasites; pith compounds (Ohigashi et al. 1994) active against parasites. Overview: https://www.snexplores.org/article/wild-medicine ; https://www.wrc.kyoto-u.ac.jp/en/members/huffman/CHIPP.html
- L16-21 (Rakus the orangutan): Laumer, Schuppli, Rahmaeti, Azhari, Huffman et al. 2024, "Active self-treatment of a facial wound with a biologically active plant by a male Sumatran orangutan", Scientific Reports 14: 8932, doi:10.1038/s41598-024-58988-7. Observed June 2022, Suaq Balimbing, Gunung Leuser NP; chewed Akar Kuning (Fibraurea tinctoria) leaves, applied juice then covered wound with chewed plant; plant used in local medicine, antibacterial/anti-inflammatory; wound closed within ~5 days, no sign of infection; "first report" of wound treatment with a known medicinal plant by a wild animal. https://www.sciencenews.org/article/orangutan-medicinal-plant-face-wound
- L22-23: interpretation from the same coverage (Huffman: self-medication as an ancient behaviour that may underlie human medicine); stated as "scientists think ... may".
- L24-25 (El Sidron nod): Weyrich et al. 2017, Nature 544: 357-361 (poplar DNA, salicylic acid; unproven intent) - see ../teeth/sources.md; stated as "may have".
- L28-35 (Shanidar 1): Trinkaus & Villotte 2017, "External auditory exostoses and hearing loss in the Shanidar 1 Neandertal", PLoS ONE 12(10): e0186684. ~50,000 yrs, Shanidar Cave, Iraq (found 1957, Solecki); blow to the face/head (left eye likely blinded), withered right arm later amputated at the elbow, injured right leg/limp, profound hearing loss; all healed; age at death ~35-50 (sources differ); "most likely required significant social support". https://www.sci.news/othersciences/anthropology/paleoanthropology/shanidar-1-neanderthal-05355.html ; https://cosmosmagazine.com/palaeontology/neanderthals-got-by-with-a-little-help-from-their-friends
- L36-39 (Tina): Conde-Valverde, Quam, Martinez, Arsuaga, Daura, Sanz & Villaverde 2024, "The child who lived: Down syndrome among Neanderthals?", Science Advances 10(26): eadn9310. Cova Negra, Valencia, Spain; inner-ear bone (temporal bone) anomalies consistent with Down syndrome, severe hearing loss and disabling vertigo; survived to at least 6; care probably beyond what the mother alone could give. https://cosmosmagazine.com/history/archaeology/down-syndrome-neanderthal-first
- L57-66 (trepanation, Ensisheim): Alt, Jeunesse, Buitrago-Tellez, Wachter, Boes & Pichler 1997, "Evidence for stone age cranial surgery", Nature 387: 360, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9163419 . Ensisheim (Alsace) Neolithic burial, ~7,000 yrs (c. 5100 BC); two openings, one completely healed, the larger partly healed; ~50-yr-old man. https://archive.archaeology.org/9709/newsbriefs/trepanation.html ; https://www.discovermagazine.com/planet-earth/stone-age-surgery . Worldwide healed trepanations, survival commonly high (e.g. Peru 75-83% in Inca period): https://www.historyhit.com/ancient-neurosurgery-what-is-trepanning/ ; Andrushko & Verano 2008, AJPA 137: 4-13.
- L67-68 (reasons): general reviews (above); stated as "nobody is completely sure ... maybe".
- L70-77 (Otzi, fungus, whipworm): died ~5,300 yrs ago; found by two hikers in the Otztal Alps, Sept 1991 (South Tyrol Museum of Archaeology, https://www.iceman.it/en/). Two birch polypore (Fomitopsis betulina) pieces on leather thongs; Capasso 1998, "5300 years ago, the Ice Man used natural laxatives and antibiotics", The Lancet 352: 1864; Aspock, Auer & Picher 1996 (Trichuris trichiura eggs in colon), Parasitology Today 12: 255. Antibacterial compounds (piptamine, polyporenic acids): https://theconversation.com/natures-first-aid-kit-a-fungus-growing-on-the-side-of-birch-trees-117290 ("we will never categorically know").
- L78-81 (tattoos): Samadelli, Melis, Miccoli, Egarter Vigl & Zink 2015, J. Cultural Heritage 16(5): 753-758 (61 tattoos, lines and crosses, mostly lower back, legs/knees, ankles); Dorfer et al. 1999, "A medical report from the stone age?", The Lancet 354: 1023-1025 (tattoos near acupuncture points/areas of joint and spinal degeneration); sceptical: Renaut 2004 (HAL halshs-00575652). https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/what-otzi-icemans-tattoos-reveal-about-copper-age-medical-practices-180970244/ ; https://livescience.com/63682-otzi-ice-man-took-medical-treatment.html

## Softened / dropped
- Borneo: "a child had part of their leg removed by surgery" (the paper: distal third of the left lower leg, probably in childhood; I avoid "foot" because outlets differ). "Planned operation" and "not an accident or animal attack" = "scientists think". Infection-prevention plants = "the scientists suggest ... may have helped". No surgical detail described (kid-safe).
- Chimp: "seemed to be feeling better within about a day"; antiparasite effect phrased as "chemicals that work against some of the worms".
- Rakus: "probably in a fight"; "first time" = "scientists think".
- Instinct-to-medicine link and El Sidron poplar = "may".
- Shanidar 1: blindness "probably"; hearing loss "scientists think"; age at death given as "around forty, or even older" (estimates 35-50). Care = "scientists think".
- Tina: Down syndrome = "may have had" (based on one ear bone; the paper's title is a question). Date (~146,000-273,000 yrs) dropped because sources disagree.
- Trepanation: Ensisheim "around seven thousand years ago" (c. 5100 BC); motives "nobody is completely sure". Dropped exact survival percentages (vary hugely by site/period) and claims of an older "oldest trepanation" (dates conflict).
- Otzi: fungus as medicine = "some scientists think ... may"; tattoos as pain treatment = debated, both sides given. Joints "may well have ached". Dropped bracken-fern-as-dewormer (could be food wrapper).
- Dropped: Neanderthal healthcare generalisations (Spikins et al. 2019) beyond the two cases; La Chapelle-aux-Saints (debated); sweat-lodge/remedy claims (no solid prehistoric evidence found).
- Ending lines ("oldest medicine ... people") are interpretation, framed with "maybe".
