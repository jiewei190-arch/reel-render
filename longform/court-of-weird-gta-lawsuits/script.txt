A teenager sits in a budget hotel room, under police protection. He is about to leak Grand Theft Auto Six.
He is on bail. His laptop has been taken away. He has a hotel television, a streaming stick, and a phone.
With those, a court later heard, he breaks into the company that makes the game, and takes ninety clips of it.
So hold on to one question. What does a court do with him?
That is case number one. Before it come nine more real court fights over Grand Theft Auto. Some of them, Rockstar started.
Number ten. Lindsay Lohan said a character in Grand Theft Auto Five was her, and took it to New York's highest court.
The character is Lacey Jonas, a young actress hiding from photographers. Lohan sues in twenty fourteen, saying the look, the voice and the clothes are hers.
Karen Gravano, of the reality show Mob Wives, files a similar claim over a different character.
A trial judge lets both cases go forward. An appeals court throws them out.
March, twenty eighteen. The Court of Appeals gives Lohan one thing. A video game avatar can count as a portrait under New York law.
Then it rules against her. The character, the court writes, is simply not recognizable as her. A generic twenty-something woman.
Both lawsuits end there.
She said the game copied a person. The next plaintiff said it copied a building.
Number nine. A Los Angeles strip club sued Grand Theft Auto for copying its sign.
The real club is the Play Pen, on the eastern edge of downtown. In San Andreas, the fictional city has a club called the Pig Pen.
Rockstar's artists had photographed Los Angeles for reference, the Play Pen included. In two thousand five, the club's owner sues for trademark infringement.
Its argument: players will think the club endorsed the game.
Two thousand eight. A federal appeals court calls that far-fetched. Nobody, it says, would think one strip club in East Los Angeles also produces a sophisticated video game.
And about players lingering in the virtual club, the judge writes: fans can spend all nine innings at the hot dog stand. That hardly makes Dodger Stadium a butcher's shop.
The First Amendment protects the game. Case dismissed.
That was a joke hidden in plain sight. The next one was hidden in the code.
Number eight. A locked scene inside San Andreas got the game pulled from store shelves, and brought in the federal government.
The makers had left an unfinished sex minigame on the disc, switched off. In two thousand five, a modder unlocks it. The mod is called Hot Coffee.
The ratings board changes the game from Mature to Adults Only. Major retailers pull it.
The Federal Trade Commission charges that the rating on the box was deceptive. In June, two thousand six, the companies settle. No fine for the past. Up to eleven thousand dollars for each future violation.
Then come the class actions. Buyers who swear they were offended can claim up to thirty-five dollars.
The game had sold in the millions. About twenty-seven hundred people file a claim, worth roughly twenty thousand dollars in total.
In July, two thousand eight, the judge undoes the class. We could not confirm how those consumer suits finally closed.
That scandal was about what was on the disc. The next lawsuit was about what a game does to a player.
Number seven. After a triple killing in Alabama, the victims' families sued the makers and sellers of Grand Theft Auto.
Fayette, Alabama, two thousand three. A teenager named Devin Moore, held at a police station, takes an officer's gun and kills two officers and a dispatcher.
He is convicted in two thousand five, and sentenced to death.
Relatives of the victims sue Sony, Take-Two and retailers. The allegation: the games he played helped cause the killings.
The companies answer that selling a game is protected speech, and ask the court to throw the case out. The judge refuses.
March, two thousand six. Alabama's Supreme Court lets the suit continue.
After that, the record we could read goes thin. Reportedly, a judge ruled for the game's publisher in two thousand nine. We could not confirm it from a court record.
The families' lawyer did not see the end of it either. He was thrown off the case. And then out of the profession.
Number six. The lawyer who spent years suing over Grand Theft Auto was permanently disbarred.
His name is Jack Thompson, a Florida attorney and the best-known courtroom critic of violent games.
In the Alabama case, the game companies' lawyers ask the judge to remove him. He tries to withdraw. In November, two thousand five, the judge instead revokes his permission to practice in that court.
Then Florida's bar brings its own charges, over his conduct in several matters.
A referee reports that he repeatedly tried to harm his opponents' reputations, with utter disregard for the administration of justice.
September, two thousand eight. The Florida Supreme Court disbars him for life, and orders him to pay about forty-three thousand dollars in costs.
Thompson's answer: it was retaliation by his enemies. That is his claim. The court did not agree.
He said the game hurt people. The next man said the game was him.
Number five. A man sued for two hundred fifty million dollars, saying the hero of San Andreas was built from his life.
Michael Washington, a model, says he met the developers in two thousand three, told them about growing up around street gangs, and gave them photographs.
His name is in the game's credits, as a model.
In two thousand ten he sues. He says the main character, CJ, is his likeness and his story.
A California trial court strikes the suit. He appeals.
October, two thousand twelve. The appeals court assumes, for argument, that his likeness was used. He still loses.
CJ, it says, is not a literal depiction. Beyond traits shared by many people, the two have nothing unique in common, and the game's story matches nothing in his life.
So far, everyone sued Rockstar. In the next case, Rockstar did the suing. Its target was the BBC.
Number four. The makers of Grand Theft Auto sued the BBC, over a television film about the makers of Grand Theft Auto.
Twenty fifteen. The BBC announces a ninety-minute drama about the games, and about Jack Thompson's fight against them.
Rockstar has no part in it. In May, it files a trademark suit against the broadcaster.
Its statement: the goal is to make sure its trademarks are not misused in an unofficial depiction of purported events.
The film aired anyway, that September, under the title The Gamechangers.
How the lawsuit itself ended, we could not confirm. No ruling was ever widely reported.
The BBC had lawyers. The next defendants were fans with a code repository.
Number three. Take-Two sued a group of fans who had rebuilt two classic Grand Theft Auto games, line by line.
The projects are known as R E Three and R E V C. Programmers reverse engineered the code of Grand Theft Auto Three and Vice City, so the old games could run on modern machines.
February, twenty twenty-one. Take-Two has the projects taken down from GitHub. The programmers file a counter notice, and the code goes back up.
So in September, Take-Two sues in federal court for copyright infringement. It asks for damages of up to one hundred fifty thousand dollars per work.
The fans' side: fair use. Players still needed the original game, and they were fixing it, not pirating it.
No court ever ruled on that. In April, twenty twenty-three, the case against the four named programmers was dismissed, with word of a settlement and no public terms.
That fight was with outsiders. The biggest money fight came from inside the building.
Number two. The producer behind the biggest Grand Theft Auto games sued his own company for one hundred fifty million dollars.
Leslie Benzies ran Rockstar North, the Scottish studio that builds the series. In twenty fourteen he goes on sabbatical. He never comes back.
In April, twenty sixteen, he sues Take-Two, Rockstar, and the brothers who lead it, Sam and Dan Houser.
His claim: unpaid royalties under a two thousand nine profit-sharing plan. He alleges he was pushed out while he was away.
The companies sue back. They say they owe him nothing more.
March, twenty eighteen. A New York court dismisses most of his claims. The plan, it finds, contains no language requiring equal payments. But he remains entitled to certain royalties.
The case reportedly ended in early twenty nineteen with a confidential settlement. The number has never been made public.
One case left. The hotel room.
Number one. The Grand Theft Auto Six leak.
September, twenty twenty-two. Clips from an unfinished Grand Theft Auto Six spread across the internet, years before release.
The trail leads to a teenager from Oxford, part of a hacking group called Lapsus. He was already on bail over attacks on other companies.
Rival hackers had posted his family's details online, so police had moved him to a hotel for his own safety, with a ban on going online.
Jurors heard he did it anyway. Hotel television, streaming stick, phone. Ninety clips.
Rockstar told the court the breach cost it five million dollars, and thousands of hours of staff time.
So. What does a court do with him?
Doctors found him unfit to stand trial. He is autistic. The jury was not asked whether he was guilty. Only whether he did the acts. It found that he did.
December, twenty twenty-three. The judge orders him held in a secure hospital, indefinitely, until doctors judge he is no longer a danger.
The game he leaked came out in November, twenty twenty-six.
Ten court fights over one series. A club, an actress, a lawyer, a producer, a teenager. Each one ended with a real judge and a real ruling, or with a settlement nobody has seen.
