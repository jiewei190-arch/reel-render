# Sources: What If You Were in an Apocalypse With Zero Experience? (Short, checked Oct 30, 2026)

Scenario is a vague, kid-friendly "the power's been out for weeks, everyone left town". No medical or survival claim is made beyond the rows below.

| Line | Claim | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 9-10 | Rule of threes as a rough rule of thumb: minutes without air, hours without shelter in harsh weather, days without water, weeks without food | Wikipedia, "Rule of threes (survival)" (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_threes_(survival)): lists 3 min air / 3 h shelter "in a harsh environment" / 3 days water / 3 weeks food, calls it "a generalization (or rule of thumb), not scientifically accurate" that "may sometimes be useful" for priorities; attributes it to survival manuals (e.g. Towell, *Essential Survival Skills*, 2011). Script uses only the units (minutes / hours / days / weeks), not exact numbers, and calls it rough. |
| 10 | "Weeks without food" (with water) | Peel M., "Hunger strikes", BMJ 1997;315:829, as summarised by Scientific American ("How long can a person survive without food?") and the Open University: severe starvation symptoms around 35-40 days; deaths of the 1981 hunger strikers after about 45-73 days. Survival varies a lot with body weight, health and especially hydration. |
| 10 | "Days without water" | Wikipedia (above) notes the 3-day figure is approximate (based on a 1944 experiment stopped early); Ready.gov "Build a Kit" (ready.gov/kit) plans one gallon per person per day for several days. Script says only "days". |
| 11-12 | Hypothermia can happen above 40 °F if you're wet; get dry and warm first | CDC, "Preventing Hypothermia" (cdc.gov/winter-weather/prevention/index.html): hypothermia "can occur even at cool temperatures (above 40°F) if a person becomes chilled from rain, sweat, or submersion in cold water"; advises removing wet clothing and keeping the person dry. CDC Yellow Book ("Heat and Cold Illness in Travelers") adds that rain and wind can cause hypothermia around 50 °F (10 °C). |
| 13 | Filter cloudy water through a clean cloth; rolling boil for 1 minute, 3 minutes above 6,500 ft | CDC, "Making Water Safe in an Emergency" (cdc.gov/healthywater/emergency/making-water-safe.html): "If the water is cloudy, first filter it through a clean cloth, paper towel, or coffee filter"; "Bring clear water to a rolling boil for 1 minute (at elevations above 6,500 feet, boil for 3 minutes)"; then let it cool. "High in the mountains" stands in for 6,500 ft on screen; the rule card can show "3 min above 6,500 ft (about 2,000 m)". |
| 13 | "With a grown-up" | Safety framing for a kid audience (open flame / boiling water), not a factual claim. |
| 5-6 | Don't drink the puddle | Consistent with CDC above (untreated water must be made safe). The script doesn't say boiling fixes everything: CDC notes boiling does not remove fuel or chemical contamination, which is left out for length; the hero boils water from a safer source (visual: rain collected on the tarp / stored bottles), never the puddle. |

## Dropped or softened
- **"Most people overprioritize food"**: no CDC, Red Cross, FEMA or peer-reviewed source found that measures this, so it's only implied by the joke (chips) and the rule card, not stated as a fact.
- **Exact rule-of-threes numbers ("3 minutes", "3 days")**: sources treat them as approximations (breath-hold divers exceed 3 min; water survival depends on heat and exertion), so the narration gives only the units.
- **Bleach disinfection (CDC: 8 drops unscented bleach per gallon, wait 30 min)**: verified but cut for length and because it isn't something kids should do.
- **Boiling doesn't remove chemicals/fuel**: verified (CDC), cut for length; handled visually by never boiling the puddle.
- **Ready.gov 1 gallon per person per day**: verified, cut for length.
