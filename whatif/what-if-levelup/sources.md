## The levelling maths (story rule, not a fact claim)
STR = 40 kg x 2^(level/3): every level x1.26 ("about a quarter stronger"), every 3 levels x2. Values said in the script:

| Level | Working | Said as |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 40 x 2^(1/3) = 50.4 | "fifty kilos" |
| 6 | 40 x 4 = 160 | "a hundred and sixty kilos" |
| 11 | 40 x 2^(11/3) = 508.0 | "five hundred and eight kilos" |
| 16 | 40 x 2^(16/3) = 1,612.7 | "over sixteen hundred kilos" |
| 23 | 40 x 2^(23/3) = 8,127 | "over eight tonnes" |
| 30 | 40 x 2^10 = 40,960 | "over forty tonnes" |

Story beats with no fact claim (the 40 kg start, the shopping, the sofa and remote, the football under the car, the cracked tiles, the night training, the pumpkin truck and its 7.5 t sticker, the hero's ~50 kg weight, "Strength, forty-four" at the end, Biscuit's level one) are the hero's own story. The 4 kg real gain at the end is a story number; it is consistent with Rule 1 (fast early, mostly neural gains) but not offered as a typical result.

| 10-12 | Beginners get stronger fast with little muscle growth at first; the nervous system learns to activate more muscle fibres; in a classic 1979 study most early gain was neural and muscle growth (hypertrophy) took over after weeks | Moritani & deVries 1979, "Neural factors versus hypertrophy in the time course of muscle strength gain", Am J Phys Med 58(3):115-130 (PMID 453338): neural factors account for most early gain; hypertrophy "the dominant factor after the first 3 to 5 weeks" of an 8-week programme. Folland & Williams 2007, "The adaptations to strength training: morphological and neurological contributions to increased strength", Sports Med 37(2):145-168 (PMID 17241104): neural factors contribute most in the early stages (increased activation/recruitment of motor units), while hypertrophy also starts at the onset. Hence "muscles barely grow" at first, not "don't grow", and "after weeks". Caveat: the 1979 study inferred hypertrophy indirectly from EMG; the later review supports the general sequence. |
| 16 | Heaviest deadlift ever recorded: 501 kg, Hafþór Júlíus Björnsson, Iceland, 2020 | Guinness World Records, "Heaviest deadlift" (guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/heaviest-deadlift): 501 kg (1,104.5 lb), Thor's Power Gym, Kópavogur, Iceland, 2 May 2020, sanctioned by World's Ultimate Strongman. Guinness news item, Sept 2023 ("...iconic deadlift is heaviest ever recorded"). Caveats: it was done with straps outside competition (Eddie Hall's 500 kg, 2016, remains the in-competition strongman mark), so the script says "heaviest deadlift ever recorded" (Guinness's own wording), not "world record in competition". A Giants Live profile reports a later 510 kg pull at the 2025 World Deadlift Championships; not confirmed by a second source, so not used. If the owner wants to update to 510 kg after checking, change line 16 and the level-11 beat (508 kg would no longer beat it; use level 12, 640 kg). |
| 18 | Muscles pull on bones through tendons; bones carry the load | Standard anatomy/physiology (e.g. OpenStax *Anatomy & Physiology*, ch. 11, "Interactions of skeletal muscles, their fascicle arrangement, and their lever systems": muscles attach to bones via tendons and move them as levers). |
| 19 | A fingernail-sized (~1 cm²) piece of thigh bone can take about two tonnes of squeezing along its length | Cortical (compact) bone longitudinal compressive strength ~190 MPa (Université de Liège "Mechanics of bones" lecture notes, GBIO001 ch. 2.3: ~130 MPa tension, ~190 MPa compression, longitudinal); IEEE TechNav "Cortical bone" overview: compressive ultimate stress 153-205 MPa. Classic source of these values: Reilly & Burstein 1975, "The elastic and ultimate properties of compact bone tissue", J Biomech 8(6):393-405. Working: 190 N/mm² x 100 mm² = 19,000 N, the weight of ~1.9 t. Said as "around two tonnes"; this is the solid wall of the bone (cortical bone), loaded straight along its length, and real bones vary with age and person. |
| 20 | Pulled, bent or twisted, bone gives up much sooner | Same sources: tensile strength (~130 MPa) is lower than compressive; across the grain it drops to ~50 MPa tension / ~130 MPa compression (Liège notes); bone is anisotropic, strongest along its long axis (IEEE overview). Shear (twisting) strength is lower again (~69 MPa used in a femur FE study; Reilly & Burstein report a similar order). Bending puts one side of the bone in tension. Kept qualitative ("much sooner"). |
| 21 | Training makes bones and tendons stronger too, but more slowly than muscle; in teen athletes muscle can outpace tendon, and that gap may raise injury risk | Mersmann, Charcharis, Bohm & Arampatzis 2017, "Muscle and tendon adaptation in adolescence: elite volleyball athletes compared to untrained boys and girls", Front Physiol 8:417 (doi:10.3389/fphys.2017.00417): training "provides a more efficient stimulus for muscle compared to tendon adaptation"; "an imbalance in the development of muscle strength and tendon stiffness"; higher tendon strain "could increase the risk ... and predispose for tendon injury", with the authors noting more direct evidence is needed (hence "may raise the risk"). Bone also adapts to loading but on a slow remodelling timescale of months (a single remodelling cycle takes several months; e.g. Hadjidakis & Androulakis 2006, "Bone remodeling", Ann N Y Acad Sci 1092:385-396). Kept qualitative. |
| 22 | That record took years | Björnsson competed in strongman for about a decade before the 2020 lift (Giants Live athlete profile; Guinness). Kept as "years". "Level-eleven muscles on level-zero bones would snap something" is the story's joke, not a measured claim, but follows from lines 18-21. |
| 25 | A small family car weighs about one and a half tonnes | Curb weights: midsize sedan ~1,360-1,580 kg, subcompact ~1,130-1,270 kg (Insurify / SuperMoney summaries); the US EPA's 2022 fleet average (~1,860 kg) is higher because it includes SUVs and pickups. Said as "a small family car, about one and a half tonnes". |
| 28-29 | Newton's third law; holding up a car pushes the car's weight plus yours through your feet into the ground | Newton's third law (OpenStax *College Physics* 4.4). Standing still, the ground must supply an upward force equal to the total weight of you plus the load. |
| 30-31 | Pressure = force / area; holding the car, your feet press more than five times harder than soft, wet ground is rated to hold | Working: (1,500 kg car + 50 kg hero) x 9.81 = ~15,200 N. Two trainer soles ~0.03 m² in contact (assumed: an adult-size sole outline is ~0.015-0.02 m² each, less actually touching). 15,200 / 0.03 = ~510 kPa (if the contact area were as big as 0.04 m², ~380 kPa). BS 8004:1986 presumed allowable bearing values: soft clays and silts < 75 kPa; very soft clay "not applicable"; loose sand < 100 kPa (geotechdata.info bearing-capacity table; excelcalcs BS 8004 table). 380-510 / 75 = 5-7x, so said as "more than five times ... is rated to hold". These are design values with a safety margin, not the exact point of sinking, hence "rated to". The cracked tiles are a comic beat, not a calculated failure. |
| 32 | Spreading the force over a bigger area lowers the pressure; that's how snowshoes work | Pressure = F/A (OpenStax *College Physics* 11.3, "Pressure", which uses the same idea for spreading weight). A 2 m² pair of planks would bring the same 15,200 N down to ~7.6 kPa. |
| 35, 38 | The "aura" is steam: hot skin and moisture rising off it condense into a cloud in cold air | Same physics as seeing your breath: warm, moist air cooling below its dew point condenses into tiny droplets (NOAA/National Weather Service glossary, "dew point"; condensation). Exercising bodies visibly steam in cold air for this reason. Kept descriptive. |
| 37 | The body turns only about a quarter of food energy into movement; most of the rest becomes heat | OpenStax/LibreTexts *Body Physics* ("Efficiency of the human body"): human efficiency about 25%, the rest released mainly as heat from muscle chemistry plus friction. Open Oregon *Body Physics* 2e gives the same ~25%. Caveat: isolated-muscle lab values can be higher (e.g. Barclay et al. 2010, J Physiol, mouse muscle up to ~0.48), so the script says "your body manages about a quarter" (whole-body), not "muscle cells". |
| 39 | Lifting a car half a metre, once, uses about the food energy of one bite of an apple | Working: 1,500 kg x 9.81 m/s² x 0.5 m = 7,360 J of work; at ~25% efficiency that needs ~29,400 J = ~7 kcal of food energy. A medium apple (~182 g) is ~95 kcal (USDA FoodData Central, "Apples, raw, with skin"); at ~12-15 bites per apple, a bite is ~6-8 kcal. Said as "about one bite". |
| 40 | Björnsson has said he eats around 10,000 calories a day in training; most adults need about 2,000 | His own 2016 social-media post as reported by BarBend and others (~10,000 kcal/day); other reports range ~8,000-12,000 depending on the phase (BarBend "Hafthor Bjornsson diet"; Fitness Volt 2024: 11,547 kcal in one prep day, ~8,000 on average). Self-reported, hence "has said" and "around". 2,000 kcal is the reference intake used on US nutrition labels (US FDA, "Daily Value on the Nutrition and Supplement Facts labels"); actual needs vary. |
| 46-48 | To push forward, your feet push back on the ground and only friction stops them sliding; maximum grip ≈ your weight x the coefficient of friction; rubber on dry concrete ≈ 1; wet mud much lower; so a ~50 kg person can push only about their own weight on dry concrete, however strong | Friction model F_max = μN (OpenStax *College Physics* 5.1, "Friction"). Rubber on dry concrete: static μ ≈ 1.0, kinetic ≈ 0.8 (OpenStax table 5.1 / Serway via hypertextbook.com); Engineering Toolbox gives 0.6-0.85 dry, 0.45-0.75 wet. Working: on level ground with a horizontal push, N ≈ body weight (50 kg x 9.81 ≈ 490 N), so the push limit is ~300-490 N, the weight of ~30-50 kg: "about as hard as you can push". No reliable published μ for trainers on wet mud was found, so it stays "much lower" / "far less" with no number. Leaning into the push, as tug-of-war teams do, changes the angles, but the limit is still set by grip; that tug-of-war line was cut for length. |
| 50 | Lifting pushes you down, not sideways | Lifting straight up needs no horizontal friction; it raises the normal force on your feet (lines 28-29), which is why the planks (line 32) matter. Story solution; the sand for the truck's wheel is ordinary traction (more friction, line 47). |
| 62 | Strength comes fast at first, mostly neural, then slowly over years as muscles, bones and tendons catch up | Lines 10-12 and 21 (Moritani & deVries; Folland & Williams; Mersmann et al.). |
| 63 | Square-cube law: twice as tall = about four times stronger but eight times heavier | Muscle force scales with cross-sectional area (L²), mass with volume (L³): J.B.S. Haldane, "On Being the Right Size" (1926); same rule as ep 4's Rule 2. "About four times stronger" assumes the same body shape scaled up. |
| 64 | Kids and teens can strength train safely with good coaching and technique | American Academy of Pediatrics clinical report: Stricker, Faigenbaum & McCambridge, "Resistance Training for Children and Adolescents", Pediatrics 2020;145(6):e20201011 (doi:10.1542/peds.2020-1011); AAP news release (June 2020) and HealthyChildren.org summary: children and teens benefit from resistance training when properly supervised by qualified people with proper technique; some conditions need a doctor first. NSCA youth position statement (Faigenbaum et al. 2009) agrees. Said as "doctors say ... with good coaching and technique". |

## Dropped or softened
- **"Each level = X% stronger" as a real thing:** the compounding rule is fiction and only ever presented as the screen's rule (line 5). The ending says plainly that "nobody doubles every three days".
- **Hafþór's later 510 kg (2025):** one source only (Giants Live); not used. The script keeps the Guinness-listed 501 kg and its wording "heaviest deadlift ever recorded". The strongman-competition record (Eddie Hall, 500 kg, 2016) is not mentioned to keep it simple.
- **Exact bone shear/twist numbers:** values vary by study (and the classic Reilly & Burstein table could not be checked in full text), so "pull, bend or twist ... gives up much sooner" stays qualitative. Only the well-agreed compressive value (~190 MPa, range 153-205) is turned into "around two tonnes".
- **"Tendons cause injuries":** softened to "may raise the risk of injury", because Mersmann et al. say more direct evidence is needed.
- **Bone remodelling timescale:** the "months" figure varies by source, so the script just says bones adapt "more slowly than muscle".
- **Reduced co-activation of opposing muscles** (a real neural adaptation in Folland & Williams 2007): written into an earlier draft, cut for length.
- **Pressure number:** shoe contact area is an estimate, so the comparison is "more than five times", not a precise multiple, and the cracked tiles have no number.
- **Friction on mud:** no reliable μ found, so no number.
- **Body heat output during exercise (often quoted as 10-20x resting):** not used; the steam beat needs no number.
- **Ant-style "lift 50x your weight" comparisons:** ratios vary hugely by species and method (ep 4 dropped them for the same reason).
- **Truck-pull and car-lift strongman feats:** records depend on equipment and rules and were not needed; not used.
- **Strength-training age limits / "lifting stunts growth":** the AAP says supervised training does not harm growth, but the script only states the positive, well-sourced point (safe with good coaching and technique).
