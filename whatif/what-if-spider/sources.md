# Sources: What If You Had Spider Powers? (checked Oct 30, 2026)

Legal note (owner-approved "safe version"): no franchise names, characters, places, catchphrases, costume, logo or set pieces. The hero's look is original (see the script header), powers come from a staring contest with a jumping spider in the shed (no bite, no lab), silk comes from the back end like a real spider's, and nothing swings between buildings. The narration never uses the "s... sense" wording; it says "you felt the air move". The franchise is only named below, where a source's own title or the reason for a cut needs it.

Story beats with no fact claim (waking on the ceiling, the drainpipe, nine eggs, the fence jump, the burger, the apple-tree rescue, Pip waving) are the hero's own story. They are not offered as real numbers.

## Dropped or softened
- **"A pencil-thick web could stop a train":** traced. The source is Bryan, Forster & Stone, "Doing whatever a spider can", University of Leicester *Journal of Physics Special Topics* (written 2012, publicised by a Leicester press release on 25 Feb 2013; ScienceDaily 130225092040). It is a short MPhys student exercise in writing and peer review, not a research paper. It modelled the train scene from the 2004 superhero film (four loaded R160 New York subway cars, ~300,000 N to stop them), found the film's webbing would need a stiffness of 3.12 GPa (inside the 1.5-12 GPa range of real orb-weaver silk) and a toughness of ~500 MJ/m³ (like Darwin's bark spider silk), and concluded the film's web is "proportional" to real silk. It never measured a pencil-thick strand; the "pencil" version is a separate, older pop claim. **Cut entirely:** the calculation exists only to test that film scene, so retelling it, even as "a student calculation", would recreate the copied set piece the legal note bans.
- **Darwin's bark spider "10 times tougher than Kevlar":** the peer-reviewed paper (Agnarsson, Kuntner & Blackledge 2010, PLoS ONE, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0011234) says "toughest known biomaterial"; the "10 times Kevlar" figure only appears in press quotes, in two different forms. The script uses the review-backed general claim (tougher than Kevlar) instead.
- **"Up to seven kinds of silk" (AMNH, Cheryl Hayashi; Australian Museum gives up to eight glands per species and ten gland types overall):** verified but cut for length; sources give different counts.
- **Exact jumping-spider field of view (360°):** only Wikipedia and HHMI state "nearly 360"; a peer-reviewed paper mentions ~350° ambiguously. Softened to "almost all the way around".
- **UV vision in jumping spiders:** the principal eyes' retina responds from near-UV to red and the posterior median eyes carry a UV opsin, but the details are technical and vary by species, so it was left out.
- **Hydraulics as THE jump engine:** the 2018 Manchester study found muscle alone could supply the power, so the script says pressure straightens the legs and that scientists still argue about the jump.
- **"Dead spiders curl up because the pressure is gone":** only popular sources, and it's grim for this audience. Cut.
- **Trichobothria count per leg (~50 vs ~100 for *Cupiennius salei*):** sources disagree, so no number is used.
- **Ant strength ratios (50x to 1,000x+):** vary hugely by species and method, so the square-cube rule stays qualitative.
- **Venom:** jumping spider venom is not a story engine here and any "venom powers" beat risks being scary, so venom is not mentioned; the eating gag uses digestion only.
- **Stanford glove date:** Tech Xplore reported it in Nov 2014; the journal issue is 2015. The script gives no year.
