# What If You Could Freeze Time for 10 Seconds? (script v1, Nov 5)

Narrator: the locked What If voice (whatif/VOICE.md, Kokoro am_fenrir), second person, calm and warm. Cast: HERO ("you", never lip-synced except quoted lines), MINA (little sister, red scarf, times everything with her stopwatch), DEV (best friend, orange beanie, films everything), BISCUIT (the cat). About 990 spoken words, so ~6.5 minutes at the ep 4 pace.

Hero look for this episode (original design, keep it this way): shaggy black hair with one cowlick that sticks straight up, round tortoiseshell glasses, a teal-and-cream striped long-sleeve tee, navy pyjama bottoms with a small star print, mismatched socks (one green, one yellow), and a chunky black digital wristwatch that is too big for the wrist (the watch matters in Rule 5). The power is only ever a finger snap: no remote, no stopwatch, no pocket watch, no gadget, no glowing eyes, no clock-face symbols on the hero. Never goggles, headphones, a silver or white hair streak, a jacket with lightning bolts, a cape or a costume. No running laps around the frozen room set to music: the hero walks, reaches and moves things by hand, and the setting stays the family kitchen at breakfast. Frozen moments are shown with a cool blue-grey tint and hanging details (juice drops like glass beads, Dev mid-sneeze, Biscuit mid-leap); normal time is warm colour again.

Story rule used all episode: one snap = ten seconds that only you live through. Each new snap fixes the last snap's problem (nobody knows how) and reveals a new one.

## COLD OPEN
1. Tuesday. Breakfast. Mina knocks her orange juice off the table, and without thinking, you snap your fingers.
2. Everything goes black. Silent. You can't even see your own hand.
3. You count to ten. Then the light comes back, the juice lands, and Mina's socks are orange.
4. Nobody knows why your snap does this. Not even you. Let's see what ten seconds can do.

## TITLE: What if you could freeze time for 10 seconds?

## SNAP 1: THE DARK
5. You tell Dev and Mina. Dev starts filming. Mina starts her stopwatch.
6. To them, nothing happened. One moment you were in your chair, the next, you were standing in a puddle.
7. But why was it dark? The lights were on. The sun was out.

## RULE 1 CARD: YOU ONLY SEE LIGHT THAT ARRIVES.
8. Light is the fastest thing there is. About three hundred thousand kilometres every second.
9. Even so, it has to travel. Sunlight takes about eight minutes and twenty seconds to get here from the Sun.
10. You see when light lands inside your eye, on the back wall, called the retina.
11. Freeze the light, and every bit of it hangs in the air, between the window and your eyes. None of it arrives.
12. So frozen time wouldn't look frozen. It would look like nothing at all.

## SNAP 2: THE WALL OF AIR
13. Second snap. This time, the snap seems to have learned. The light keeps moving.
14. And it's beautiful. Juice drops hang like glass beads. Dev is frozen mid-sneeze. Biscuit is mid-leap, aiming at the butter.
15. You step forward, and walk into something soft. Like a mattress you can't see.
16. You try to gasp. Nothing comes in.

## RULE 2 CARD: AIR IS STUFF.
17. Air looks like nothing, but it's packed with molecules. In one cubic centimetre, about the size of a fingertip, there are around twenty-five billion billion.
18. And they never sit still. At room temperature, they zoom around at roughly five hundred metres a second, faster than a jet airliner.
19. All that bumping is air pressure. When you breathe in, your diaphragm makes room in your chest, and the air outside pushes itself in.
20. Frozen molecules can't push anything. So nothing flows into your lungs.
21. And air is heavy. The air in a small kitchen weighs about as much as you do.
22. If every molecule stayed stuck, you couldn't open a door, or even move your hand. You'd be a statue too.
23. Luckily, ten seconds of holding your breath is fine. You count, cheeks puffed, until the world starts again.

## SNAP 3: THE CAT
24. Third snap. Now the air around you moves. You can breathe. You can walk.
25. Biscuit is still frozen mid-leap, paws out, inches from the butter.
26. You gently lift her out of the air, and set her down on the floor. Butter saved.
27. Time restarts. And Biscuit shoots sideways across the tiles like a furry hockey puck, straight into the bin.

## RULE 3 CARD: MOTION DOESN'T PAUSE.
28. Newton's first law: a moving thing keeps moving the same way, unless a force pushes or pulls it.
29. Moving things carry momentum. It's their mass times their speed. A heavier or faster thing is harder to stop.
30. Picking Biscuit up didn't take her speed away. In our story, freezing doesn't delete motion. It saves it for later.
31. To stop a moving thing, you have to push against its motion. Like a goalkeeper's gloves.
32. Biscuit climbs out of the bin and gives you a long, cold look.

## SNAP 4: THE STUCK CLOCK
33. Fourth snap. Ten quiet seconds. Then time restarts.
34. You glance up at the kitchen clock. The second hand doesn't move.
35. Your heart jumps. Is it still frozen? Then it ticks. And ticks again. Normal.

## RULE 4 CARD: YOUR BRAIN CAN STOP A CLOCK.
36. That has a real name. The stopped clock illusion.
37. Your eyes don't glide. They jump, about three times a second. During each jump, the view is a blur.
38. Your brain hides the blur. It fills the gap with whatever your eyes land on.
39. So the first tick you see seems to last too long. In 2001, scientists in London showed the brain stretches that moment back in time, to just before your eyes moved.
40. Everyone has frozen a clock. Just for a split second.

## THE WATCH
41. That evening, Mina checks your big black watch against the oven clock.
42. "Yours is forty seconds fast," she says. Four snaps. Ten seconds each.
43. You've lived forty seconds more than everyone else today.

## RULE 5 CARD: CLOCKS REALLY CAN DISAGREE.
44. In real life, that happens. Just much, much smaller.
45. Satellites for GPS, the system behind maps on phones, carry super-accurate clocks.
46. Up there, gravity is weaker, so their clocks tick a tiny bit faster. They're also moving fast, which slows them a little.
47. Add it up, and they gain about thirty-eight millionths of a second every day, compared to clocks down here. Einstein predicted it.
48. If engineers didn't correct for it, your map would soon be off by kilometres.

## THE RESCUE
49. Wednesday. Breakfast. Biscuit leaps for the butter again, and hits the milk jug.
50. It tips off the table, straight towards Mina's school project. Snap.
51. Light: moving. Air: moving. You take a breath.
52. Rule three. You can't just put the jug back. It would still be falling.
53. So you slide Mina's project away, and put the big mixing bowl underneath the jug.
54. Time restarts. Splash. Every drop lands in the bowl.
55. Mina looks at her dry project. Then at you. Then at her stopwatch. "Zero point zero seconds."

## THE TWIST
56. But during those ten seconds, something moved that shouldn't have.
57. Biscuit. She blinked at you, stretched, and walked across the frozen kitchen.
58. Maybe that's why cats always appear in places they couldn't have reached.
59. Next morning, you snap. Nothing happens. Your snap is gone.
60. Biscuit flicks her tail. And somehow, she's already on the table, eating your toast.

## ENDING
61. In real life, nobody can stop time. And if it did stop, you'd be stuck in the dark, unable to breathe or move.
62. But scientists have stopped light. In 2001, at Harvard, Lene Hau's team trapped a pulse of light in a cloud of super-cold atoms for about a thousandth of a second, then let it go again.
63. Your brain stretches moments every day. And satellite clocks really do run at a different speed.
64. So if you had ten frozen seconds, what would you do with them? Tell us in the comments.
65. And subscribe for more What Ifs.
