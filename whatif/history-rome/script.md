# How Did the Roman Empire Get Destroyed? (History Hook, script v1, Nov 8)

Format: I.Awaken "History Hook" (IAWAKEN_PLAYBOOK.md, NEW FORMAT). Narrator: Kokoro am_fenrir, third person, documentary storyteller. Pacing: slow and heavy in THE CLIP (speed 0.88), story pace in the chapters (0.97), slow again for the return to 410 and the twist (0.90). `<!-- speed x -->` lines set the pace for `narrate.py` (whatif/history-rome/narrate.py, a copy of whatif/voice/fenrir.py with that one switch). Only numbered lines are spoken; `##` headers (title card, chapter cards) are on-screen only and add a 1.4 s break. About 1,150 spoken words, so ~7.5 minutes.

Recurring cast (original designs, no real person's likeness):
- **LIVIA** (410): a Roman girl of about twelve, dark curls tied back, a moss-green palla (shawl) with a cream border, a plain cord around her neck with the family coin on it. The episode's face (cold open, thumbnail, ending). Fictional.
- **GAIUS** (117): Livia's ancestor, a young legionary: red tunic, segmented armour, helmet with cheek guards, the same moss-green scarf at the neck (the family colour). He is paid the silver denarius that becomes the family coin. Fictional.
- **FELIX** (235-301): Gaius's great-grandson, a baker in Rome (moss-green apron), who watches emperors and prices change. Later his son (same apron, a beard) holds the new coin next to the old one. Fictional.
- **THE FAMILY COIN**: a bright silver denarius (laurel head, no readable real portrait), worn on a cord. Recurs in every chapter; it is the visual thread (silver vs. the later copper coins).
- **EMPERORS**: generic figures with a laurel wreath and purple cloak, each told apart by a NAME CHIP (Trajan, Severus Alexander, Valerian, Diocletian, Valens, Theodosius, Honorius, Romulus Augustulus). Stylised, no portrait likeness. Romulus Augustulus is drawn as a small boy on a too-big throne with a wreath that slips.
- **THE GOTHS**: a Gothic family on the Danube bank in 376 (a mother, a father, a boy), told as people, not monsters. ALARIC as an adult commander (fur-trimmed cloak, long braid, a round shield with a red sun) from 394 on, then a silhouette at the gate in 410.
- **ODOACER**: a big bearded general in a dark-red cloak, calm, never shown fighting.

Violence rule: NO gore. Fighting and the sacks are shown only as silhouettes, torchlight, fire, smoke, falling shadows, banners and sound. Nobody is shown wounded or dead; deaths are told by the narrator over a fallen symbol (a helmet in the grass, a dropped wreath, an empty throne).

## THE CLIP
<!-- speed 0.88 -->
1. August, the year 410. Midnight. A gate in the walls of Rome swings open.
2. Gothic warriors pour into the streets. Torches. Smoke. Doors breaking.
3. For the first time in about eight hundred years, a foreign enemy is inside the city of Rome.
4. A twelve-year-old girl named Livia hides in the dark, gripping an old silver coin. It has been in her family for almost three hundred years.
5. Rome. The city that ruled the known world. And now, parts of it are burning.
6. How did the greatest empire on Earth end up here?
7. The answer takes three hundred years and five big mistakes. And it ends with a twist most people have never heard.

## TITLE: HOW DID THE ROMAN EMPIRE GET DESTROYED?

## THE PEAK
<!-- speed 0.97 -->
8. To understand how, we have to go back. To that coin.
9. The year 117. Livia's ancestor Gaius is a young soldier in a Roman legion, and Rome is at its peak.
10. Under the emperor Trajan, the empire stretches from Britain to Egypt, and from Spain to the rivers of Mesopotamia.
11. Around five million square kilometres, and somewhere between fifty and seventy-five million people.
12. One army. One money. And Gaius is paid in that money: a heavy silver coin called a denarius.
13. He keeps one. It becomes the family's lucky coin. For a while, it really looks like Rome can never lose.
14. But a hundred years later, the cracks start to show.

## CHAPTER 1 CARD: EMPERORS, ONE AFTER ANOTHER

15. In the year 235, his own soldiers kill the emperor Severus Alexander. What comes next, historians call the Crisis of the Third Century.
16. In about fifty years, Rome has more than twenty emperors. Most of them die violently, killed by rivals or by their own troops.
17. An army crowns its general, he marches on Rome and wins. Then another army does the same to him.
18. Gaius's great-grandson Felix runs a bakery in Rome. The faces on the coins change faster than he can learn the names.
19. And Rome's enemies smell weakness. In 260, the Persians capture the emperor Valerian alive. A Roman emperor, taken prisoner.
20. Things get so bad that Rome builds huge new walls around the city itself. Remember those walls.
21. But all this fighting has a price. And someone has to pay it.

## CHAPTER 2 CARD: THE MONEY MELTS

22. So the emperors cheat. Every new batch of coins gets a little less silver, and a little more cheap copper.
23. More coins from the same silver. It works, for a while. Then people notice.
24. By the 270s, Rome's everyday silver coin is often less than five percent silver. It's basically copper, with a shiny coat.
25. Felix's son holds a new coin next to Gaius's old one. One is real silver. The other only looks like it.
26. When money is worth less, prices go up. Historians argue about how bad it got. But in 301, the emperor Diocletian sets maximum prices for more than a thousand goods and wages, and threatens death for breaking them.
27. It doesn't work. Goods vanish from the markets instead.
28. And the army still needs paying, so taxes keep rising, mostly on ordinary farmers.
29. Diocletian sees another problem too. The empire is just too big for one man.

## CHAPTER 3 CARD: TOO BIG FOR ONE EMPEROR

30. A message from one end to the other could take weeks. By the time an emperor hears about an invasion, it's old news.
31. So in 293, Diocletian splits the job: four rulers, each guarding his own part of the empire.
32. It works, until they fight each other. The winner, Constantine, builds a new capital in the East, where Europe meets Asia: Constantinople.
33. After him, the empire is usually run by two emperors at once: one for the West, one for the East.
34. The East is richer, with bigger cities. The West has long borders, and less money to guard them. Keep that in mind.

## CHAPTER 4 CARD: A STORM FROM THE EAST

35. Around the year 370, a new people rides out of the steppes. Horse archers. The Huns.
36. Historians still argue about exactly where they came from. But the Goths, living north of the Danube river, are terrified.
37. In 376, tens of thousands of Goths gather on the riverbank, begging to be let into the empire.
38. The emperor of the East, Valens, says yes. He wants new farmers and new soldiers.
39. But the Roman officers in charge make a mess of it. Food runs out, and the hungry Goths are cheated and mistreated.
40. The Goths rise up. And on the 9th of August, 378, near the city of Adrianople, Valens attacks them.
41. It's a disaster. Valens is killed, and so are about two-thirds of his army, according to the historian Ammianus.
42. An emperor of Rome, killed by people Rome had let in. And Rome can't beat them. So it tries something else.

## CHAPTER 5 CARD: HIRING THE ENEMY

43. In 382, the emperor Theodosius makes a deal. The Goths can live inside the empire, under their own leaders, if they fight for Rome.
44. More and more, Rome's armies are outsiders, fighting for pay, loyal to their own commanders.
45. In 394, a young Gothic commander fights for Theodosius in a civil war. Thousands of his Goths die. He feels Rome used them. His name is Alaric.
46. A few months later, Theodosius dies, and the empire is split between his two sons, for good. The West gets a ten-year-old boy, Honorius.
47. Alaric wants land for his people, gold, and a top job in the Roman army. The West keeps saying no.
48. Then, in 408, the West executes its best general, Stilicho. Roman soldiers attack the families of barbarian troops in Italy, and thousands of those troops join Alaric.
49. Alaric marches on Rome. Twice, he surrounds the city and it pays him to leave.
50. The third time, nobody pays.

## BACK TO 410
<!-- speed 0.90 -->
51. The night of the 24th of August, 410. The gate opens. Now you know how it got here.
52. For three days, the Goths loot the city. Many are Christians, and they spare the great churches and the people hiding inside.
53. Livia survives. Far away in Bethlehem, a scholar named Jerome hears the news and writes: the city that had taken the whole world was itself taken.
54. But it doesn't stop there. In 455, the Vandals sail from North Africa and loot Rome again. This time, for two weeks.
55. And in 476, a general named Odoacer, the leader of the barbarian troops in Italy, removes the last western emperor. A boy. His name is Romulus Augustulus.
56. He doesn't even kill him. He sends the boy to a villa, and ships the emperor's crown and purple robes to Constantinople. The West doesn't need an emperor anymore.
57. Most history books stop right there. 476. The fall of Rome.

## THE TWIST
58. But remember the split? Rome had two halves. And the eastern half was still standing.
59. Its emperors still ruled from Constantinople. Its people still called themselves Romans.
60. About sixty years later, they even took back Italy, and the city of Rome itself, for a while.
61. Today we call it the Byzantine Empire, but that name came much later. They just called it Rome.
62. And it lasted almost another thousand years. Until the 29th of May, 1453, when Ottoman cannons broke the walls of Constantinople.
63. So did Rome really fall in 476? Historians still fight about it. Some say the West truly collapsed, and life got poorer for centuries. Others say it slowly changed into the Middle Ages. One historian collected two hundred and ten reasons people have given for the fall.
64. Maybe Rome wasn't destroyed in a day. Maybe it never fully fell at all.
65. And Livia's coin? It's still silver.
