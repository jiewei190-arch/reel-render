Saturday. You're in the garden shed looking for Mina's bike pump, and something is looking back at you.
A tiny, fuzzy spider. Two enormous eyes. It tilts its head. You tilt yours.
It hops onto your thumb. No bite. Just a very long stare. Mina names it Pip.
Next morning, you wake up stuck to the ceiling. For about two seconds. Then you're on the bed, and Biscuit isn't.
Nobody knows how a staring contest gives you spider powers. Not even Pip. Let's see what one week does to you.
Day one. You press your hands on the hallway wall, and they stick. So do your socks.
You climb, then slowly slide back down, like a wet sticker. Dev drops his cereal anyway.
Mina checks your palms with a magnifying glass. "No glue."
Jumping spiders don't use glue either. Each foot has a tuft of hairs, and every hair splits into even tinier ones.
Up that close, molecules pull on each other. It's called van der Waals force. One tip barely grips. Hundreds of thousands grip a lot.
In one study, scientists estimated that a jumping spider's feet could hold more than a hundred and seventy times its own weight.
Geckos stick the same way, and peel their feet off like tape.
Day two. Halfway up the drainpipe, you start sliding again.
Not because the trick stopped working. Because you're enormous.
Here's the problem. Make something twice as tall, and its weight goes up eight times, but the area of its feet only goes up four times. It's called the square-cube law.
It's also why tiny creatures seem super strong for their size. Scale them up, and that advantage disappears.
Cambridge researchers compared two hundred and twenty-five climbing species, from mites to geckos. The bigger the animal, the more of its body was sticky foot.
For a human to climb that way, about forty percent of your whole body would need sticky pads. Or shoes in US size one hundred and fourteen.
Their verdict: geckos are about the size limit for climbing with sticky feet.
Mina covers your hoodie in sticky notes. "Forty percent," she says. It does not help.
Day three. Silk. Bad news: spiders don't make it with their hands.
It comes from spinnerets, at the back end. So that's where yours comes from too.
Dev films you walking backwards across the garden, trailing a shiny line. He's never been happier.
Inside a spider, silk is a liquid protein. As it's pulled out through tiny nozzles, it turns into solid thread.
The safety line, dragline silk, is amazing. Weight for weight, it rivals steel. And it's tougher than Kevlar, the fibre in bulletproof vests.
Thread for thread, steel still wins. Silk is just much lighter.
But protein comes from food. Some spiders even eat their old webs to recycle it.
By lunch, you've eaten nine eggs and all of Dev's sandwiches.
Day four. Mina creeps up behind you with a water balloon. You step aside without looking.
You felt the air move.
Spiders have special hairs called trichobothria. The faintest puff of air bends them. They work close to the physical limit of what any sensor can detect.
In 2016, Cornell researchers found jumping spiders reacting to sounds three metres away. About six hundred body lengths. No ears. Just hairs.
When the team played a low hum, like the wingbeat of the wasps that hunt them, most froze.
Then there are the eight eyes. Two big front ones see sharp detail, like zoom lenses. The rest spot movement almost all the way around.
Biscuit fails to sneak up on you for the first time ever. She takes it personally.
Day five. You leap from the back step onto the garden fence. Clean.
But your knees feel strange. Like water balloons.
Spiders have muscles to bend their legs. But at two big leg joints, they have no muscles to straighten them.
Instead, they raise the pressure of the fluid inside them, like a hydraulic machine, and that pushes the legs straight.
A jumping spider can leap up to six times its body length from a standing start. The best humans manage about one and a half.
Scientists still argue how much jumping uses that pressure. One spider, trained to jump on cue in Manchester, suggests muscle alone could be enough.
Dev measures your fence jump. One body length. Very human.
Day six. Dev hands you a burger, and your body has an idea.
Most spiders can't chew. They cover their food in digestive juices, wait for soup, and slurp.
You look at the burger. The burger looks at you. You have a milkshake instead.
Day seven. A storm blows Mina's red scarf into the apple tree. Biscuit goes after it. Now Biscuit's stuck too.
You climb branch by branch, the normal way. Rule two.
But you feel every gust coming on your arm hairs, and hug the trunk before it hits.
At the top, you can't hold on and hold a cat. So you tie off a silk line, wrap Biscuit in the scarf, and lower her down.
One soggy cat is easy.
Biscuit lands in Mina's arms, furious.
Next morning, you wake up in bed, not on the ceiling. In the shed, Pip waves one leg.
In real life, a staring contest with a spider gives you nothing but a staring contest.
But people are borrowing the tricks. With gecko-inspired hand pads from Stanford, a seventy-kilo climber went up a glass wall nearly four metres tall.
Labs spin artificial spider silk, too. So far, it's weaker than the real thing.
The catch is always size. What works at the size of a raisin falls apart at the size of you.
