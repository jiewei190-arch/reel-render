You roll out of bed and make a promise. Every day, for three years: a hundred push-ups, a hundred sit-ups, a hundred squats, and a ten kilometre run. About six miles.
No days off. No idea what you're getting into.
Mina looks at your hair, then at you. "Everyone says if you train that hard, your hair falls out."
Dev nods, very seriously. "It's a known thing."
Is it? Three years. One thousand and ninety-five days. Let's find out.
Day one. Push-ups first. You hit twenty-three, and your arms just... stop.
You finish the rest in tiny sets, then on your knees. Biscuit sits on your back to help.
The run takes an hour and twenty minutes. A lot of it is walking.
Day two, you can't lift your arms to brush your hair. Mina does it, then checks the brush. "Still all there."
That ache has a name: delayed onset muscle soreness. It usually peaks one to three days after the workout.
It hits hardest after moves your muscles aren't used to, especially the lowering part, like sinking into a squat.
The good news? Do the same workout again, and the soreness is usually much milder. Scientists call it the repeated bout effect. Your muscles learn.
By week one, you can walk downstairs without the rail. Mostly.
Month one. A hundred push-ups in five sets. Dev films it. Mina measures your hairline and writes it in a notebook labelled "Hair Log".
Your abs, though? Nothing.
Sit-ups don't burn the fat on top of those muscles. In one study, six weeks of ab exercises made stomach muscles last longer, but didn't shrink belly fat at all. You can't choose where fat comes off.
Month three. The hundreds get... easy.
A hundred push-ups in two sets. You're not tired. You're bored.
Your body adapts to exactly what you do. Coaches call it specificity. Ask for a hundred push-ups, and it gets brilliant at a hundred push-ups.
But once that's easy, the question stops being hard, and the answer stops changing.
Light exercises can build muscle, but mainly when you push close to your limit. And for raw strength, heavier loads win.
Real training plans keep adding a little more. It's called progressive overload. Your plan has a hundred. Forever.
Dev hands you a backpack full of textbooks. "Wear this." Suddenly it's day one again.
Year one. Ten kilometres in under fifty-five minutes.
Running burns roughly one calorie per kilo of body weight, per kilometre. For someone weighing seventy kilos, that's around seven hundred calories. Every day.
More than a quarter of what an adult man typically needs. You're always hungry. Mina hides the cereal anyway. "For your own good." It isn't.
Then your shins start to ache, right along the bone.
Shin splints. An overuse injury: too much running, too little recovery. Keep pounding, and it can become a stress fracture, a tiny crack in the bone.
In studies that follow runners over time, about four in ten get injured.
Your body doesn't get stronger during a workout. It rebuilds in the rest afterwards. Skip the rest, and the damage outruns the repairs.
So you bend your rule. On bad days, you walk the ten kilometres. Biscuit watches from the window, like she always knew.
Year two. One morning, there's hair in the shower drain. A lot of it.
Dev gasps. "It's happening."
First, relax. Everyone sheds fifty to a hundred hairs a day.
Male pattern baldness is mostly genetic. Twin studies suggest around eighty percent of the difference between men comes down to their genes.
Exercise can nudge hormones. In one year-long trial, men doing an hour of cardio six days a week ended up with slightly more DHT, a hormone linked to balding. But no study has shown that working out makes you bald.
What can make hair fall out is a shock to the body, like serious stress or losing a lot of weight fast. A few months later, hair sheds in handfuls. Doctors call it telogen effluvium.
Seven hundred calories a day, and no cereal. That's you.
So you eat properly again. Dermatologists say this shedding is usually temporary, and within about six to nine months, your curls are back.
Mina writes one word in the Hair Log: "Unchanged." Dev looks almost disappointed.
Year three. You check your pulse on the sofa. It's slow. Really slow.
Endurance training lowers your resting heart rate. For most adults, normal is sixty to a hundred beats a minute. A well-trained athlete can be closer to forty.
A trained heart pumps more blood per beat, so it beats less often.
But people improve by different amounts. In one big study, over seven hundred inactive adults did the same twenty-week program. Some boosted their fitness hugely. Some barely changed.
You're a good responder. Biscuit, who has trained for zero days, is not.
Day one thousand and ninety-five. Mina rides beside you. Dev holds a finish line made of toilet paper.
Over a hundred and nine thousand push-ups. Nearly eleven thousand kilometres. More than a quarter of the way around the Earth.
Dev grabs a pillow. "Okay. One punch. Show us what three years did."
You wind up. Everyone holds their breath. You punch.
Flump. The pillow wobbles. Biscuit, asleep on top of it, opens one eye.
And your hair doesn't move at all.
In real life, three years of this would change you a lot. A stronger heart, serious endurance, and a lot of laundry.
Health experts suggest adults get at least a hundred and fifty minutes of moderate activity a week. This blows past that.
But it won't make you superhuman. You'd stall unless you made it harder, and without rest, your body pushes back.
As for your hair? That was always up to your family tree.
