# Sources: cranberries (Why do cranberries float?)
Researched Oct 28 2026. Growers' institute, peer-reviewed physics and National Park Service first.

| Claim (script) | Source |
|---|---|
| Cranberries don't grow underwater; bogs are flooded to harvest | Cranberry Institute, "How cranberries grow: Fall" https://www.cranberries.org/fall ("bogs can be flooded to aid in removal of fruit from the vines"); Farmers' Almanac "Cranberries don't grow underwater, so why are the bogs flooded every fall?" https://www.farmersalmanac.com/why-are-cranberries-harvested-underwater |
| Cut one in half: four little air pockets inside | "The physics of cranberry bogs", American Journal of Physics 94(4):283 (2026) https://pubs.aip.org/aapt/ajp/article/94/4/283/3383704/The-physics-of-cranberry-bogs (arXiv 2601.11593): the fruit's four air pockets make it much less dense than water |
| Trapped air makes the berry less dense than water, so it floats | Same paper; Cranberry Institute ("Cranberries have pockets of air inside the fruit") |
| In the wild that may help seeds drift to new places | Same paper (buoyancy lets the berries float long distances to disperse their seeds); voiced as "scientists think" |
| Cranberries grow on low vines in sandy beds called bogs | Cranberry Institute (cranberries.org, How cranberries grow) |
| A water reel, nicknamed an egg-beater, churns the vines and knocks berries loose | Cranberry Institute: "Water reels, nicknamed 'egg-beaters' are used to stir up the water in the bogs" |
| They bob to the surface; floating booms sweep them together | Cranberry Institute: "Wooden or plastic 'booms' are used to round up the berries"; Farmers' Almanac (berries rise and are gathered with floating booms) |
| More than 90 percent of the crop is picked this way, for juice and sauce | Cranberry Institute: "More than 90% of the crop is wet harvested"; used "for juices, sauces, sweetened dried cranberries" |
| Fresh-market berries are combed off dry by walk-behind machines, then graded with a bounce | Cranberry Institute: dry harvesting "uses walk-behind machines to comb the berries off the vines"; fresh fruit "graded and screened based on color and ability to bounce (soft berries will not bounce)" |
| Legend: New Jersey grower John Webb noticed it when berries tumbled down his stairs | National Park Service, Pinelands history (chapter 7) https://www.nps.gov/parkhistory/online_books/nj3/chap7.htm (John "Peg Leg" Webb, Cassville NJ; the stairs story is told as tradition) |

## Softened / careful wording
1. The Webb story is voiced as "Legend says"; no date is given (sources differ: 1840s vs later).
2. The seed-drift idea is "scientists think" (the paper's interpretation).
3. No density number is given (one weak source claims "80% less dense"; not used).
4. The bounce is not tied to the air pockets in the voiceover (only grower material makes that link); firm vs soft is what the Cranberry Institute states.
