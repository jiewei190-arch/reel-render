Why do cranberries float? Cut one in half and you'll find four little air pockets inside. That trapped air makes the whole berry less dense than water, and scientists think it helps the seeds drift to new places in the wild. Cranberries grow on low vines in sandy bogs, not underwater. At harvest, farmers flood the bog, a water reel (nicknamed an egg-beater) knocks the berries loose, they bob to the surface, and floating booms sweep them into a red raft. More than 90% of the crop is picked this way, for juice and sauce. Fresh berries are combed off dry and graded with a bounce: firm ones bounce, soft ones don't. Have you ever bounced a cranberry?

Sources: Cranberry Institute; American Journal of Physics, "The physics of cranberry bogs"; National Park Service.
