# Sources: caffeine (how caffeine fools your brain)

| Claim in the reel | Source |
|---|---|
| Adenosine slowly builds up in the body while you are awake and makes you drowsy; it dissipates during sleep | Johns Hopkins Medicine, "Sleep/Wake Cycles" https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/health/conditions-and-diseases/sleepwake-cycles |
| Adenosine acts by binding to receptors on brain cells; caffeine promotes wakefulness by blocking the receptors for adenosine | Johns Hopkins Medicine (above); Harvard Health, "What is it about coffee?" https://www.health.harvard.edu/healthbeat/what-is-it-about-coffee (adenosine receptors are the main target) |
| Caffeine is an antagonist at adenosine receptors (it occupies them without activating them); the A2a receptor is particularly responsible for the wakefulness effect | StatPearls, "Caffeine" (NCBI Bookshelf) https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK519490/ |
| Caffeine's molecular shape is similar enough to adenosine's to fit the same receptors | Explanatory summary in secondary sources (UBC Communicating Science blog; CSPI); the primary-source wording is "antagonist" (StatPearls) |
| Blocking adenosine receptors affects dopamine and arousal-related brain areas | Harvard Health, "What is it about coffee?" (above) |
| Circulating caffeine peaks about 30 to 45 minutes after ingestion | Harvard Health, "What is it about coffee?" (above) |
| Caffeine does not remove adenosine; it blocks the receptors adenosine binds to | Logical consequence of the receptor-blocking mechanism (Johns Hopkins; StatPearls); not stated as a separate finding in any one source |
| Caffeine's half-life is typically about four to six hours | StatPearls, "Caffeine Withdrawal" https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK430790/ (typical half-life 4 to 6 hours) |
| Too much caffeine can cause insomnia or sleep disruption | FDA, "Spilling the Beans: How Much Caffeine Is Too Much?" https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much |
| FDA cites 400 mg a day for most adults as an amount not generally associated with negative effects, about two to three 12-oz cups of coffee | FDA (above) |

## Dropped / unverified
- "Caffeine gives no energy": stated only as "doesn't give you energy; it blocks the tired signal", i.e. the mechanism above; no calorie claim is made.
- The "crash when caffeine wears off" idea (adenosine suddenly rushing in) is not stated in the sources found, so it is not in the reel.
- Peak time varies by source (15-120 min in some StatPearls passages, 30-45 min in Harvard Health); the reel uses Harvard Health's 30 to 45 minutes.
- The cartoon (adenosine as lavender blobs, receptors as parking spots, caffeine in disguise) is an analogy, not a depiction of real molecules.
