# Sources: eyeblink ("Why do we blink so much?")

| Claim in the script | Source | Type |
|---|---|---|
| We blink about fifteen times a minute | American Academy of Ophthalmology, "Computers, Digital Devices, and Eye Strain" (https://www.aao.org/eye-health/tips-prevention/computer-usage): "Humans normally blink about 15 times in one minute." | professional body (institutional) |
| "You go blind for a moment" (vision is blanked during each blink) | Nakano et al. 2009 (below), Introduction: "Our visual world is interrupted by eyeblinks every few seconds." | peer-reviewed |
| Way more than your eyes need to stay wet | Osaka University press release on Nakano et al. 2013 (https://resou.osaka-u.ac.jp/en/research/2012/20121225_1): blinks occur "every few seconds, more often than necessary for ocular lubrication" | university |
| Each blink spreads a fresh layer of tears; that's the known job | AAO (same page): "Blinking is the eye's way of getting the moisture it needs on its surface." | institutional |
| On a screen, about five to seven a minute; eyes get drier but cope | AAO (same page): "we only blink about 5 to 7 times in a minute while using computers"; prolonged screen use "won't cause permanent eye damage" but eyes may feel dry | institutional |
| People watching the same video blinked together, almost in sync | Nakano T, Yamamoto Y, Kitajo K, Takahashi T, Kitazawa S. "Synchronization of spontaneous eyeblinks while viewing video stories." Proc R Soc B 276(1673):3635-3644 (2009), doi:10.1098/rspb.2009.0828 (PMC2817301) | peer-reviewed |
| The blinks landed on calm moments: an action ending, the main character leaving the screen | Nakano et al. 2009, abstract: synchronized blinks occurred "at the conclusion of an action, during the absence of the main character", during scenes that required less attention | peer-reviewed |
| Blinks seem to fall at natural pauses, where you're least likely to miss something | Nakano et al. 2009: blinks seek "an implicit timing that is appropriate to minimize the chance of losing critical information"; Osaka release: "eyeblinks tend to occur at implicit breakpoints" | peer-reviewed; university |
| A brain-scan study: right after a blink, attention areas dipped and the default mode network lit up | Nakano T, Kato M, Morito Y, Itoi S, Kitazawa S. "Blink-related momentary activation of the default mode network while viewing videos." PNAS (published online Dec 2012, 2013 issue; 20 volunteers watching Mr Bean clips in fMRI): activity "momentarily decreases in the dorsal attention network after blink onset but increases in the default-mode network" | peer-reviewed |
| A fake blink (screen blacked out) didn't shift the brain | Same PNAS paper: physical blackouts of the video "do not elicit such reciprocal changes in brain networks" | peer-reviewed |
| Each blink may be a micro-break / tiny reset for attention; studies suggest, still testing | Osaka release: "we hypothesized that eyeblinks are actively involved in the release of attention"; the PNAS abstract: blinks may be "actively involved in the process of attentional disengagement" | university; peer-reviewed |
| Bonus (pinned): no sync for a background video or an audio story | Nakano et al. 2009, abstract | peer-reviewed |

## Softened / left out
- AAO's figure is "about 15"; the main session's 15-20 range was cut to match it.
- "Micro-break", "tiny reset" and "may" frame the attention idea as a hypothesis; the end says "scientists are still testing it".
- "Lit up" stands for "increased activity"; the board says "resting network" without numbers.
- No blink-duration or "10 % of your day blind" figure (the 2009 paper's 10 % assumes 400 ms per blink).
- The 2010 speaker/listener paper (blinks at speech pauses) is only alluded to in the closing question.
