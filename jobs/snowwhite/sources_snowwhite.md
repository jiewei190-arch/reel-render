# Sources: snowwhite (why is snow white if ice is clear?)

| Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|
| Snow looks white because the ice grains scatter a large amount of light and reflect nearly all visible light without favouring a colour | NSIDC (National Snow and Ice Data Center), "Science of snow": https://nsidc.org/learn/parts-cryosphere/snow/science-snow [institutional] |
| "Light is scattered and bounces off the ice crystals in the snow"; "snow reflects all colors. And all the colors of light add up to white" | UCAR Center for Science Education, "Snow: white, maybe or maybe not": https://scied.ucar.edu/learning-zone/how-weather-works/snow-white-maybe-or-maybe-not [institutional] |
| Most of the volume of a snow layer is air | NSIDC "Science of snow" ("most of the total volume of a snow layer is made up of air") |
| Each crystal has flat faces; at each face light partly reflects and partly enters and bends; light bounces from crystal to crystal and comes back out in every direction | NSIDC and UCAR (scattering by ice grains); Live Science, "Why is snow white?" (irregular crystal surfaces scatter light in all directions): https://www.livescience.com/17677-snow-show-east.html |
| Ice barely absorbs visible light, so every colour comes back together | NSIDC (reflects nearly all visible light without favouring any colour) |
| A smashed windowpane: clear glass, but the pile of pieces looks white | Caltech physicist Kenneth Libbrecht's comparison, quoted by Live Science (link above) |
| Poke a hole in the snow and look down: you may see a bluish colour; deep snow absorbs red light; light travelling through a metre or more of snow loses more red than blue | NSIDC "Science of snow" |
| The deeper the light travels, the bluer it gets | NSIDC (more snow, more red absorbed); Live Science (the more scattering, the stronger the blue) |
