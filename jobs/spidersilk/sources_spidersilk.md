# Sources: spidersilk (Why spider silk is so strong for its weight)
Researched Oct 28 2026.

| Claim (script) | Source |
|---|---|
| A web's silk stops a flying insect (prey impact) | Sensenig, Lorentz, Kelly & Blackledge, "Spider orb webs rely on radial threads to absorb prey kinetic energy", J. R. Soc. Interface 9(73):1880 (2012). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3385755/ |
| It's not stronger than steel, thread for thread; good silk is about as strong as a mid-range steel | University of Cambridge Engineering / The Conversation, "Spider silk is a wonder of nature, but it's not stronger than steel": "For one variety of spider silk the value of tensile strength is just above 1 GPa... a mid-range value for steel, where strengths range from 0.2 GPa to nearly 2 GPa." https://theconversation.com/spider-silk-is-a-wonder-of-nature-but-its-not-stronger-than-steel-14879 (also https://www.eng.cam.ac.uk/node/151) |
| About six times lighter, so per weight silk wins | Same article: density "almost six times less"; "On a per-weight basis... the ratio of strength to density exceeding that of steel." |
| Silk starts as a liquid protein stored in glands; pulled out through spigots on the spinnerets and hardens into a fibre | Silk proteins are spidroins (Babb et al., Nature Genetics 2017, orb-weaver silk genome); each gland leads to spinneret spigots; the liquid solidifies into a fibre as it leaves (summaries: Reconnect With Nature, "How do spiders make silk"; Frontiers in Ecology and Evolution review 2020) |
| An orb weaver can make up to seven kinds of silk | Babb et al., Nature Genetics 49:895 (2017): typical orb-weaver female makes major and minor ampullate, piriform, aciniform, tubuliform, flagelliform and aggregate silk; Frontiers in Ecology and Evolution (2020) lists the same seven glands |
| Silk is tough: it stretches and absorbs energy before breaking; some spider silks are tougher than Kevlar pound for pound | Encyclopaedia Britannica (M. Petruzzello), "Is spider silk stronger than steel?": some silks are stronger than steel and tougher than Kevlar pound for pound; a steel wire of the same thickness is generally stronger; silk stretches 30-200% depending on type; Gosline et al., J. Exp. Biol. 202:3295 (1999) (toughness of dragline silk exceeds synthetic fibres) |
| In a web, the stiff spokes (radial threads) take most of the hit | Sensenig et al. 2012: "Radial silk dominated energy absorption in all webs"; "Radii are approximately three orders of magnitude stiffer than capture spirals" |
| Much of the energy turns into heat, so the bug doesn't bounce out | Sensenig et al. 2012: energy is "permanently removed from the prey through viscous dissipation as flight energy is converted to heat", "critical for preventing insects from 'catapulting' back out of the web" |
| The sticky spiral, up to ten times stretchier, holds the prey | Sensenig et al. 2012: "Capture spiral silks are highly compliant and up to an order of magnitude more extensible than radial silk"; capture spiral selected for "adhering to and retaining prey" |

## Softened / careful wording
1. The popular "stronger than steel" claim is corrected, not repeated: strength is comparable to mid-range steel, the win is per weight (Cambridge/Conversation; Britannica).
2. "about six times lighter" = the article's "almost six times less" dense.
3. "some spider silks are even tougher than Kevlar, pound for pound" is attributed to "some" silks (Britannica); no numbers shown. The toughness bars in FIG. 4 are qualitative.
4. The force-stretch curves (FIG. 4) are schematic shapes, not measured data; the "springy net" is a hypothetical comparison, labelled "NOT SILK".
5. "~1 GPa" on both specimens in FIG. 2 follows the Conversation article (silk just above 1 GPa; steel ranges 0.2-2 GPa).
