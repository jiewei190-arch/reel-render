# Sources: catfeet (how do cats always land on their feet?)

- University physics: Greg Gbur (Professor of Physics and Optical Science, UNC Charlotte), *Falling Felines and Fundamental Physics* (Yale University Press, 2019); UNC Charlotte news item quoting him on the falling-cat problem: https://inside.charlotte.edu/?p=43711 ; book coverage: https://www.livescience.com/animals/cats/how-do-cats-land-on-their-feet
- Primary physics: T. R. Kane and M. P. Scher, "A dynamical explanation of the falling cat phenomenon", International Journal of Solids and Structures 5:663-670 (1969) (the bend-and-twist model).
- Vet school, primary: D. Vnuk et al. (Veterinary Faculty, University of Zagreb), "Feline high-rise syndrome: 119 cases (1998-2001)", Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery 6:305-312 (2004), PMID 15363762: https://catvets.com/publication/feline-high-rise-syndrome-119-cases-1998-2001/
- Background (the term): W. O. Whitney and C. J. Mehlhaff, "High-rise syndrome in cats", J Am Vet Med Assoc 191(11):1399-1403 (1987).

| Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|
| It's called the righting reflex, and it puzzled physicists for a long time | Gbur: "One of the reasons that physicists were surprised that cats could rotate to always land on their feet is the conservation of angular momentum" |
| A falling cat has nothing to push against; you can't start spinning in midair from nothing (conservation of angular momentum) | Gbur (as above); Kane & Scher 1969 (a falling cat turns over with zero total angular momentum) |
| A cat bends in the middle; the front and back halves turn separately, each twist balanced by an opposite twist, so the total spin stays zero | Kane & Scher 1969 (the bend-and-twist model); Gbur's book: a cat bends at the waist and turns its front and back halves in opposite directions |
| First it turns its front half, tucking its front legs in to turn faster, then the back half | Gbur's book (the tuck-and-turn model, one of the competing explanations) |
| Physicists think cats mix moves: the bend and twist, and the tuck and turn | Gbur's book lists several models; Live Science: "it's likely they employ more than one technique" |
| Vets call falls from windows and balconies high-rise syndrome | Whitney & Mehlhaff 1987 (the term); Vnuk 2004 |
| In one study of 119 cats, nearly half had broken legs | Vnuk 2004: 119 cats; about 46% had limb fractures |
| Falls from the seventh floor or higher caused worse injuries | Vnuk 2004: falls from the seventh floor or higher were associated with more severe injuries and more chest trauma |

Not used: the "righting reflex develops by about 7 weeks" age (only pet-care sites were found for it, no vet-school or primary source). No terminal-velocity or "higher is safer" claim (the studies disagree; Vnuk found 7+ floors worse).
