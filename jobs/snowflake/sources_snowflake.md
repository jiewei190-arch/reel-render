# Sources: snowflake (why do snowflakes have six sides?)

| Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|
| Six-fold symmetry comes from how water molecules are arranged in the ice crystal lattice (a precise hexagonal array) | Kenneth G. Libbrecht (Caltech), SnowCrystals.com, "Snowflake Science": http://www.snowcrystals.com/science/science.html ("The six-fold symmetry you see in a snow crystal arises from the arrangement of water molecules in the ice crystal lattice"; "the water molecules are all lined up in a precise hexagonal array") [university scientist, primary] |
| A water molecule is one oxygen and two hydrogens in a bent V; hydrogen bonds (hydrogen of one molecule to the oxygen of the next) lock them into a hexagonal ring | KQED Deep Look, "Identical Snowflakes? Scientist Ruins Winter For Everyone" (Josh Cassidy, Apr 11 2017, updated Feb 6 2025), with Libbrecht: https://www.kqed.org/science/1536955/identical-snowflakes-scientist-ruins-winter-for-everyone-deep-look ("bonds forming between hydrogen of one molecule and the oxygen of another molecule", a hexagonal ring, "which is why you tend to find snowflakes with six arms") [public media, with a Caltech scientist] |
| A snow crystal starts out as a tiny hexagonal prism | Libbrecht, "Snowflake Science" ("When snow crystals first begin growing, they are shaped like the simple hexagonal prisms"); Caltech News, "Snowflake Science" (Dec 6 2011): https://www.caltech.edu/news/snowflake-science-2023 ("starts out, as all do, as a hexagonal crystal of ice") |
| As it grows, branches sprout from the six corners | Libbrecht, "Snowflake Science" ("branches sprout from the six corners when the crystal grows larger") |
| The six arms match because they grow at the same time through the same changing conditions | Libbrecht ("the six arms all took the same path, and so each experienced the same changes at the same times"; "the six arms grow in synchrony"); KQED ("the crystals experienced the same conditions at the same time") |
| Temperature picks the shape: near -2 °C thin plates, near -5 °C needles/columns, near -15 °C big starry (stellar) flakes | Caltech News ("near -2°C, they grow into thin, platelike forms; near -5°C, they create slender columns and needles"; plates again near -15°C, thin large plates in high humidity); Libbrecht ("Needles and columns are best found around -6 C"; the largest stellar crystals "only grow in a narrow temperature range around -15 C") |
| No two flakes take the same path through the clouds, so no two look exactly alike | Libbrecht ("since no two snow crystals follow the exact same path through the clouds as they fall, no two look exactly alike"); KQED ("you're not likely to find two that match exactly") |
| Snowflakes are ice crystals that fall from a cloud and are often six-sided | NSIDC, "Science of snow": https://nsidc.org/learn/parts-cryosphere/snow/science-snow [government-funded data center] |
| Pinned bonus: near-identical twin snowflakes grown in a lab | KQED Deep Look (Libbrecht grows "identical twin snowflakes" side by side, very similar but not exactly alike) |

Not used on purpose: the often-quoted "104.5° bond angle makes the hexagon". 104.5° is the gas-phase angle; in ice the arrangement is tetrahedral (about 109.5°; ChemEd X, "The Diamonds of Winter"), and sources disagree on whether the angle by itself explains the six sides. The VO says only "bent, like a V" plus the hydrogen-bond ring. Also left out: the Nakaya morphology diagram by name and date (the temperatures are shown instead) and three-fold (trigonal) flakes.
