# earwax: sources

1. Schwartz SR et al., "Clinical Practice Guideline (Update): Earwax (Cerumen Impaction)", Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery 156(1_suppl):S1-S29 (2017), AAO-HNS Foundation. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28045591/ ; AAFP summary (Aug 2017): "Although there are benefits to cerumen, including cleaning and protecting the ear canal…"; "Patients should be advised against using cotton swabs or small objects to clean the ear."; "approximately 12 million persons present for problems related to cerumen". https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2017/0815/p263.html
2. AAO-HNS Foundation, ENT Health, "Earwax (Cerumen)". Earwax "serves as a self-cleaning agent with protective, lubricating, and antibacterial properties". Wax and dead skin cells move slowly from the eardrum toward the ear opening, helped by chewing and jaw movement, and the wax dries, flakes and falls out. Cotton swabs "only push the wax in deeper" and can injure the eardrum. https://www.enthealth.org/conditions/earwax-cerumen/
3. MedlinePlus (NIH/NLM), "Ear wax". Glands in the ear canal make a waxy oil called cerumen. It traps dust, bacteria and other germs and small objects. "Never try to clean the ear by putting any object, such as a cotton swab, into the ear canal." https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000979.htm
4. Yoshiura K et al., "A SNP in the ABCC11 gene is the determinant of human earwax type", Nature Genetics 38(3):324-330 (2006). The 538G>A SNP (rs17822931): AA = dry earwax; GA and GG = wet. Allele A frequency is highest in Chinese and Koreans. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16444273/

Wording notes:
- "about 12 million Americans to a doctor each year": the guideline counts about 12 million people a year presenting with cerumen problems in the US.
- "one letter in one gene": a single-base change (SNP) in ABCC11; the reel shows G vs A cards.
- "Dry wax is most common in East Asia, like China and Korea": from Yoshiura's frequency data.
- "a self-cleaning, self-moving shield" is the reel's summary of sources 1-3.
