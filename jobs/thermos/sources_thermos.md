# Sources: thermos (How does a thermos keep drinks hot AND cold?)
Researched Oct 28 2026. Institutional sources first.

| Claim (script) | Source |
|---|---|
| A thermos doesn't make heat or cold; it slows heat crossing in both directions | University of Sydney School of Physics, "Thermos Flask" tutorial sheet: the vacuum "prevents heat being transferred from the inside to the outside"; the same walls work for cold contents (heat in). https://www.physics.usyd.edu.au/super/physics_tut/activities/Thermal_Physics/Thermos_Flask.doc ; Encyclopaedia Britannica, "vacuum bottle" https://www.britannica.com/technology/vacuum-bottle |
| Heat travels three ways: conduction, convection, radiation | Same Sydney sheet (all three routes discussed); standard school physics |
| Two walls with almost nothing between them: a vacuum | Sydney: "A thermos flask has double walls, which are evacuated"; Royal Institution: glass flasks "fitted one inside the other and sealed at the neck with a partial vacuum between them" https://rigb.org/node/3131 |
| With no air, heat can't conduct across and no currents can carry it | Sydney: "The vacuum between the two walls prevents heat being transferred ... by conduction and convection. With very little air between the walls, there is almost no transfer of heat ... by convection." |
| That leaves radiation (infrared); the walls are silvered to reflect it back inside | Sydney: "The silvered walls reflect radiated heat back to the inside, the same way a space blanket does"; Britannica: radiation is minimised by silvering |
| Heat still escapes at the top: the walls join at the neck | Sydney: "Conduction can only occur at the points where the two walls meet, at the top of the bottle and through an insulated support at the bottom"; Britannica: the neck, where the walls meet, is the main remaining path |
| That's why the neck is narrow and the stopper is thick | Royal Institution: the design was improved "by narrowing the neck even further"; Sydney: "It has a thick stopper" |
| James Dewar showed it in London on Christmas Day 1892 | Royal Institution collection entry (Dewar flask, 1892): first exhibited at the Ri "on Christmas Day 1892" https://rigb.org/node/3131 |
| He built it to keep liquefied gases cold and never patented it | Royal Institution: made "to hold liquefied gases at extremely low temperatures"; "Dewar never patented his invention" |
| A German company later patented its own version and called it Thermos | Royal Institution: the Thermos company, set up by German glassblowers, patented and adapted it in 1904; Thermos history (thermos.com/pages/history) |

## Softened / careful wording
1. "Almost nothing between them" (a partial vacuum, not a perfect one).
2. "Heat can't conduct across": except at the neck and the base support, which the script covers as the weak point.
3. No temperatures or hours-of-keeping numbers are given (none sourced from an institutional page).
4. The silvering: the RI says the silver coating was a later refinement of Dewar's first flask, so the script describes the modern flask's silvered walls, not the 1892 original.
5. "A German company later patented its own version": RI says 1904; Wikipedia says a 1903 German patent and the 1904 trademark, so the voiceover gives no year for it (the on-screen note says "1904 · THERMOS", matching the RI).
