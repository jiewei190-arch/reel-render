# Sources: moonday (why can you sometimes see the Moon during the day?)

- NASA, "We Asked a NASA Scientist: Why Can You See the Moon During the Day?" (Episode 19, Sarah Noble): https://www.nasa.gov/feature/episode-19-we-asked-a-nasa-scientist-why-can-you-see-the-moon-during-the-day
- Washington State University, Ask Dr. Universe, "Why can you sometimes see the moon during the day?" (WSU astronomer Michael Allen): https://askdruniverse.wsu.edu/2025/09/25/can-sometimes-see-moon-day/
- University of Arizona Lunar and Planetary Laboratory, Catalina Sky Survey moon-phase page (first quarter): https://catalina.lpl.arizona.edu/moon-phases/2022-07-06

| Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|
| The Moon makes no light of its own; it reflects sunlight | NASA: "Unlike our Sun, the Moon doesn't create its own light" |
| It's bright enough to shine through the blue daytime sky | NASA: "the Moon actually shines bright enough that you can see it day or night as long as it's in the right part of the sky" |
| Stars are up in the daytime too, but the sky's glare hides them | NASA (stars are also up during the day but washed out by the sky's brightness) |
| Each day the Moon rises about fifty minutes later | NASA: it sets (and rises) about 50 minutes later each day as it moves through its phases |
| The Moon spends almost as much time in the daytime sky as at night | NASA: "the Moon actually spends almost as much time in the daytime sky as the night" |
| Near new Moon it sits close to the Sun, lost in the glare | NASA (visibility depends on the Moon's position relative to the Sun); WSU: we see the Moon only "when it's in the right part of the sky and reflecting enough light" |
| About a week after new Moon, the first quarter Moon rises around noon and glows all afternoon | WSU: "Look for the first quarter moon in the afternoon about one week after the new moon"; Univ. of Arizona LPL: the first-quarter Moon rises around noon and is high at sunset |
| About a week after full Moon, the third quarter Moon hangs in the morning sky | WSU: "Or the third quarter moon in the morning about one week after the full moon" |

Illustrative, labelled "(about)": the moonrise log's clock times (12:00, 12:50, 13:40, 14:30) only show the ~50-minute daily slip. The brightness gauge ranks Moon > day sky > stars qualitatively, with no numbers.
