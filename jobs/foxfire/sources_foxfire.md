# Sources: foxfire (why some mushrooms glow)

| Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|
| Some fungi glow on their own (bioluminescence); the glow on decaying wood is called "foxfire" | Wikipedia, "Foxfire": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foxfire |
| The light comes from luciferin, oxidised with the help of the enzyme luciferase (reaction with oxygen) | Wikipedia, "Foxfire"; Kaskova et al., "Mechanism and color modulation of fungal bioluminescence", Science Advances 3(4): e1602847 (2017): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5406138 |
| The fungal light is green ("bluish-green") and is cold light (Aristotle's "cold to the touch") | Wikipedia, "Foxfire" (Aristotle's notes describe a light that, unlike fire, was cold to the touch) |
| Aristotle wrote about it more than 2,000 years ago | Dartmouth Geisel School of Medicine news, Mar 2015: https://geiselmed.dartmouth.edu/news/2015/researchers-discover-why-some-mushrooms-glow/ ; Wikipedia, "Foxfire" |
| The fungus recycles the leftovers into new fuel (luciferin made from caffeic acid in a cycle; oxyluciferin is turned back into caffeic acid) | Kaskova et al. 2017 (Science Advances): "a biosynthetic cycle that makes fungal luciferin from caffeic acid"; review figure of the caffeic acid cycle |
| Foxfire lit the instruments (compass needle) of the Turtle submarine, built 1775, used 1776 | Wikipedia, "Turtle (submersible)": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turtle_(submersible) ; Wikipedia, "Foxfire" (compass and barometer) |
| 2015 study in Brazil (Neonothopanus gardneri): fake acrylic mushrooms lit by green LEDs drew more insects than dark ones | Oliveira et al., "Circadian control sheds light on fungal bioluminescence", Current Biology (2015), doi:10.1016/j.cub.2015.02.021; Sci.News: https://www.sci.news/biology/science-study-why-some-fungi-glow-neonothopanus-gardneri-02623.html ; The Conversation: https://theconversation.com/some-mushrooms-glow-in-the-dark-heres-why-39075 |
| Insects may carry the spores (the authors' hypothesis) | Dartmouth news 2015; Sci.News |
| The glow follows a (temperature-compensated circadian) clock, likely to save energy, lighting up when it is easy to see | Dartmouth news 2015: "turning on the light only when it's easy to see"; Sci.News |
| The fungus glows at night / mostly at night | The Conversation 2015 ("the fungus glows only at night") |
