# honey: sources

1. Nicolson, S. W., Human, H. & Pirk, C. W. W. (2022), "Honey bees save energy in honey processing by dehydrating nectar before returning to the nest", Scientific Reports 12: 16224 (University of Pretoria, Dept. of Zoology & Entomology). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9519551/
   - "Honey bees process nectar into honey by active evaporation on the tongue", "passive evaporation involving nest ventilation and fanning behaviour" and "enzymatic action".
   - Nectar is "collected at concentrations ranging from 20 to 60% w/w" sugar (so 40-80% water) and must be concentrated to over 80% sugar for storage.
   - "nectar in their crops has approximately twice the sugar concentration of the fresh nectar in flowers."
   - "Returning foragers pass their nectar loads by trophallaxis to receiver bees."
   - "Individual bees first actively evaporate the nectar by repeated regurgitation and re-ingestion of nectar droplets" ("tongue lashing"); sucrose hydrolysis "with enzymes being added to nectar in the crop".
2. NC State University News, "How Do Bees Make Honey?" (June 2013): https://news.ncsu.edu/2013/06/how-do-bees-make-honey
   - Processor bees "add an enzyme called invertase every time they regurgitate their nectar"; invertase splits sucrose into glucose and fructose.
   - Fanning their wings "creates airflow around the honeycomb and helps water evaporate"; nectar is usually about 70% water; "by definition, honey contains less than 18.6 percent water"; ripe cells are capped with wax.
   - (Fetched via search snippets; the page returned HTTP 503 when fetched directly on Oct 28.)
3. Gland studies: a 2015 PLoS ONE study (https://doaj.org/article/61d5c926d8104f0bbf3fdabfda0127e5): "nurse bee HPGs secrete mainly major royal jelly proteins, whereas forager HPGs secrete mainly α-glucosidase III", the enzyme (the bees' invertase) that splits nectar sucrose into glucose and fructose; a 1997 European Journal of Biochemistry paper reports the same switch "from bee-milk proteins to alpha-glucosidase".
4. Texas A&M AgriLife Extension, Department of Entomology, "Honey Judging and Standards": https://extensionentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2015/04/Honey-Judging-and-Standards.pdf
   - "Water content above 18.6% is disqualified"; fermentation disqualifies honey; follows the USDA grade standards.
5. USDA Agricultural Marketing Service, "United States Standards for Grades of Extracted Honey": https://www.ams.usda.gov/grades-standards/extracted-honey-grades-and-standards (Grade A and B: moisture no more than 18.6%).
6. Government of Western Australia, DPIRD, "Removing honey from the hive": bees cap the cells with beeswax once the honey is ripe (moisture reduced during processing).

Wording notes:
- "about 18% water": ripe honey is typically about 17-18%; the USDA/Texas A&M limit is 18.6% (caption). The on-screen counter falls from 60 (inside nectar's 40-80% range) to 18.
- "about twice as sugary" in the crop: source 1.
- "Stored like that, it would ferment": sources 2 and 4 (high water and fermentation; that is why honey is dried before it is capped).
- The invertase cut is drawn as two hexagon rings (sucrose = glucose + fructose), a schematic, not the real structure.
