# Sources: hurricane ("How a hurricane forms")

| Claim in the reel | Source |
|---|---|
| Hurricanes are low-pressure systems with organized thunderstorms over tropical/subtropical water; they draw energy from warm ocean water | NOAA, "Hurricanes" https://www.noaa.gov/education/resource-collections/weather-atmosphere/hurricanes |
| Warm ocean water of at least 80 F / 27 C is needed (Wikipedia: commonly accepted minimum 26-27 C, sea surface about 27 C) | NOAA (same page); Wikipedia, "Tropical cyclone" https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_cyclone |
| Energy comes from ocean water evaporating into warm, moist air; the vapour condenses into clouds and rain as the air rises and cools | Wikipedia, "Tropical cyclone" |
| Needs moist air, an unstable atmosphere and low wind shear ("little change in wind speed or direction with height") | NOAA (same page); Wikipedia, "Tropical cyclone" |
| Rising, moist air spins because of Earth's rotation (Coriolis effect): counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere, clockwise in the Southern | Wikipedia, "Tropical cyclone"; NOAA |
| Cyclones rarely form within about 5 degrees of the equator, where the Coriolis effect is too weak (NOAA: usually at least about 200 miles north or south of it) | Wikipedia, "Tropical cyclone"; NOAA |
| Tropical storms have winds of 39-73 mph; hurricanes 74 mph or greater | NOAA, Saffir-Simpson scale |
| A calm, clear eye at the centre where air sinks; the eyewall around it has the strongest winds | Wikipedia, "Tropical cyclone"; NOAA |
| Over land the storm loses its warm, moist ocean air supply, and friction weakens it | Wikipedia, "Tropical cyclone" |

## Dropped / unverified
- "The low pressure makes air rush in": described qualitatively; it is standard physics (a low-pressure system draws air toward it) and not quoted from a specific line in the sources read.
- "Heat engine" is an explanatory analogy for the evaporation-condensation energy source, not a quoted term.
- The regional names (hurricane/typhoon/cyclone) were cut to keep the reel under 86 s.
- Sea temperature display counts up from 24 C to 27 C as a visual; only the threshold is a sourced claim.
- Storm size, spiral shape and eye are schematic, not a real storm.
- The reel's 74 mph threshold is the Atlantic/NE Pacific definition used by NOAA.
