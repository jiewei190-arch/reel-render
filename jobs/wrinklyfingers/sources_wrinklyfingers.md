# Sources: wrinklyfingers (Why do your fingers wrinkle in water?)
Researched Oct 28 2026. Primary/peer-reviewed sources first.

| Claim (script) | Source |
|---|---|
| Wrinkling is controlled by the nervous system, not passive soaking; it is "sympathetic nervous system-dependent" | Haseleu, Omerbašić, Frenzel, Gross & Lewin, "Water-Induced Finger Wrinkles Do Not Affect Touch Acuity or Dexterity in Handling Wet Objects", PLoS ONE 9(1):e84949 (2014), abstract and introduction. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3885627/ |
| If the nerve is damaged, those fingers stay smooth; doctors noticed it in the 1930s in patients with median nerve damage | Haseleu et al. 2014, introduction: Lewis and Pickering described "the absence of wrinkling in patients with median nerve palsy" (1930s; reference dated 1936) |
| In warm water, nerves make the blood vessels under the fingertips narrow (vasoconstriction) | Wilder-Smith & Chow, "Water-immersion wrinkling is due to vasoconstriction", Muscle & Nerve 27(3):307 (2003); cited in Haseleu et al. 2014: "water-induced skin wrinkling is directly linked to vasoconstriction" |
| Less blood means less volume, so the skin on top is pulled into folds | Wilder-Smith's account as reported by The Scientist ("The Science Behind Pruney Fingers"): constricted vessels below the skin cause "negative pressure and downward tugging" of the outer skin. https://www.the-scientist.com/the-science-behind-pruney-fingers-71410 |
| It happens only on hairless (glabrous) skin: fingertips, palms, soles | Haseleu et al. 2014: "Water-induced wrinkling of human non-hairy (glabrous) skin of the fingers, palms and soles" |
| Doctors use a warm-soak wrinkle test as a bedside test of nerve function | Haseleu et al. 2014: "the implementation of the wrinkling test as a bedside test of sympathetic nerve function" |
| A 2025 study: the same fingers wrinkle in the same pattern every time; the folds follow the blood vessels, which barely move | Binghamton University study (Laytin & German), Journal of the Mechanical Behavior of Biomedical Materials (2025); coverage: Science News Explores, "After every soak, fingers wrinkle — and always the same way" https://www.snexplores.org/article/after-every-soak-fingers-wrinkle-and-always-the-same-way ; Popular Science https://www.popsci.com/science/fingers-wrinkle-same-pattern/ |
| The leading idea is grip (the "rain tread" idea) | Changizi et al. (2011), described in Haseleu et al. 2014 as the 'rain tread' hypothesis: wrinkles "serve as drainage networks for channeling water away during grip in wet conditions" |
| In a 2013 study, people moved wet objects faster with wrinkly fingers | Kareklas, Nettle & Smulders, "Water-induced finger wrinkles improve handling of wet objects", Biology Letters 9(2):20120999 (2013), Newcastle University |
| A 2014 study using the same test found no benefit | Haseleu et al. 2014 (Max Delbrück Center, Berlin): "skin wrinkling did not improve dexterity in handling wet objects"; MDC press release: "could not reproduce the result ... even though they used exactly the same experimental design" https://www.mdc-berlin.de/news/archive/2014/20140109-better_grip_with_wrinkly_fingers__mdc-rese |

## Softened / careful wording
1. "Not just soggy skin": water does enter the outer skin (one proposed trigger is salt imbalance through sweat ducts), so the script never says water plays no part; it says the wrinkling itself needs working nerves.
2. "In the 1930s" (Lewis & Pickering, cited as 1936) rather than a single year.
3. The grip function is presented as "the leading idea", with the 2013 positive and 2014 negative results side by side; the reel ends with "still open".
4. The 2025 repeat-pattern study had 3 participants; the voiceover says "a 2025 study" and does not generalise to everyone.
5. The 12% speed figure in press coverage of the 2013 study is left out (press number, not checked against the paper).
