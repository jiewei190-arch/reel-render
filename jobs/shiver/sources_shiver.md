# Sources: shiver ("Why do we shiver when we're cold?")

| Claim in the script | Source | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Shivering = muscles tightening and relaxing, fast, to make heat ("muscles into a furnace", "shaking makes heat") | MedlinePlus (U.S. National Library of Medicine, NIH), "Chills" (https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003091.htm; alternative names "Rigors; Shivering"): "Chills are caused by rapid muscle contraction and relaxation. They are the body's way of producing heat when it feels cold." | government (NIH) |
| The hypothalamus works like a thermostat and can switch on shivering | OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology 2e, 24.6 Energy and Heat Balance (Rice University; https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/24-6-energy-and-heat-balance): "The hypothalamus in the brain is the master switch that works as a thermostat to regulate the body's core temperature"; "the hypothalamus can initiate shivering to generate heat" | university textbook |
| Sometimes a steady tremble, sometimes bursts | Haman F, Blondin DP. "Shivering thermogenesis in humans: Origin, contribution and metabolic requirement." Temperature 4(3):217-226 (2017), doi:10.1080/23328940.2017.1328999 (PMC5605160): "shivering patterns (continuous and burst shivering)" | peer-reviewed review |
| Muscles never turn fuel into motion perfectly; much of the energy escapes as heat | OpenStax (same section): "approximately 60 percent of the energy produced is in the form of heat" | university textbook |
| Shivering: lots of muscle work, no real movement, so the energy ends up warming you | MedlinePlus (above): the contractions are "the body's way of producing heat"; Haman & Blondin 2017: shivering skeletal muscle is "the most important contributor of heat production in cold-exposed adult humans" | government; peer-reviewed |
| People in cold water: metabolism about three and a half times resting | Martineau L, Jacobs I. "Muscle glycogen utilization during shivering thermogenesis in humans." J Appl Physiol 65(5):2046-2050 (1988), doi:10.1152/jappl.1988.65.5.2046: 14 subjects immersed to the shoulders in 18 °C water; "Metabolic rate increased during the immersion to 3.5 ± 0.3 (SE) times resting values" | peer-reviewed |
| That heater burns fuel, like the sugar stored in your muscles | Martineau & Jacobs 1988: "intramuscular glycogen reserves are used as a metabolic substrate to fuel" shivering (vastus lateralis glycogen fell from 410 to 332 mmol/kg) | peer-reviewed |
| A quieter heater: brown fat makes heat without shaking | Haman & Blondin 2017: "the thermogenic role of brown adipose tissue" explains part of the differences in how much shivering contributes (non-shivering thermogenesis) | peer-reviewed review |
| Shivering can show up when a fever is on the way | MedlinePlus "Chills": chills "often predict the coming of a fever or an increase in the body's core temperature" | government (NIH) |

## Softened / left out
- "About three and a half times" is one study's average in 18 °C water (not a general maximum); the 5-6× peak figures seen online are left out.
- The fever "set-point up" dial is a picture of MedlinePlus's "increase in the body's core temperature"; no thermostat numbers.
- Vasoconstriction, goosebumps and infants were left out (no fetched institutional source in this pass).
- NIH StatPearls (NCBI Bookshelf) blocked the fetcher with a reCAPTCHA; MedlinePlus is the NIH source used instead.
