# rainbow: sources

1. USGS Water Science School, "Rainbows: Water and Light" (secondary-bow explanation credited to NCAR / UCAR Office of Programs): https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/rainbows-water-and-light
   - Raindrops refract sunlight into a spectrum; the primary bow comes from one internal reflection.
   - Red primary-bow light leaves at 42°.
   - The secondary bow comes from two internal reflections, exits at about 50°, and its colours are reversed.
   - The lower the sun, the more arc you see; from higher up (an airplane) the bow looks more circular.
2. Arizona State University, PHS 208 "Rainbows, part IV": https://www.asu.edu/courses/phs208/patternsbb/PiN/rdg/rainbow/rainbow4.shtml
   - The drops making your rainbow lie on a cone with its apex at your eye. Red is returned at 42° from the antisolar line, blue at about 40°.
   - The horizon blocks most of the rain, so you see an arc.
   - From a mountaintop or an airplane, full circles can be seen on cloud layers below.
   - Each viewer sees a different rainbow (different drops).
3. Univ. of Nevada, Las Vegas (D. Jeffery), "Rainbow explication": https://www.physics.unlv.edu/~jeffery/astro/electromagnetic_radiation/rainbow_explication.html
   - Average primary angle 42.1°, secondary 50.8°.
   - Full rainbows can be seen from high buildings or airplanes.

Wording notes:
- "the point straight opposite the Sun: the shadow of your head": the antisolar point (source 2's "antisolar line") is where your head's shadow points.
- "about 50°" for the second bow: USGS says 50°; UNLV gives 50.8°; the main session's brief said ~51°.
- "There's no rain below the horizon to light up": ASU says the horizon blocks most of the rain.
- The drop diagram exaggerates the colour fan (marked NOT TO SCALE in the title block).
