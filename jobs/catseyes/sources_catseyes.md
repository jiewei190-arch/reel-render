# Sources: catseyes (why cats' eyes glow)

| Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|
| Cats have a reflective layer behind the retina, the tapetum lucidum; it is a retroreflector that sends light back through the retina, which is why the eyes appear to glow (eyeshine) | Wikipedia, "Tapetum lucidum": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tapetum_lucidum |
| Light that misses the photoreceptors is reflected back "for a second chance" (works like a mirror) | Dr. Cynthia Powell, CSU veterinary ophthalmologist, via Colorado State University news: https://newsmediarelations.colostate.edu/inthenews/night-vision-specialists-cats-bats-and-owls/ |
| It increases the light available to the photoreceptors (helps in dim light) | Wikipedia, "Tapetum lucidum" ("increasing the light available to the photoreceptors"; in cats it "decreases the absolute threshold for vision") |
| The catch: it slightly blurs the image | Wikipedia, "Tapetum lucidum" ("although slightly blurring the image"; in cats "increased light scatter ... slightly compromises visual acuity") |
| A phone/camera flash sits close to the lens, so light reflected straight back from the eye reaches the camera | Mental Floss, "Why do we get red eye in photos?": https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/19964/why-do-we-get-red-eye-photos ; Wikipedia "Tapetum lucidum" (retroreflector) |
| Humans lack a tapetum; flash photos show red-eye from the blood-rich choroid at the back of the eye | Wikipedia, "Tapetum lucidum" (humans "lack a tapetum lucidum"; "a weak reflection from the choroid" = red-eye); Mental Floss (choroid's blood vessels) |
| Kittens' eyeshine is often blue; adult cats range from yellow/orange to green | Wikipedia, "Tapetum lucidum" ("Young cats exhibit a blue appearance, which shifts to yellow with age"; adults "light orange to green") |
| Many night animals have one too: dogs, owls, most spiders | Wikipedia, "Tapetum lucidum" (dogs/canids section; "many nightjars, owls, and other night birds"; "Most species of spider also have a tapetum") |
