# Sources: earpop ("Why do your ears pop on a plane?")

| Claim in the script | Source | Type |
|---|---|---|
| The pop is a bubble of air entering the middle ear through the Eustachian tube | Stony Brook Medicine, Otolaryngology: "Ears and Altitude" patient handout (https://www.stonybrookmedicine.edu/sites/default/files/ears_and_altitude.pdf): "swallowing causes a little click or popping sound in the ear. This occurs because a small bubble of air has entered the middle ear, up from the back of the nose." | institutional (university ENT) |
| Middle ear = air pocket behind the eardrum; tube to the back of the nose "about the size of a pencil lead" | Stony Brook handout (as above); MedlinePlus "Ear barotrauma" (https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/001064.htm): "the connection between the middle ear and the back of the nose and upper throat" | institutional (university; US NLM) |
| The tube opens when you swallow or yawn ("stays shut until...") | MedlinePlus: "Swallowing or yawning opens the eustachian tube and allows air to flow into or out of the middle ear." Stony Brook: "Swallowing activates the muscles that open the Eustachian tube." | institutional |
| Cabin kept like six to eight thousand feet up | CDC Yellow Book, "Air travel" (https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/travel-air-sea/air-travel.html): cabin pressure "typically maintained at an equivalent of 1,830–2,440 m" (6,000–8,000 ft). FAA rule 14 CFR 25.841 (https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/25.841): cabin pressure altitude of not more than 8,000 feet | government / regulation |
| About three quarters of ground pressure | International Standard Atmosphere: pressure at 8,000 ft ≈ 75 kPa vs 101.3 kPa at sea level (≈ 74%); at 6,000 ft ≈ 81%. Said as "about three quarters" (true at 8,000 ft; four fifths at 6,000 ft) | standard physics table (e.g. Engineering ToolBox "Altitude above sea level and air pressure") |
| On the climb, middle-ear air pushes out; a swallow lets it escape | Stony Brook (pressure equalises through the tube); MedlinePlus (air flows "into or out of" the middle ear) | institutional |
| On the way down cabin pressure climbs back up; ear trouble happens most on descent | CDC Yellow Book: barotrauma "most commonly occurs" during descent "when cabin pressure quickly rises". Mirza & Richardson, "Otic barotrauma from air travel", J Laryngol Otol 2005;119(5):366-70, PMID 15949100: "developing after ascent or more usually descent" | government; peer-reviewed review |
| Eardrum pushed inward and stretched; muffled, full, painful | Stony Brook: a vacuum "sucking the eardrum inward and stretching it... sounds are muffled or blocked, and the stretching can be painful". KidsHealth (Nemours) "Flying and your child's ears" (https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/flying-ears.html): "the higher air pressure pushes on one side of the eardrum and causes pain" | institutional |
| The catch: air has to get in, the tube has to open (especially when landing) | Stony Brook: "the Eustachian tube must open frequently and wide enough to equalize the changes in pressure. This is especially true when the airplane is landing" | institutional |
| Swallow, yawn or chew gum | Stony Brook: "Swallowing occurs more often when chewing gum... Yawning is even better." | institutional |
| Babies can't pop their ears on purpose; their tubes are narrower | Stony Brook: "Babies cannot intentionally pop their ears... Children are especially vulnerable to blockages because their Eustachian tubes are narrower than in adults." KidsHealth: "the relatively narrow Eustachian tubes" | institutional |
| So many cry near landing; a bottle or pacifier helps them swallow | KidsHealth: "That's why so many babies cry during those last few minutes of the flight"; "Take a bottle or pacifier or breastfeed... It encourages swallowing, which opens the Eustachian tubes." | institutional |

## Softened / left out
- The "flutter valve" model (the tube lets air out easily but resists air coming in) appears only on secondary sites, so the script says only that air has to get *in* and the tube has to *open*, which Stony Brook and CDC support.
- "Three quarters" is the 8,000 ft figure; the on-screen gauge says "≈ ¾".
- No decongestant or Valsalva advice (health topic: educational only, not medical advice).
- The ear drawing is a simplified cross-section (proportions stylised).
