# Sources: microwavelight ("Why does a microwave have a metal mesh on the door?")

| # | Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Microwaves are a kind of light (electromagnetic waves), just with much longer waves | NASA Science, "Microwaves" (Tour of the Electromagnetic Spectrum) https://science.nasa.gov/ems/06_microwaves/ ; Swinburne University (below) |
| 2 | In your oven each wave is about 12 cm long | Dr Manas Kumar Haldar, Swinburne University of Technology Sarawak, "Why you don't get cooked by your microwave oven" https://www.swinburne.edu.my/?p=4929 : "for a frequency of 2.45 GHz used in a microwave oven, the wavelength is 12.2 cm" |
| 3 | The mesh holes are only a few millimetres across, much smaller than the wave, so the mesh blocks it | Swinburne (above): "a mesh with holes which are much smaller than the wavelength of the waves can block the wave ... the holes are much smaller than this value. This blocks the RF." Hole size: typically around 1-2 mm (The Straight Dope, "What keeps microwave radiation from leaking out the oven door?"); worded "a few millimeters" |
| 4 | The metal bounces the microwaves back inside; it's the idea behind a Faraday cage | Swinburne (above) on the mesh blocking; Wikipedia, "Faraday cage" (microwave ovens use a metal enclosure/screen as a Faraday cage to keep the microwaves in). Worded "the idea behind" |
| 5 | Visible light has tiny waves, less than a thousandth of a millimetre, so it slips through and you can watch your food | Swinburne (above): "the wavelength of visible light ranges from about 0.4 to 0.7 micrometers. So the holes cannot block light from the light bulb inside" |
| 6 | Trapped inside, the waves stack up into hot and cold spots about 6 cm apart (standing waves) | University of Arizona, Wyant College of Optical Sciences outreach, "Measure the Speed of Light with a Microwave" https://wp.optics.arizona.edu/oscoutreach/measure-the-speed-of-light-with-a-microwave : melted spots about 6 cm apart = half a wavelength; The Open University, OpenLearn, "Measure the speed of light with your microwave" |
| 7 | That's why the plate spins: the turntable carries food through the hot spots | OpenLearn (above): the turntable must be removed or the hot spots smear out; standing-wave cold spots cause uneven heating and the turntable moves the food through them |
| 8 | Take the turntable out, heat a chocolate bar, double the gap, multiply by the oven's frequency, and you get roughly the speed of light | Arizona (above): 0.12 m × 2.45 × 10^9 Hz ≈ 2.94 × 10^8 m/s, "close to the accepted value"; check the oven's label for its frequency |

## Numbers on screen and in the voice (main-session check)
| Number | Value used | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Oven frequency → wavelength | 2.45 GHz → 12.2 cm ("about 12 centimeters") | Swinburne University (university physics/engineering page, above); University of Arizona Optical Sciences outreach (2.45 GHz, ~12 cm) |
| Hot-spot spacing | half a wavelength, ~6 cm | University of Arizona outreach: melted spots ~6 cm apart = half the wavelength; The Open University OpenLearn |
| Mesh holes | ~1-2 mm ("a few millimeters") | The Straight Dope (secondary); Swinburne gives only "much smaller than this value". No primary page states an exact size, so the voice keeps "a few millimeters" |
| Visible light | 400-700 nm ("less than a thousandth of a millimeter") | Swinburne: "about 0.4 to 0.7 micrometers" |
| Speed of light from chocolate | 0.12 m × 2.45 GHz ≈ 2.94 × 10^8 m/s, said as "roughly the speed of light" | University of Arizona outreach |
FDA's microwave-oven pages (fda.gov) returned HTTP 401 from this session, so they could not be quoted directly; the two university pages are the primary sources.

Visual notes: wave drawings, hole sizes and the hot/cold spot grid are schematic. The numbers on screen (12 cm, 6 cm, 2.45 GHz) match the sources.
Safety: the reel doesn't suggest running an empty oven; the chocolate test uses a few seconds of heating (as the Arizona outreach page describes).
