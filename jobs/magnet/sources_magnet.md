# Sources: magnet (How a magnet really works)
Researched Oct 27 2026. Pages were opened and checked.

| Claim (script) | Source |
|---|---|
| Cutting a magnet gives two magnets; each piece keeps a north and a south pole | University of Illinois Physics "Ask the Van": "If you cut one magnet into two pieces you get two similar magnets, each has a north pole and a south pole." https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/12384 |
| Every electron acts like a tiny magnet with its own north and south | National MagLab, Magnetic Domains tutorial: "Every electron is a teeny tiny magnet. They have a north and a south pole..." https://nationalmaglab.org/magnet-academy/watch-play/interactive-tutorials/magnetic-domains/ |
| In most materials, electrons' magnetism cancels out | National MagLab (same page): "In most materials, the magnetic orientation of one electron cancels out the orientation of another." |
| In iron, unpaired electron spins line up with their neighbours | HyperPhysics (Georgia State Univ.), Ferromagnetism: "causes the unpaired electron spins to line up parallel with each other in a region called a domain"; the interaction "locks the magnetic moments of neighboring atoms" into line. http://hyperphysics.phy-astr.gsu.edu/hbase/Solids/ferro.html |
| Domains are about 0.1 mm to a few mm across | HyperPhysics (same page): "Sizes of domains range from a 0.1 mm to a few mm." |
| In unmagnetised iron the domains point randomly and cancel; a nearby magnet lines them up, so the iron becomes a magnet | HyperPhysics (same page): bulk material "usually unmagnetized because the many domains will themselves be randomly oriented"; an external field "can cause the magnetic domains to line up". National MagLab tutorial shows domains rotating as a bar magnet approaches. |
| Above about 770 °C iron loses its magnetism (Curie point) | HyperPhysics: "The Curie temperature of iron is about 1043 K" (= about 770 °C); Britannica, "Curie point" (iron 770 °C / 1,418 °F) https://www.britannica.com/science/Curie-point |
| Fridge magnets are stripes of alternating poles that grip on one side and barely on the other | K&J Magnetics, "Halbach Arrays": "the most commonly seen example of a Halbach array is the flexible refrigerator magnet ... sticks well on one side, but not at all on the other" (viewing film shows the striped magnetisation) https://www.kjmagnetics.com/blog/halbach-arrays ; Stanford Magnets: field on the other side "nearly zero" https://www.stanfordmagnets.com/the-magnetic-sticker-on-refrigerator-is-single-sided-magnet.html |
| No one has ever found a single-pole magnet (monopole); physicists are still searching | Univ. of Illinois (above): monopoles "just don't exist" in searches so far; CERN, MoEDAL-MAPP experiment "searches directly for the magnetic monopole – a hypothetical particle" https://home.cern/science/experiments/moedal-mapp |

## Softened / careful wording
1. "Every electron acts like a teeny magnet": we did not say electrons literally spin. Spin is a quantum property.
2. "neighbouring atoms line them up together" simplifies the quantum exchange interaction that HyperPhysics describes.
3. "about seven hundred seventy degrees Celsius": HyperPhysics gives 1043 K and Britannica 770 °C; other sources say 769 °C.
4. "Nobody has ever found one": no confirmed observation of a magnetic monopole exists; searches continue.
5. On screen, "billions of tiny ones" is shown as a zoom into tiny magnets. That is a picture, not a count.
