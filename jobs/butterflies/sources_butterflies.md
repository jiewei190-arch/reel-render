# Sources: butterflies (Why do you get butterflies in your stomach?)
Researched Oct 28 2026.

- Elliott B. (Lecturer in Physiology, University of Westminster), "Explainer: why do we get butterflies in our stomachs?", The Conversation, Feb 20 2017: https://theconversation.com/explainer-why-do-we-get-butterflies-in-our-stomachs-72232
- Harvard Health Publishing, "The gut-brain connection" (Jul 18 2023): https://www.health.harvard.edu/diseases-and-conditions/the-gut-brain-connection
- Harvard Medical School, "The Gut and the Brain" (HMS magazine, Winter 2017; Braden Kuo, MD): https://hms.harvard.edu/node/20976
- Cleveland Clinic, "Vagus Nerve": https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/body/22279-vagus-nerve

| Claim (script) | Source |
|---|---|
| Fight-or-flight; adrenaline is released | Elliott (The Conversation) |
| Heart speeds up; blood sent to the muscles | Elliott (adrenaline raises heart rate); fight-or-flight shunts blood toward skeletal muscle |
| Adrenaline shunts blood away from the gut; digestion slows | Elliott: adrenaline "shunts blood away from the gut"; "slowed digestion" |
| Physiologists think the stomach's own nerves notice the shortage = the flutter | Elliott: the reduced gut blood flow lets the stomach's sensory nerves signal the shortage, producing the "butterflies" feeling (hedged as "physiologists think") |
| The gut has its own nervous system, often called the "second brain" | HMS: "The enteric nervous system ... is often called the body's 'second brain'" |
| The vagus nerve carries signals between brain and gut | Cleveland Clinic: "The vagal nerves carry signals between your brain, heart and digestive system" |
| A Harvard gut doctor: "immense crosstalk"; a stressed brain can upset the gut, a troubled gut can signal back | HMS (Braden Kuo: "There is immense crosstalk between these two large nerve centers"); Harvard Health ("A troubled intestine can send signals to the brain, just as a troubled brain can send signals to the gut") |
| Stress can show up as nausea, cramps or butterflies | HMS: "abdominal pain, diarrhea, nausea, or 'butterflies'" |
| Rest-and-digest takes over again afterwards | Cleveland Clinic: the parasympathetic system controls "rest and digest"; Elliott: the two branches act in opposition |
