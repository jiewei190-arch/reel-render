# earringing: sources

1. NIDCD (NIH), "Noise-Induced Hearing Loss": https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/noise-induced-hearing-loss
   - Sound makes the hair cells in the cochlea move; their stereocilia bend and send an electrical signal to the brain.
   - Most noise-induced hearing loss comes from damage to, and eventually death of, these hair cells.
   - Unlike bird and amphibian hair cells, "human hair cells don't grow back."
   - "Long or repeated exposure to sounds at or above 85 dBA can cause hearing loss."
   - Headphones at max volume, sporting events and concerts: 94-110 dBA. Sirens: 110-129 dBA.
   - Loud noise can cause tinnitus (ringing, buzzing or roaring), which may fade over time.
   - Temporary hearing loss may recover within 16 to 48 hours.
   - Protection: earplugs or other protective devices; move away from the noise. NIHL is "the only type of hearing loss that is completely preventable."
2. CDC / NIOSH, "About Noise and Hearing Loss Prevention": https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/noise/about/index.html
   - The NIOSH recommended exposure limit is 85 dBA averaged over 8 hours; noise at or above 85 dBA is hazardous.
   - Decibels are logarithmic: a sound 10 dB louder is ten times more intense.
   - Tinnitus is "an annoying buzzing, rushing, or ringing noise in your ears or in your head."

Wording notes:
- "That's close to a siren at full blast": concerts top out at 110 dBA; sirens start at 110 dBA (NIDCD).
- "Muffled hearing often comes back within a day or two": NIDCD's 16-48 hours for temporary hearing loss.
- "your hearing system reacting to the strain": tinnitus after loud noise (NIDCD), phrased loosely.
- "Birds and frogs can regrow theirs": NIDCD says bird and amphibian hair cells regrow.
