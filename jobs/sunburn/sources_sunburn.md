# sunburn: sources

1. CDC, "Sun Safety" (skin cancer prevention): https://www.cdc.gov/skin-cancer/sun-safety/index.html
   - "UV rays can reach you on cloudy and cool days."
   - UV reflects off water, concrete, sand and snow.
   - "When the UV index is 3 or higher in your area, protect your skin."
   - Protection: shade, clothing, a hat, sunglasses, broad-spectrum SPF 15+; UV is strongest about 10 a.m.-4 p.m.
2. US EPA, "UV Index Scale": https://www.epa.gov/sunsafety/uv-index-scale-0
   - Follows WHO guidelines.
   - 3-7: protection needed; seek shade late morning through mid-afternoon; SPF 15+ sunscreen, clothing, hat, sunglasses.
3. Skin Cancer Foundation, "UV Radiation": https://www.skincancer.org/risk-factors/uv-radiation/
   - UVB is "mainly associated with sunburn"; UVA travels deeper into the skin.
   - "UVA can penetrate windows and cloud cover."
4. WHO, "Radiation: the known health effects of ultraviolet radiation": https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/radiation-the-known-health-effects-of-ultraviolet-radiation
   - Sunburn is caused mainly by UV-B: redness and swelling, blistering in serious cases.
   - "fresh snow can reflect up to 80% of incident UV radiation"; on a cloudy day, UV exposure may still be high.
5. NOAA Climate Prediction Center, "UV Index: effects of clouds": https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/stratosphere/uv_index/uv_clouds.shtml
   - Clouds transmit, reflect and scatter UV; generally, larger and thicker clouds let less UV through.

Wording notes:
- "thicker clouds block more UV, but not all of it": NOAA CPC plus CDC (UV reaches you on cloudy days). The often-quoted "up to 80% passes through clouds" figure was NOT used: no official page for it was found.
- "you can't feel UV. A cool, grey day can still have strong UV": CDC (cloudy and cool days) and WHO (cloudy days can still be high).
- "UV is usually strongest from late morning to mid-afternoon": EPA and CDC.
- Health advice stays at what CDC/EPA say (check the UV index; shade, clothing, sunscreen).
