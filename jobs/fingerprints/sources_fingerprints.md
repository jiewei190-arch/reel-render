# fingerprints: sources

1. Warman, P. H. & Ennos, A. R. (2009), "Fingerprints are unlikely to increase the friction of primate fingerpads", Journal of Experimental Biology 212: 2016-2022. https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/212/13/2016/18731/
   - A strip of Perspex (acrylic) was pulled across a volunteer's fingertip while a force gauge measured friction (University of Manchester).
   - The ridges reduced the actual contact area by about a third versus smooth skin, so on smooth surfaces prints lower friction.
   - The authors suggest other roles: grip on rough surfaces, draining water in wet conditions, resisting blisters.
   - Coverage: ScienceDaily, "Fingerprints don't improve grip, study finds" (12 June 2009) https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/06/090612092729.htm
2. Scheibert, J., Leurent, S., Prevost, A. & Debrégeas, G. (2009), "The role of fingerprints in the coding of tactile information probed with a biomimetic sensor", Science 323: 1503-1506. Preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/0911.4885
   - A MEMS force sensor covered with a ridged ("fingerprint-like") skin was scanned over rough surfaces (ENS, Paris).
   - With ridges, fine textures make vibrations at a frequency set by scanning speed over ridge spacing, which falls in the best-sensitivity range of Pacinian corpuscles (deep vibration receptors).
3. MedlinePlus Genetics (US National Library of Medicine), "Is fingerprint pattern determined by genetics?" https://medlineplus.gov/genetics/understanding/traits/fingerprints/
   - "The ridges begin to develop during the third month of fetal development, and they are fully formed by the sixth month."
   - Genes influence the overall pattern (loops, whorls, arches); the environment in the womb (position, blood flow, amniotic fluid, finger length) shapes the details.
   - "Even identical twins, who have the same DNA, have different fingerprints."
