# Sources: catsknead (why do cats knead?)

Institutional / vet-school source: Susan Hazel (Senior Lecturer, School of Animal and Veterinary Science, University of Adelaide) and Julia Henning (PhD candidate, University of Adelaide), "Why do cats knead?", The Conversation: https://theconversation.com/why-do-cats-knead-192743 (cited below as Hazel & Henning).
Veterinary sources: VCA Animal Hospitals, "Why do cats knead? (Or, the art of biscuit making!)": https://vcahospitals.com/resources/behavior-cat/true-or-false-do-cats-knead ; Vetstreet, "Why does my cat knead?": https://www.vetstreet.com/our-pet-experts/why-does-my-cat-knead

| Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|
| The internet calls it "making biscuits" | VCA's page title ("the art of biscuit making") |
| It starts in the first days of life: a nursing kitten presses its mother's belly, which helps get the milk flowing | Hazel & Henning: "Kneading is associated with suckling, which helps stimulate a mother cat's milk supply"; VCA: kittens knead to "stimulate milk production"; Vetstreet: it starts shortly after birth ("milk treading") |
| Each push helps release oxytocin, the hormone that lets milk out | Hazel & Henning (milk supply stimulated through the release of oxytocin); VCA (kneading may stimulate oxytocin) |
| Grown-up cats never stopped; scientists call it neoteny, keeping a baby habit as an adult | Hazel & Henning: "Neoteny is when an animal retains their juvenile physical or behaviour traits into adulthood" |
| Theory 1: kneading still brings back that comfort; it often comes with purring on a soft lap | Hazel & Henning: "In most cases, kneading likely indicates your cat is comfortable"; VCA: adults may knead to recreate the calm of nursing |
| Theory 2: cats have scent glands in their soft paw pads, which release pheromones, chemical messages for other cats; each press may leave a scent | Hazel & Henning: "Cats have scent glands in their soft paw pads, and when they knead, these glands release pheromones"; VCA: kneading may be one way they mark territory |
| Theory 3: wild cats trampled grass into a soft bed | VCA: outdoor cats may have kneaded areas to make them more comfortable for resting; Vetstreet / other vet sources list nest preparation as a proposed reason |
| If it turns frantic or nonstop, call the vet | Hazel & Henning: frequent, compulsive kneading, especially with sucking, may point to stress or pain; VCA: kneading much more than normal is worth a call to a vet; Vetstreet: sudden frantic or compulsive kneading warrants a vet visit |
| Claws out? A thick blanket on your lap | Vetstreet: claws during kneading are usually involuntary; a blanket on your lap is the better fix |

Theories marked as theories: the script says "The leading theory", "a second, sneakier theory" and "A third idea", and "each press MAY leave a smell". The "MINE!" stamp is presented as the scent-marking theory, not a fact. Cornell Feline Health Center and the ASPCA were searched; neither had a kneading page I could cite, so the University of Adelaide vet-school authors stand in as the institutional source.
