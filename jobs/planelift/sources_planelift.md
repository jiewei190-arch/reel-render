# Sources: planelift ("How do planes actually stay in the air?")

| Claim in the script | Source | Type |
|---|---|---|
| The classic answer: air splits, the top air must race to meet the bottom air at the back (equal transit / longer path) | NASA Glenn Research Center, Beginner's Guide to Aeronautics, "Equal Transit Theory" (https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/foilw1): "one of the most widely circulated, incorrect explanations"; "Longer Path" or "Equal Transit Time" theory | government (NASA) |
| NASA calls it one of the most widely circulated, incorrect explanations | same page (quoted) | government |
| Smoke tests show the top air reaches the back first | NASA Glenn, same page: "particles moving over the top arrive at the trailing edge before particles moving under the airfoil"; University of Cambridge, "How wings really work" (25 Jan 2012), Holger Babinsky's smoke-pulse film (https://www.cam.ac.uk/research/news/how-wings-really-work) | government; university |
| A sail has the same length on both sides and still makes lift | Cambridge / Babinsky (2012): a sail generates lift though its surfaces "are the same length"; "it's the curvature that creates lift, not the distance" | university |
| The wing (curved and tilted) bends the air downward; push air down, the air pushes the wing up (Newton's third law) | NASA Glenn, "What is Lift?" (https://www.grc.nasa.gov/WWW/K-12/VirtualAero/BottleRocket/airplane/lift1.html): "Lift occurs when a flow of gas is turned by a solid object. The flow is turned in one direction, and the lift is generated in the opposite direction"; "both the upper and lower surfaces contribute to the flow turning" | government |
| Bending the airflow changes the pressure: lower above the wing, higher below | Babinsky H, "How do wings work?", Physics Education 38(6):497 (2003); Cambridge (2012): "introducing a shape into the airflow, which curves the streamlines and introduces pressure changes": lower pressure on the upper surface, higher on the lower | peer-reviewed (education); university |
| These aren't rival theories; both are true at the same time; neither alone is the full story | Ed Regis, "No One Can Explain Why Planes Stay in the Air", Scientific American (Feb 2020) (https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/no-one-can-explain-why-planes-stay-in-the-air/): each of the two popular explanations "holds up on its own terms. Yet neither offers a full accounting" | published (magazine) |
