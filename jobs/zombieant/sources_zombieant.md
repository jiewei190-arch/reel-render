# Sources: zombieant (The fungus that turns ants into "zombies")
Researched Oct 28 2026.

| Claim (script) | Source |
|---|---|
| Ophiocordyceps (O. unilateralis s.l.) is a fungus of tropical rainforests that infects carpenter ants (Camponotus) | Hughes et al., "Behavioral mechanisms and morphological symptoms of zombie ants dying from fungal infection", BMC Ecology 11:13 (2011), Thai rainforest, host Camponotus leonardi. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3118224/ |
| The ants nest high in the trees, about 20 m up | Hughes et al. 2011: canopy nests "ca. 20 m above the forest floor" |
| Infected ants leave and wander in a random "drunken walk" | Hughes et al. 2011: "random 'drunkard's walk'" on the ground and vegetation |
| Spasms make it fall, so it can't climb back | Hughes et al. 2011: "repeated convulsions that make them fall down and thus precludes returning to the canopy" |
| Around noon it bites the vein on the underside of a leaf | Hughes et al. 2011: the death grip is "synchronized around solar noon"; ants "always bite into abaxial leaf veins" |
| About 25 cm up, in the humid understory | Hughes et al. 2011: dead ants "ca. 25 cm above the soil surface where conditions for parasite development are optimal"; infected workers leave the "dry, hot canopy" for the "humid understory" |
| Jaw muscles waste away, so the bite locks and it stays put | Hughes et al. 2011: mandibles penetrate the vein, "atrophy of the mandibular muscles" means the ant "will remain attached to the leaf after death" |
| In 2017 scientists found fungal cells around the ant's muscles in a connected network, not in the brain | Fredericksen et al., "Three-dimensional visualization and a deep-learning model reveal complex fungal parasite networks in behaviorally manipulated ants", PNAS 114(47) (2017); summarised by National Geographic, Nov 2017 https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/article/controlling-zombie-ants-fungus-spd |
| Exactly how it controls the ant is still being worked out | Same coverage: Hughes says more research is needed to determine the brain's role |
| Within days a stalk grows from the back of the ant's head | Hughes et al. 2011: "growth of a large stalk from the back of the ant's head", beginning "within 2-3 days after host death" |
| It releases spores to infect more ants; infection happens on the forest floor | ScienceDaily / University of Exeter, Aug 18 2010: the body stays put "so the fungus has time to grow and release its spores to infect other ants" https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/08/100818105730.htm ; Pontoppidan et al., PLoS ONE (2009): host trails "occasionally descend to the forest floor where infection occurs" |
| A 48-million-year-old fossil leaf shows the same bite marks | Hughes, Wappler & Labandeira, "Ancient death-grip leaf scars reveal ant-fungal parasitism", Biology Letters (2010), Messel Pit, Germany (ScienceDaily link above) |
| It can't infect people; our bodies are too warm | NPR, "The zombie fungus from 'The Last of Us' is real - but not nearly as deadly", Jan 30 2023 (University of Utah mycologist: cannot infect humans; human body temperature) https://www.kuow.org/stories/the-zombie-fungus-from-the-last-of-us-is-real-but-not-nearly-as-deadly ; Science News 2023 (species-specific) |

## Softened / careful wording
1. "Spasms" = the paper's "convulsions"; worded gently for a non-gory reel.
2. "releases spores over the forest floor, to infect more ants": sources say the spores infect other ants and that infection happens on the forest floor; the exact fall path of spores is not stated, so no "rain onto trails" claim.
3. "Exactly how it pulls the strings is still being worked out": the 2017 study found no fungus in the brain, but the control mechanism is unresolved.
4. "can't infect you": true today per NPR/Science News; some experts call future heat adaptation of fungi a hypothesis, so we say nothing about the future.
5. Visuals are stylised pencil drawings: the stalk is drawn as a plant-like line, no injury detail.
