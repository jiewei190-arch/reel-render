# carsick: sources

1. CDC Yellow Book, "Motion Sickness" (Travel-related infectious and noninfectious risks): https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/travel-air-sea/motion-sickness.html
   - "The most widely accepted explanation for motion sickness is the sensory conflict or neural mismatch theory": vestibular, visual and kinesthetic inputs conflict.
   - Children under 2 usually don't get it; susceptibility peaks at ages 7 to 12, then declines.
   - Prevention: a visual horizon reference; avoid reading; avoid head movements; car passengers can lean into bends.
   - Habituation is the most effective countermeasure.
2. MedlinePlus (NIH/NLM), "Motion Sickness": https://medlineplus.gov/motionsickness.html
   - The brain tracks movement from the inner ears, eyes, muscles and joints.
   - Sickness happens when the signals don't match, e.g. reading during a ride: the eyes see a still page while the inner ear senses motion.
   - Tips: the front seat of a car, look at the horizon, avoid reading.
3. M. Treisman, "Motion sickness: an evolutionary hypothesis", Science 197:493-495 (1977), PMID 731645: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/731645/
   - The "poison" or toxin hypothesis: the systems that detect odd sensory signals evolved to trigger vomiting after neurotoxins; motion sickness is a by-product.
   - The reel labels it a THEORY twice.
4. Bárány Society, "Motion sickness diagnostic criteria" (J Vestib Res 2021): https://www.doi.org/10.3233/VES-200005
   - Describes motion-induced vomiting as a by-product of an older protective response to neurotoxins (a hypothesis).

Wording notes:
- "tiny tubes of fluid feel every turn": the semicircular canals of the vestibular system (general anatomy; MedlinePlus "inner ears").
- "like a spirit level": an analogy, not a claim.
- "Kids get it most. It peaks around ages seven to twelve": CDC. The curve in the reel is qualitative, with no numbers on the y axis.
- "the more you ride, the more your brain gets used to it": CDC, habituation.
