# Sources: hairwet ("Why does wet hair look darker?")

| Claim in the script | Source | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Water doesn't dye the hair; it changes where the light goes, so less comes back to your eyes | NC State University, Electrical & Computer Engineering, Prof. Michael Kudenov, "Why do things look darker when they're wet?" (2024, https://ece.ncsu.edu/2024/why-do-things-look-darker-when-theyre-wet): the thin water film reflects light back toward the surface, so less of it reaches our eyes | university |
| Rough dry surfaces scatter light every way; plenty bounces back | Lekner J, Dorf MC. "Why some things are darker when wet." Applied Optics 27(7):1278-1280 (1988) (https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/scps/staff/pdf/darkerwhenwet.pdf): rough materials reflect diffusely | peer-reviewed |
| Light scattered at a steep angle bounces back inside the film (total internal reflection), and each extra bounce gives more chances to be absorbed | Lekner & Dorf 1988: diffuse reflection "makes possible total internal reflection in the water film covering them, increasing the likelihood of the absorption of light" (extends Ångström's 1925 model) | peer-reviewed |
| Water helps light slip into the strand: water and hair bend light more alike than air and hair | Lekner & Dorf 1988: the lower relative refractive index at the wetted interface means less reflection and more light entering the absorbing material | peer-reviewed |
| Wet sand reflected about half of what dry sand did | Lekner & Dorf 1988, Table III: sand albedo 0.182 dry vs 0.091 wet (shown on screen as 18% -> 9%, "Lekner & Dorf, 1988", "sand · reflected light") | peer-reviewed |
| The shine: a smooth water film reflects like a tiny mirror | Lekner & Dorf 1988: an optically smooth surface reflects specularly | peer-reviewed |
| The same trick darkens wet sand, concrete and jeans | NC State (Kudenov): wet jeans and concrete; Lekner & Dorf 1988: sand | university; peer-reviewed |
| When the water dries, the colour comes back | Follows from both sources (the film is what traps the light) | inference |

## Softened / left out
- The "about half" figure is tied on screen to sand (Lekner & Dorf's measured albedo), not to hair; no hair-specific percentage is claimed.
- The model was made for rough absorbing materials in general; hair is treated as one of them ("like most surfaces").
- Hair swelling and clumping strands (which also change the look) were left out: no institutional source was fetched in this pass.
