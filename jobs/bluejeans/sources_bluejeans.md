# bluejeans: sources

1. Levi Strauss & Co., "The History of Denim" (company history page). "One of Levi's customers was a tailor named Jacob Davis." "One day the wife of a local laborer asked Jacob to make a pair of pants for her husband that wouldn't fall apart." Davis put "metal rivets at points of strain, like pocket corners and the base of the button fly." "The two men received patent #139,121 from the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office on May 20, 1873." They "were called 'waist overalls' or 'overalls' until 1960, when baby boomers adopted the name 'jeans.'" https://www.levistrauss.com/our-story/the-history-of-denim/
2. Tracey Panek (Levi Strauss & Co. historian, LS&Co. Archives), "Pockets Full of History" (Jan 12, 2017). "The small pocket on the right front side is called a watch pocket, since it was intended to store a pocket watch." "There were four pockets on the original pants and all of them were riveted." "During World War II, we removed the rivets on the watch pocket to save metal needed for the war effort." https://levistrauss.com/2017/01/12/pockets-full-history/
3. U.S. National Archives, Prologue blog, "Forever in Blue Jeans… and in Court" (May 20, 2010). On May 20, 1873, Jacob W. Davis received patent #139,121 for an "improvement in fastening pocket openings": "the employment of a metal rivet or eyelet at each edge of the pocket opening." The patent rights expired in 1890, after which rivets on work pants became a common design feature. https://prologue.blogs.archives.gov/2010/05/20/forever-in-blue-jeansand-in-court/
4. US Patent 139,121 (J. W. Davis, assignor to himself and Levi Strauss & Co., "Improvement in Fastening Pocket-Openings", May 20, 1873). https://patents.google.com/patent/US139121A/en

Wording notes:
- "something lots of people carried in the late eighteen hundreds": Levi's blog calls the pocket watch "a typical possession during the late 1800s".
- "rivets showed up on everyone's work pants": the National Archives post says rivets on work pants quickly became a default design feature after the patent expired.
- The reel doesn't name a city or a year for the customer's request (the sources differ).
- Spoken years and numbers are written in words in the spec ("eighteen seventy-three", "one three nine, one two one").
