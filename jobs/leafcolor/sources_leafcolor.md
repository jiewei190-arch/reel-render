# Sources: leafcolor (Why do leaves change color in the fall?)
Researched Oct 28 2026. USDA Forest Service and university extension first.

| Claim (script) | Source |
|---|---|
| Chlorophyll is the green pigment that turns sunlight into food (sugars) | USDA Forest Service, "Science of Fall Colors" https://www.fs.usda.gov/visit/fall-colors/science-of-fall-colors (chlorophyll is necessary for photosynthesis, which lets plants use sunlight to make sugars) |
| As nights get longer, trees stop making chlorophyll and the green breaks down | USDA Forest Service (same page): as nights lengthen, chlorophyll production slows, then stops, and the chlorophyll is destroyed; timing is regulated mainly by night length |
| Yellow/orange carotenoids were there all along, hidden by the green | USDA Forest Service: carotenoids are present in the chloroplasts all season; Utah State University "Ask an expert: what causes autumn leaves to change color" https://www.usu.edu/today/story/ask-an-expert-what-causes-autumn-leaves-to-change-color ; a Forest Service plant physiologist (NBC News, "What's behind those fall colors?" https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna15307812): "The yellow color has been there all summer, but you don't see it until the green fades away." |
| Reds and purples are anthocyanins, mostly made fresh in the fall | USDA Forest Service: most anthocyanins are produced in autumn, in response to bright light and excess sugars in leaf cells |
| A cork-like layer grows across the leaf stem; sugar gets trapped in the leaf | Purdue Extension FNR-FAQ-5 https://www.extension.purdue.edu/extmedia/FNR/FNR-FAQ-5.pdf (abscission layer; sugars trapped feed anthocyanin production); Clemson HGIC "Color changes in autumn leaves" https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/color-changes-in-autumn-leaves/ |
| Bright sunshine and that trapped sugar help make the red | USDA Forest Service (bright light + excess sugars); WSU Extension "Vivid Autumn Leaves" https://wpcdn.web.wsu.edu/extension/uploads/sites/57/2020/09/Vivid-Autumn-Leaves.pdf |
| Scientists think the red may work like a sunscreen | NBC News quoting the Forest Service: red pigments "act as sunscreen" by shading the leaf from excess light (voiced as "scientists think… may") |
| Warm, sunny days and cool nights bring the brightest reds | USDA Forest Service: the most vibrant colours come with warm sunny days and cool, crisp but not freezing nights |
| Brown comes last: tannins | Utah State University (tannins are the last pigment left before the leaf falls); Purdue FNR-FAQ-5 (browns from tannins) |
| The cork-like layer seals off and the leaf lets go | Purdue FNR-FAQ-5; Clemson HGIC (the layer seals off the leaf, which then drops) |

## Softened / careful wording
1. "Scientists think it may work like a sunscreen": the photoprotection idea is a leading hypothesis, not settled.
2. "Most are made fresh in the fall" (not "all").
3. The cork-like layer and trapped sugar are told in the order the extension pages give, without exact timing.
4. Frost/drought effects are left out (sources differ on details).
