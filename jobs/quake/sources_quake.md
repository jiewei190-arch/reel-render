# Sources: quake (why earthquakes can't be predicted)

| Claim in the reel | Source |
|---|---|
| Neither the USGS nor any other scientists have ever predicted a major earthquake; "we do not know how, and we do not expect to know how any time in the foreseeable future" | USGS FAQ "Can you predict earthquakes?" https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/can-you-predict-earthquakes (quoted via NPR, 7 Feb 2023: https://www.npr.org/2023/02/07/1154893886/earthquake-prediction-turkey-usgs) |
| A prediction must give date and time, location, and magnitude | Same USGS FAQ |
| Scientists give probabilities over years or decades, not dates | Same USGS FAQ (long-range forecasts "usually span several decades or more") |
| The action happens at depth, where conditions are poorly known | USGS scientist quoted by NPR (lab quakes may not look like faults at depth in the crust; USGS has drilled boreholes into the San Andreas) |
| Stress builds on a fault and is released in a sudden slip (stick-slip / elastic rebound) | USGS describes studying "how stresses build up on faults" (NPR article above); the stuck-then-slip picture is the standard elastic rebound model (general seismology; no single page fetched) |
| 1985: USGS announced a M5.5-6 quake was expected near Parkfield; average interval about 22 years | USGS Parkfield pages: https://earthquake.usgs.gov/learn/parkfield/pressrelease.html and Bakun & Lindh 1985 (Science), https://earthquake.usgs.gov/learn/parkfield/bakunLindh85.html |
| Forecast window: before 1993 | Bakun & Lindh 1985; USGS Eos 1993 summary https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70017620 |
| A M6.0 quake hit Parkfield 28 Sep 2004, 11 years after the forecast window closed | USGS preliminary report https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70029155 ; NASA Earth Observatory https://earthobservatory.nasa.gov/images/4874/parkfield-earthquake |
| About 1 in 20 (about 5%) earthquakes is followed by a larger one nearby within a week; you can't tell a quake is a foreshock until a larger one follows | USGS FAQ "What is the probability that an earthquake will be a foreshock to a larger earthquake?" https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/what-probability-earthquake-foreshock-larger-earthquake |
| Early warning detects a quake already underway, using the faster P-wave before the stronger S-wave; warning is seconds to tens of seconds | USGS ShakeAlert fact sheet https://pubs.usgs.gov/fs/2023/3033/fs20233033.pdf ; Caltech explainer https://magazine.caltech.edu/esblog/preparing-for-earthquakes-with-shakealert |
| "A stopped clock is right twice a day" quip | Attributed to USGS seismologist Susan Hough in the NPR article above |
