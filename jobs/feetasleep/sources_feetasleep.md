# feetasleep: sources

1. NINDS (NIH), "Paresthesia Information Page" (Disorders A-Z), as kept by BrainFacts: https://www.brainfacts.org/diseases-and-disorders/neurological-disorders-az/diseases-a-to-z-from-ninds/paresthesia
   - Most people have had temporary paresthesia, "a feeling of 'pins and needles'", after sitting with their legs crossed too long or falling asleep with an arm crooked under their head.
   - "It happens when sustained pressure is placed on a nerve."
   - "The feeling quickly goes away once the pressure is relieved."
   - Chronic paresthesia is often a symptom of an underlying neurological disease or nerve damage.
2. Cleveland Clinic, "Paresthesia": https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/24932-paresthesia
   - Transient paresthesia commonly happens because of body positioning that "puts pressure on a nerve or limits blood flow".
   - The pins-and-needles feeling "happens when you change position".
   - It doesn't last long; once you move, feeling returns to normal.
   - It is usually harmless. See a provider if it happens frequently or constantly and isn't related to position, or if it comes with weakness or loss of function.
3. NBC News / LiveScience, "What makes our feet fall asleep?", quoting neurologist Dr. Robert Daroff (Case Western Reserve University): https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna3076701
   - Pressure can compress arteries that feed the nerves, or block the nerve pathways, so impulses don't reach the brain normally.
   - Some nerves stop firing while others fire hyperactively.
   - When the pressure is released, pins and needles often intensifies until the neurons re-establish normal transmission.

Wording notes:
- "the tiny blood vessels that feed it": Cleveland Clinic says "limits blood flow"; Daroff names compressed arteries.
- "Some fibres stay quiet, and others fire too much": from Daroff (source 3).
- "the worst feeling comes after you move": Cleveland Clinic (tingling when you change position) and Daroff (it intensifies after release).
- "Nerves are like wires": an analogy; nerves carry signals between the body and the brain.
- Doctor line: Cleveland Clinic's when-to-see-a-provider list (often, not position-related, weakness).
