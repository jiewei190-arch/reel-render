| Claim (script) | Source |
|---|---|
| The Moon's gravity pulls on all of Earth, unevenly: hardest on the near side, weakest on the far side | NASA: "The strongest pull occurs on the points closest to the Moon, and the weakest on the points farthest away" |
| Near side: the ocean is pulled toward the Moon (one bulge) | NOAA tutorial: water on the "near side" is pulled toward the moon |
| Far side: the water tends to keep going and lags behind (second bulge) | NOAA tutorial: "inertia exceeds the gravitational force, and the water tries to keep going in a straight line"; NASA: bulges on both sides |
| Earth spins through both bulges: most coasts get two highs and two lows a day | NASA: "Most shorelines experience two high and low tides per day" |
| High tide comes a little later each day as the Moon moves on (~50 min) | NASA: the cycle is "a little over 12 hours"; ~50 min extra rotation to catch the Moon |
| The Sun pulls too, about half as strongly, because it's so far away | NOAA: "the sun's tide-generating force is about half that of the moon"; the Sun is ~390 times farther away |
| New and full moon: Sun, Earth, Moon in line, pulls add: spring tides | NOAA tutorial ("additive effect"); NASA |
| Spring tides have nothing to do with spring; "springing forth", twice a month, all year | NOAA facts: "nothing to do with the season of spring"; "springing forth"; "twice each lunar month all year long" |
| Right angles: partly cancel: neap tides | NOAA: "the solar tide partially cancels out the lunar tide"; NASA |
| Coastlines and seafloor change the details, tides differ from place to place | NOAA SciJinks (continents block the water; tide ranges vary by location) |
