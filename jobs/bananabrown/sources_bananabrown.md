# Sources: bananabrown ("Why do bananas turn brown?")

| Claim in the script | Source | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Brown banana spots are made of melanin, the same kind of pigment as a suntan | Chang L-Y, Sargent SA, Kim J, Brecht JK. "Delaying ripening using 1-MCP reveals chilling injury symptom development at the putative chilling threshold temperature for mature green banana." Frontiers in Plant Science 13:966789 (2022), doi:10.3389/fpls.2022.966789 (University of Florida, Horticultural Sciences; PMC9515583): the browning o-quinone "would then polymerize to produce melanins as the major browning substance" | peer-reviewed (university) |
| Bananas make their own ripening gas, ethylene | Chang et al. 2022: banana is a "climacteric tropical fruit"; "ripening-related ethylene production by climacteric fruits like banana" | peer-reviewed |
| Extremely sensitive: a trace as small as 15 parts per billion can start ripening | Chang et al. 2022: banana fruit are "extremely ethylene-sensitive. It has been reported that the ripening process in physiologically MG banana fruit can be triggered by 0.015 to 0.5 μL L−1 ethylene" (0.015 μL/L = 15 parts per billion) | peer-reviewed |
| Inside the peel, an enzyme and plant compounds are packed apart | Chang et al. 2022: "Lutoid vesicles in banana latex may compartmentalize polyphenol oxidase (PPO) and phenols" | peer-reviewed |
| The main compound that browns is dopamine (the same molecule the brain uses) | Chang et al. 2022: "Browning reaction of banana fruit results from the oxidation of mainly dopamine by PPO" | peer-reviewed |
| The enzyme uses oxygen to turn dopamine into brown pigment | Chang et al. 2022 (above, PPO oxidation of dopamine to quinones, polymerising to melanins); enzymatic browning needs the phenolic substrate, the enzyme and oxygen together | peer-reviewed |
| Bruises brown faster: crushed cells let everything mix at once | Scientific American, "Why does bruised fruit turn brown?": in intact cells the enzyme and phenolic compounds are separated; "It is the breaking down of the cell structure caused by the bruising that initiates the oxidation reaction" | published (expert answer) |
| Below about 13 °C, banana peel gets chill damage | Chang et al. 2022: banana is "chilling-sensitive", "recommended storage temperature range from 13.3 to 14.4°C"; classical chilling-injury symptoms include "peel surface discoloration, subepidermal vascular browning" | peer-reviewed |
| The peel goes dark brown or black, but the fruit inside stays fresh | Utah State University Extension, Fruit and Vegetable Guide Series: Bananas (https://extension.usu.edu/nutrition/research/bananas.pdf): "The outside will turn brown in the fridge, but the fruit will still be fresh and delicious." | university extension |
| Ripen on the counter, refrigerate once ripe | USU Extension (same): "Bananas ripen quickly at room temperature and can be stored in the refrigerator after they are ripe ... Be careful not to refrigerate bananas before they are ripe as they will not ripen properly." | university extension |

## Softened / left out
- "Same kind of pigment as a suntan": the paper calls the browning products "melanins"; skin and bananas make them by different routes (main-session wording).
- "Black" peel: USU says "brown"; chilling-injury literature describes dark discoloration; the script says "dark brown or even black".
- The 13 °C threshold depends on cultivar, ripeness and time (the paper's recommended storage is 13.3-14.4 °C); the script says "about 13 degrees".
- No 1-MCP, no ethylene-bag trick (no institutional source fetched for it).
