# Sources: daylightsleep ("Why does the time change mess with your sleep?")

| Claim in the script | Source | Type |
|---|---|---|
| A master clock in the brain, a cluster of nerve cells called the SCN | NIH NIGMS fact sheet "Circadian Rhythms" (https://www.nigms.nih.gov/education/fact-sheets/Pages/circadian-rhythms): "The human master clock is a large group of nerve cells that form a structure called the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN)." | government |
| It sets when you get sleepy, on a roughly 24-hour cycle | NIGMS: circadian rhythms are changes "over a 24-hour cycle"; "In the evening, a person's master clock tells their brain to make more melatonin, causing sleepiness." | government |
| Its main setting knob is light hitting your eyes | NIGMS: "Light and dark have the biggest influence on circadian rhythms"; the SCN "controls production of the hormone melatonin based on the amount of light the eyes receive." | government |
| Left alone, the body clock runs a bit long: about 24 h 10 min | Czeisler CA et al., "Stability, precision, and near-24-hour period of the human circadian pacemaker", Science 284:2177-81 (1999), PMID 10381883: intrinsic period "averages 24.18 hours" (= 24 h 10.8 min) | peer-reviewed |
| So shifting later is easy, earlier is hard; spring forward means darker mornings | Inference from the >24 h period (Czeisler 1999), supported by Kantermann 2007 (below); Fritz et al. 2020: spring DST makes "mornings are darker and evenings brighter" | peer-reviewed |
| In one study, sleep shifted after fall back but not after spring forward, especially for night owls | Kantermann T, Juda M, Merrow M, Roenneberg T, "The human circadian clock's seasonal adjustment is disrupted by daylight saving time", Curr Biol 17:1996-2000 (2007): sleep and activity "readily adjust to the release from DST in autumn but the timing of activity does not adjust to the DST imposition in spring, especially in late chronotypes" (late chronotypes = night owls) | peer-reviewed |
| Fatal car crashes rose about 6% in the week after spring forward (US data); no rise after fall back | Fritz J, VoPham T, Wright KP Jr, Vetter C, "A Chronobiological Evaluation of the Acute Effects of Daylight Saving Time on Traffic Accident Risk", Curr Biol 30:729-735 (2020), PMID 32008905: 732,835 fatal accidents, 1996-2017; "spring DST significantly increased fatal MVA risk by 6%"; "there were no effects of the fall-back transition" | peer-reviewed |
| Sleep doctors want to stop switching and keep standard time all year | American Academy of Sleep Medicine position statement, Rishi MA et al., J Clin Sleep Med 16(10):1781-1784 (2020) (https://aasm.org/advocacy/position-statements/daylight-saving-time-an-american-academy-of-sleep-medicine-position-statement): "these seasonal time changes should be abolished in favor of a fixed, national, year-round standard time" | professional body |

## Softened / left out
- No heart-attack or stroke numbers (AASM mentions "adverse cardiovascular events"; we did not find a primary figure we could read).
- "In the week after spring forward": the 6% is the study's estimate for the DST week, and the risk "waned" over the following week.
- The 24 h dial shows the 10-minute overrun exaggerated (drawn 6x) so it's visible; not to scale.
- No sleep tips (health topic: educational only).
