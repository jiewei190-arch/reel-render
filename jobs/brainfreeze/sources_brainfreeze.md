# Sources: brainfreeze ("Why do you get brain freeze?")

| # | Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Eating or drinking something cold fast brings on a sudden pain in the front of the head; it's harmless and passes in seconds to a couple of minutes | Cleveland Clinic, "Brain Freeze (Ice Cream Headache)" https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/21478-brain-freeze ("usually lasts only a few seconds to two minutes"); medical name sphenopalatine ganglioneuralgia (same page) |
| 2 | Your brain isn't actually freezing; brain tissue itself has no pain sensors | University of Pittsburgh School of Medicine, Pitt Med "Kid Stuff: Pain in the membrane" https://www.pittmed.health.pitt.edu/node/419 (headache pain comes from the meninges, not brain tissue); BrainLine Q&A, Dr Brian Greenwald, "Can the brain itself feel pain?" https://www.brainline.org/author/brian-greenwald/qa/can-brain-itself-feel-pain ("there are no pain receptors in the brain itself"); Prof. Peter McNaughton (Cambridge) on The Naked Scientists, "How does a headache hurt?" https://www.thenakedscientists.com/node/178440 . Houston Methodist: the pain "comes from your mouth, not your brain" https://www.houstonmethodist.org/blog/articles/2020/jun/what-causes-brain-freeze/ |
| 3 | It starts when the roof of the mouth (palate) is chilled | Houston Methodist (above): it happens "when the roof of your mouth goes from its usual, warm temperature to one that's much, much colder" |
| 4 | The leading idea: the vessels there tighten, then widen, letting warm blood rush in | Houston Methodist (above): "blood vessels in the area constrict and then relax", blood flow surges and triggers pain signals; Cleveland Clinic (above): vessels expand to let extra blood in for warmth. Worded "the leading idea" because the exact mechanism is still studied (Live Science, "What Is Brain Freeze?") |
| 5 | In one lab study, 13 volunteers sipped ice water against the roof of the mouth; as the pain hit, a big artery at the front of the brain widened fast, and as it narrowed the pain faded | Serrador J. et al., "Cerebral Vascular Blood Flow Changes During 'Brain Freeze'", Experimental Biology 2012 abstract (FASEB J 26:1_supplement); American Physiological Society release via ScienceDaily, Apr 22 2012 https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/04/120422231742.htm : 13 healthy adults, straw against the upper palate, transcranial Doppler; the anterior cerebral artery dilated rapidly as pain was felt and constricted as it receded. It's an abstract, so "one lab study" |
| 6 | The trigeminal nerve carries feeling from the mouth and the face, including the forehead; the brain puts the pain in the wrong spot (referred pain) | Houston Methodist (above): brain freeze "triggers this nerve", which "controls sensation, including pain, in your face"; the forehead pain is "referred pain". Popular Science, "What's a brain freeze and why do they happen?" https://www.popsci.com/science/what-is-brain-freeze/ (a major branch reaches the forehead) |
| 7 | 145 students ate ice cream; the ones told to finish in under 5 seconds got brain freeze about twice as often: 27% vs 13% | Kaczorowski M. & Kaczorowski J., "Ice cream evoked headaches (ICE-H) study: randomised trial of accelerated versus cautious ice cream eating regimen", BMJ 2002;325:1445-1446, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC139031 : 145 students (Hamilton, Ontario), 100 ml in under 5 s: 20/73 (27%); over 30 s: 9/72 (13%) |
| 8 | The fix: press your tongue to the roof of your mouth and warm it back up | Cleveland Clinic (above): "Press your tongue or thumb against the roof of your mouth"; drink a warm or room-temperature liquid. Houston Methodist (above): "Press and hold your tongue against the roof of your mouth" |

Cut or softened: no "about 40% of people get it" figure (secondary sources only); the migraine link is left out for length; the "pressure in the skull" explanation from Serrador is called speculation in the release, so it isn't used.
Visual notes: the blood-flow trace, artery icon, nerve branches and head are schematic, not data. The cafeteria bars use the real 27% / 13%.
- Pinned bonus fact: the BMJ paper lists the lead author, Maya Kaczorowski, as a grade 8 student (PMC139031).
