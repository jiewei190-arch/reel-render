# Sources: eggflip ("Why do eggs turn solid when you cook them?")

| # | Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Egg white is almost 90 percent water, with proteins in it | USDA FoodData Central, "Egg, white, raw, fresh" (SR Legacy 172183): water 87.6 g and protein 10.9 g per 100 g. On screen: "~88%" |
| 2 | Each protein is a chain folded into a ball; heat makes the chains shake, weak bonds break and the proteins unfold (denaturing) | Exploratorium, "The Accidental Scientist: Science of Cooking: Eggs" https://annex.exploratorium.edu/cooking/eggs/eggscience.html : proteins "twisted and folded and curled up"; heat jostles them, breaking the weak bonds, and "the egg proteins uncurl" |
| 3 | Unfolded chains grab each other and tangle into a mesh that traps the water | Exploratorium (above): the uncurled proteins "bump into other proteins that have also uncurled", bond, and "the water in which the proteins once floated is captured and held in the protein web" |
| 4 | The tight mesh turns clear into white and runny into solid | DESY / University of Tübingen, "The egg in the X-ray beam" (Mar 2021) https://photon-science.desy.de/news__events/news__highlights/archive/archive_of_2021/the_egg_in_the_x_ray_beam/PIF_eng.html : the proteins in the transparent egg white "unfold and cross-link" into "a tightly meshed, opaque network". The light arrows on the page are schematic |
| 5 | Each protein lets go at its own heat; the white starts to set around 63 °C, the yolk sets around 70 °C | Harold McGee, On Food and Cooking: the white begins to thicken at 63 °C and becomes a tender solid near 65 °C; yolk proteins thicken at 65 °C and set at 70 °C (also in the cooking-temperature chart at edinformatics.com). Note on the page: "80 °C white firm (main protein)": ovalbumin denatures around 80 °C (DESY above: "at about 80 degrees Celsius, egg white becomes solid and opaque"; PMC12191903, Gels 2025: ovalbumin Td ≈ 80 °C) |
| 6 | Keep heating and too many links form; the egg turns rubbery | Exploratorium (above): at high heat for too long "too many bonds form and the egg white becomes rubbery" |
| 7 | You can't uncook an egg in your kitchen; in 2015 chemists "sort of" did: they dissolved boiled egg white, spun it in a vortex machine and refolded one protein, lysozyme | UC Irvine News, "UCI, fellow chemists find a way to unboil eggs" (Jan 23 2015) https://news.uci.edu/?p=81927 : whites boiled 20 min at 90 °C, liquefied with urea, then a vortex fluid device (Colin Raston's lab, Flinders University) restored lysozyme; published in ChemBioChem. "Sort of" and "not a raw egg again, just one protein" because only lysozyme was refolded |
| 8 | The goal: cheaper proteins for medicine | UC Irvine (above): the method could cut costs for cancer treatments and other biotech work; Weiss: "It's not so much that we're interested in processing the eggs; that's just demonstrating how powerful this process is" |

Cut or softened: no "unboiled an egg" claim (only one protein was refolded); the claim that light scattering makes the white opaque is drawn schematically, not stated; no exact soft-boiled timings (they depend on egg size and pan).
