# Sources: cricketchirp ("Can you tell the temperature from cricket chirps?")

| Claim in the script | Source | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Count the chirps in 15 seconds, add 40 = temperature in °F | NOAA Office of Education, "Can crickets tell temperature? Answer is in their chirp" (https://www.noaa.gov/office-of-education/explainers/can-crickets-tell-temperature-answer-is-in-their-chirp): "Temperature (F) = number of cricket chirps in 15 seconds + 40" | government (NOAA) |
| In 1897, physicist Amos Dolbear wrote it down as a formula | Dolbear AE, "The Cricket as a Thermometer," The American Naturalist 31(371):970-971 (Nov 1897), doi:10.1086/276739; NOAA (same page): the law is named after Amos Dolbear, whose 1897 work is the most widely referenced early publication. Dolbear was a physics professor at Tufts College | peer-reviewed (historic); government |
| About 80 chirps a minute at 60 degrees, 120 at 70 | Dolbear 1897 (scan at journals.uchicago.edu): "80 per minute at 60° F ... 120 a minute at 70° F" (Dolbear's formula: T = 50 + (N - 40)/4, N per minute) | peer-reviewed (historic) |
| The cricket isn't measuring anything; it's cold-blooded; its body is as warm as the air | NOAA (same page): crickets are ectotherms, so "their body temperature depends on the temperature of their environment" | government |
| It chirps by rubbing its wings together; warm wings rub faster | NOAA (same page): "As crickets warm, they can rub their wings together faster" | government |
| Works best for one kind of cricket; Dolbear never named his; most think it was the snowy tree cricket, the "thermometer cricket" | NOAA (same page): "Dolbear didn't identify what species of cricket his formula referenced"; many believe it was the snowy tree cricket, nicknamed "the thermometer cricket"; Penn State Extension, Bug of the Month: Snowy tree cricket (Oecanthus fultoni) (https://pollinators.psu.edu/assets/uploads/documents/BOTM_SnowyTreeCricket.pdf): "called 'thermometer crickets' because their chirp rate follows the temperature" | government; university |
| Other crickets keep their own pace, so the same formula can be off | NOAA (same page): "Different cricket species may work best with a different formula for temperature" | government |
| On cold nights they slow way down | Penn State (same): "Chirping is faster in warm temperature and slows in cooler temperatures" | university |

## Softened / left out
- 15 s, not 14 s: the main session's brief said 14 s + 40 (a common variant); NOAA, the institutional source, gives 15 s + 40, so the script and screens use 15 s.
- "Most people think" for the species (NOAA: "many people believe"); Dolbear's paper names no species.
- No accuracy figure (the "±1.5 °F within 55-100 °F" claim seen online has no institutional source); no lower temperature limit given.
- The on-screen 30 + 40 = 70 °F example is arithmetic on NOAA's rule, not a measurement.
