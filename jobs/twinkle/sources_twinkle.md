# twinkle: sources

1. NASA APOD, "Scintillating" (2011 April 28), now on science.nasa.gov: https://science.nasa.gov/image-article/apod-2011-april-28-scintillating
   - "Atmospheric turbulence causes the image of the star to scintillate."
   - The star is "effectively a point source of light seen as a narrow bundle of light rays", so turbulence affects it more.
   - Its light varies in brightness and colour.
   - Mars, closer and showing a disk, is less affected and looks steadier.
2. StarDate, McDonald Observatory, The University of Texas at Austin, "Why do stars twinkle?": https://stardate.org/faq/why-do-stars-twinkle
   - Stars look like points; "irregularities in Earth's atmosphere cause starlight to dance around"; small shifts make "apparent changes in color".
   - Light passes through more air near the horizon, so the effect is stronger there.
   - Planets form a small disk "just large enough to counter the distorting effect of turbulence"; extended objects only twinkle through very large amounts of air (near the horizon).

Wording notes:
- "warm and cool, thin and dense": the atmosphere's irregularities (temperature and density differences that refract light), stated generally.
- "Through a telescope, it's a tiny disk": planets show a resolvable disk (APOD: Mars has a disk).
- "Each point wobbles a different way, so they average out": the disk is many points whose shifts average (APOD and StarDate, in plain words).
- "Even planets can twinkle there": StarDate (extended objects twinkle through very large amounts of atmosphere, near the horizon).
