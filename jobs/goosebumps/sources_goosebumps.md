# Sources: goosebumps (why do we get goosebumps?)

| Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|
| Under each hair is a tiny muscle, the arrector pili; when it contracts, the hair stands on end | Tina Hesman Saey, "Goose bumps may have hairy benefits", Science News Explores (Jan 8 2019): https://www.snexplores.org/article/goose-bumps-may-have-hairy-benefits ("When these muscles contract, they make hair cells stand on end"; "tiny arrector pili muscles") |
| A nerve (the sympathetic nerve) triggers the muscle in the cold | Shwartz Y, ... Hsu YC, "Cell Types Promoting Goosebumps Form a Niche to Regulate Hair Follicle Stem Cells", Cell 2020 Aug 6;182(3):578-593.e19 (PMID 32679029), abstract on the Harvard Stem Cell & Regenerative Biology page: https://hscrb.harvard.edu/publication/shwartz-y-gonzalez-celeiro-m-chen-cl-pasolli-ha-sheu-sh-fan-sm-shamsi-f-assaad-s-lin-et-zhang-b-tsai-pc-he-m-tseng-yh-lin-sj-hsu-yc-cell-types-promoting-goosebumps-form-a-niche-to-regul ("Piloerection (goosebumps) requires concerted actions of the hair follicle, the arrector pili muscle (APM), and the sympathetic nerve") [primary, university]; ScienceAlert on the Harvard release (Jul 21 2020): https://www.sciencealert.com/science-may-have-finally-explained-the-reason-why-we-get-goosebumps ("When low temperatures are sensed, these muscles bridge the gap between sympathetic nerves and hair follicles") |
| Fur standing on end traps air, a layer of insulation (a fluffy coat) | New York Stem Cell Foundation, "Why do we get goosebumps? The answer lies with the stem cells that grow hair": https://nyscf.org/resources/why-do-we-get-goosebumps-the-answer-lies-with-the-stem-cells-that-grow-hair/ ("standing hair on end traps air to create a layer of insulation") [research foundation; Hsu is a NYSCF investigator] |
| Our hairier ancestors were helped against the cold; with little fur, on us it's mostly a leftover | ScienceAlert (Harvard release): when humans were much hairier, goosebumps "would have provided some immediate defence against the cold"; NYSCF: humans don't have a coat of thick fur. Worded "mostly a leftover", not "useless" |
| The same nerve connects to the stem cells that grow hair (synapse-like contacts) | Cell 2020 abstract: "Sympathetic nerves form synapse-like structures with HFSCs and regulate HFSCs through norepinephrine" |
| In mice, a long cold spell made the nerve fire harder and the stem cells grow hair faster | NYSCF: "in prolonged cold, however, the sympathetic nerve activates at higher levels, causing hair follicle stem cells to jump into action and grow hair at a faster pace"; ScienceAlert: the analysis was done in mice; in the long term, "it appears to promote hair growth" |
| The goosebump muscle keeps the nerve connected ("plugged in") | Cell 2020 abstract: "APMs maintain sympathetic innervation to HFSCs" |
| Quick cold: hair stands up; long cold (mice): more fur grows | ScienceAlert: short term, hair stands up; long term, hair growth |
| Pinned: fear and emotion can trigger goosebumps through the same "fight or flight" (sympathetic) system | NYSCF (the sympathetic nervous system is the "fight or flight" regulator). The music link is asked as a question only |

Kept out: any claim that cold or goosebumps regrow human hair or treat baldness (the findings are in mice; Salon and others caution against that reading).
