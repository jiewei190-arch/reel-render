# Sources: catpurr (How do cats purr?)
Researched Oct 28 2026. Primary paper + university press release first.

| Claim (script) | Source |
|---|---|
| Purring happens breathing in and breathing out | Remmers & Gautier 1972, "Neural and mechanical mechanisms of feline purring", Respiration Physiology 16:351-361 (purring on both inspiration and expiration); Scientific American, "Why do cats purr?" https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-do-cats-purr/ |
| Classic answer: larynx (voice box) muscles twitch on and off, ~25-30 times a second, opening and snapping the folds shut | Remmers & Gautier 1972 (neural bursts ~20-30/s to the laryngeal muscles); described as the "active muscle contraction" theory in Herbst et al. 2023 |
| That chops the moving air into a rumble | Scientific American (above): the glottis opening and closing interrupts the airflow |
| 2023: air blown through cat voice boxes in the lab, no nerves or muscles working, still purred at 25-30/s | Herbst, Prigge, Garcia, Hampala, Hofer, Weissengruber, Švec, Fitch 2023, "Domestic cat larynges can produce purring frequencies without neural input", Current Biology 33(20):4727-4732; University of Vienna press release "Cats purr differently than previously thought" https://medienportal.univie.ac.at/en/media/recent-press-releases/detailansicht-en/artikel/cats-purr-differently-than-previously-thought (8 excised larynges from cats euthanised for terminal disease; self-sustained oscillation at 25-30 Hz) |
| Soft pads of tissue inside the vocal folds make them heavy, so they vibrate slowly; how a small cat makes a deep sound | Same paper/press release: connective-tissue masses up to ~4 mm embedded in the vocal folds likely allow the unusually low frequency |
| Muscles or pads? Probably both; the debate is still open | Herbst et al. 2023: data do not rule out muscle involvement; purring "possibly augmented by muscle activity"; univie release: our understanding is incomplete |
| Cats also purr at the vet or when hurt; it may help them cope | Scientific American (cats often purr under duress, at the vet, recovering from injury); Live Science "Why do cats purr?" https://www.livescience.com/animals/cats/why-do-cats-purr |
| Kittens purr while nursing | Live Science (Cornell vet: kittens purr from a few days old, while nursing, to stay close to the mother) |
| Some cats hide a cry inside the purr to ask for food | McComb, Taylor, Wilson & Charlton 2009, "The cry embedded within the purr", Current Biology 19(13):R507-R508; University of Sussex news https://www.sussex.ac.uk/broadcast/read/1208 (high 220-520 Hz peak inside the 25-30 Hz purr when soliciting food) |

## Softened / careful wording
1. "Probably both. The debate is still open." Herbst's paper itself says muscle involvement isn't ruled out; we do not claim the old theory was disproved.
2. "It may help them cope": no healing or bone-repair claim (that 25-50 Hz healing idea is a hypothesis, left out).
3. "25 to 30 times a second": the typical purr fundamental; some sources quote a wider 25-150 Hz range, not used.
4. Folds shown "slowed way down" on screen: real opening/closing is 25-30/s, far too fast to draw at 30 fps.
5. "Some cats" hide a cry: McComb 2009 found it mostly in cats with one-on-one owner relationships.
6. Lab voice boxes came from cats put down for terminal illness (not said in the voiceover; on screen just "cat voice box, in the lab").
