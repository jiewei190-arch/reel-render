# Sources: pumpkin ("Why a carved jack-o'-lantern rots so fast")

| # | Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A carved pumpkin starts to sag/rot within a few days; carved pumpkins often last only a few days to a week | K-State Research and Extension, "Picking and preserving the perfect pumpkin" (Pat Melgares, quoting horticulturist Cynthia Domenghini, Oct 17 2024): carved pumpkins last "sometimes only a few days up to a week" before rot is noticeable. https://extension.k-state.edu/news-and-publications/news/stories/2024/10/horticulture-preserving-perfect-pumpkins.html . Utah State University Extension (Nick Volesky, Oct 9 2020): a carved pumpkin can start to wither and rot "just a few days later". https://extension.usu.edu/news_sections/home_family_and_food/prevent-your-carved-creation-from-becoming-a-fungal-fiasco |
| 2 | An uncut pumpkin lasts far longer | Mississippi State University Extension, "How to extend the life of pumpkins" (Michaela Parker): pumpkins "generally have a long postharvest life"; "A pumpkin's lifespan is shortened once carved." https://extension.msstate.edu/blogs/extension-for-real-life/how-extend-the-life-pumpkins |
| 3 | An uncut pumpkin has a hard rind with a waxy coat that helps keep it from drying | K-State (above): mature pumpkins have "a hard rind" and a waxy coating that helps prevent drying |
| 4 | The rind keeps germs out; carving opens the inside to infection | USU Extension: "When you carve a pumpkin, it exposes the insides, making it more susceptible to infection through air flow." |
| 5 | A pumpkin is about 90 percent water | University of Illinois Extension, county newsletter (Oct 2025): "Pumpkins are 90% water"; the same University of Illinois pumpkin facts are widely reprinted (e.g. Daily Herald, 2007). K-State: pumpkins "have a high concentration of water." |
| 6 | Cut flesh loses water and dries out | USU Extension: petroleum jelly on the cut areas "helps lock in the moisture and slow the drying process"; MSU Extension: petroleum jelly "keeps the pumpkin moist" |
| 7 | The face shrivels and softens | USU Extension: infection can "cause carved pumpkins to shrivel, soften and start to grow fuzzy, grey mold." |
| 8 | Mould spores travel in the air, land and grow into fuzzy grey mould | USU Extension: fungi "release tiny, lightweight spores that allow them to travel through the air"; "fuzzy, grey mold" |
| 9 | Moulds like Penicillium and Aspergillus | USU Extension: "Some common interior molds include Cladosporium, Penicillium, Aspergillus and Alternaria." |
| 10 | Bacteria join in | MSU Extension: petroleum jelly "creates a barrier to bacteria"; a bleach solution is used "to kill any bacteria" |
| 11 | A real candle's heat speeds up the rot | K-State: "Avoid using a real candle inside the pumpkin, since the heat speeds up rotting." USU: an electric light or glow stick instead of a candle "will prevent the gourd from 'cooking' and spoiling faster." |
| 12 | Scoop out all the insides | USU: "Remove all of the pumpkin's insides to reduce the surface area where potential fungi can grow." K-State: remove all seeds and loose string. |
| 13 | Keep it cool and out of the sun | K-State: "keep them cool and out of sunlight to extend their life." |
| 14 | Petroleum jelly on the cuts holds in moisture; use an LED light | USU: "Rub the cut areas with petroleum jelly"; "Consider using an electric light or glow stick instead of a candle." |

The bleach-solution tip appears in all three extension sources but was left out of the voice-over (sources disagree on strength, and it is not a kid-safe tip to show in a Halloween reel).
