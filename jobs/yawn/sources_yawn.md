# Sources: yawn ("Why are yawns contagious?")

| # | Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Seeing someone yawn often makes you yawn; why is still debated | Gallup & Eldakar (2013), "The thermoregulatory theory of yawning: what we know from over 5 years of research", Frontiers in Neuroscience 6:188 (reviews the competing explanations). https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2012.00188/full |
| 2 | Just thinking about yawning made most people in one study yawn, or want to | Robert Provine's yawning studies: reading or thinking about yawning produced yawns or the urge to yawn in most subjects. Provine, "Yawning", American Scientist 93 (2005) https://www.americanscientist.org/sites/americanscientist.org/files/200510314450_646.pdf ; NPR, "The Wonderful, Mysterious Yawn" (Dec 2007) https://www.npr.org/2007/12/06/16962788/the-wonderful-mysterious-yawn |
| 3 | A yawn = a big breath in, a jaw stretch, a long breath out | Gallup & Eldakar 2013 (deep inhalation of cooler air; jaw stretching increases blood flow) |
| 4 | 21 of 29 dogs yawned after watching a person yawn; later studies were mixed | Joly-Mascheroni, Senju & Shepherd (2008), "Dogs catch human yawns", Biology Letters 4(5):446-448, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2610100 ; Harr, Gilbert & Phillips (2009) found little evidence (reported in later reviews), so "mixed" |
| 5 | Some chimpanzees yawned more while watching videos of chimps yawning | Anderson, Myowa-Yamakoshi & Matsuzawa (2004), "Contagious yawning in chimpanzees", Proc. R. Soc. B (Biology Letters suppl.), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1810104 : 2 of 6 adult females yawned significantly more to yawn videos (hence "some") |
| 6 | Idea 1, brain cooling: a yawn may pull in cooler air and blood, like a radiator | Gallup & Eldakar 2013: yawning may "act like a radiator by removing hyperthermic blood from brain", with "inhalation of cooler ambient air" |
| 7 | With a cold pack on the forehead, people caught far fewer yawns: about 9% vs 41% | Gallup & Gallup (2007), "Yawning as a brain cooling mechanism: nasal breathing and forehead cooling diminish the incidence of contagious yawning", Evolutionary Psychology 5(1). https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/147470490700500109 ; University at Albany report (2007) https://www.albany.edu/news/pdf_files/Yawning_Report_Gallup_May_2007.pdf : 9% with a cold pack vs 41% with a warm or room-temperature pack |
| 8 | Idea 2, social: yawns spread most between family, then friends, then strangers | Norscia & Palagi (2011), "Yawn contagion and empathy in Homo sapiens", PLoS ONE 6(12):e28472, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3233580 (observational study; Gallup has suggested people may just notice familiar people's yawns more) |
| 9 | The empathy link is contested: in 328 people, catching yawns tracked age, not empathy | Bartholomew & Cirulli (2014), "Individual variation in contagious yawning susceptibility is highly stable and largely unexplained by empathy or other known factors", PLoS ONE 9(3):e91773, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3954725 ; Duke University via ScienceDaily (Mar 2014) |
| 10 | Both ideas may play a part; scientists are still testing them | Gallup & Eldakar 2013 (contagion "at least partially mediated" by thermoregulation, alongside social factors); Bartholomew & Cirulli 2014 ("the vast majority of variation" unexplained) |
