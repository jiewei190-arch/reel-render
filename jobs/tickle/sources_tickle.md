# Sources: tickle ("Why you can't tickle yourself")

| Claim in the reel | Source |
|---|---|
| The cerebellum helps monitor movement and predicts the sensation when your own movement causes it, but not when someone else does; it uses the prediction to dampen the response of other brain areas | Scientific American, "Why can't a person tickle himself?" https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-cant-a-person-tickle/ (quoting Sarah-Jayne Blakemore, UCL) |
| The somatosensory cortex (touch) and the anterior cingulate cortex (pleasant sensations) are less active during self-tickling than when someone else tickles you | Scientific American (same article); Blakemore, Wolpert & Frith (1998), Nature Neuroscience, fMRI study: somatosensory cortex activity was higher for externally produced touch; the cerebellum was less active when the movement produced the touch |
| Blakemore's robot studies: a small delay between your movement and the touch makes it feel ticklish; "the longer the delay, the more tickly it feels" | Scientific American (same article) |
| Tickling (laughter-type, gargalesis) is thought to need surprise; self-touch has no surprise | Wikipedia, "Tickling" https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tickling |
| Aristotle proposed that laughter arises from being deceived or surprised, and that you can't deceive yourself | Wikipedia, "Tickling" |

## Dropped / unverified
- No delay numbers (e.g. "up to 2 seconds"): that figure came only from a secondary source that I could not tie to the paper. **Dropped.** The delay meter on screen is unitless.
- "Robot touched the palm after a button press": the exact device details were not confirmed. The reel only says a robot touched people's palms after their own movement.
- Light-touch itch (knismesis) can be self-induced; not mentioned in the reel.
- Brain positions and cartoon shapes are schematic, not anatomical.
