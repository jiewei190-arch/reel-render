# Sources: ouija ("Why a Ouija board's pointer moves")

| # | Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The pointer (planchette) moves because of the players' own unconscious movements | Wikipedia, "Ouija": "The action of the board can be most easily explained by unconscious movements of those controlling the pointer", attributed to the ideomotor effect. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouija . The Skeptic's Dictionary, "ideomotor effect": "The movement of pointers on Ouija boards" is due to ideomotor action. https://www.skepdic.com/ideomotor.html |
| 2 | In 1853 Michael Faraday investigated table-turning at séances | Faraday, letter to the Editor of The Times, 28 June 1853 (published 30 June 1853), Epsilon Faraday correspondence no. 2691. https://epsilon.ac.uk/view/faraday/letters/Faraday2691 . Wikipedia, "Ouija": "Michael Faraday first described this effect in 1853, while investigating table-turning." |
| 3 | Sitters rested their hands on a table, which seemed to turn by itself | Wikipedia, "Ideomotor phenomenon" (table-turning illustration: hands on a table, "the ideomotor effect causes the table to tilt"). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideomotor_phenomenon ; Faraday's letter (above) |
| 4 | Faraday put a simple pointer (index) between the hands and the table; it showed whether the hands or the table moved first | Faraday, Times letter: "a light lever, having its fulcrum on the table, its short arm attached to a pin fixed on a cardboard", its long arm "as an index of motion"; it "tells truly whether they are pressing downwards only or obliquely" |
| 5 | The hands were pushing slightly, without the sitters noticing | Faraday, Times letter: the cause was "a quasi involuntary muscular action" |
| 6 | Once sitters could watch the pointer, the table stopped turning | Faraday, Times letter: once the index is placed before the sitters and they see it, "then all effects of table-turning cease" |
| 7 | A year earlier (1852) William Carpenter named it the ideomotor effect | Wikipedia, "Ideomotor phenomenon": "The term ideomotor was first used by William Benjamin Carpenter in 1852." Skeptic's Dictionary: coined by William B. Carpenter in 1852 |
| 8 | The brain expects a movement and the muscles make it, too small to notice | Skeptic's Dictionary: "the influence of suggestion or expectation on involuntary and unconscious motor behavior"; Wikipedia, "Ideomotor phenomenon": motions made unconsciously, often minuscule |
| 9 | In one TV test, with every player blindfolded, the board stopped working | Wikipedia, "Ouija": the Brain Games episode "Paranormal" "clearly showed the board did not work when all participants were blindfolded" |
| 10 | A small 2012 study: unsure players scored 65% with the board vs 50% by guessing | Gauchou, Rensink & Fels (2012), "Expression of nonconscious knowledge via ideomotor actions", Consciousness and Cognition 21(2): 976-982, doi:10.1016/j.concog.2012.01.016; abstract: https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~rensink/publications/abs.12.3.html (verbal accuracy at chance, 50%; Ouija responses 65% when participants believed they were guessing). Also reported by Mental Floss, "The Spooky Science Behind Ouija Boards". |

Note: a 2021 replication attempt with a pendulum (Dienes & Moga) did not reproduce the 2012 effect (FORRT replication atlas). The script therefore says "a small 2012 study" and does not claim the board "reads your mind".
