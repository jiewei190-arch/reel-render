# Sources: auroras (what causes the northern lights?)

Institutional sources: NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center (SWPC) and NASA (Space Place, Heliophysics, Earth Observatory).

- SWPC, "Aurora": https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/phenomena/aurora
- NASA Space Place, "What is an aurora?": https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/aurora/en/
- NASA/NOAA solar maximum announcement (Oct 15, 2024): https://science.nasa.gov/science-research/heliophysics/nasa-noaa-sun-reaches-maximum-phase-in-11-year-solar-cycle/
- NASA Earth Observatory, "Historic Geomagnetic Storm Dazzles" (May 10-11, 2024): https://earthobservatory.nasa.gov/images/152815/historic-geomagnetic-storm-dazzles

| Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|
| It starts on the Sun, 93 million miles away; the Sun constantly blows out charged particles, the solar wind | NASA Space Place (the solar wind of charged particles from the Sun); SWPC (aurora driven by the solar wind) |
| Sometimes it hurls a giant cloud of them, a coronal mass ejection | SWPC: the largest aurora events follow coronal mass ejections that reach Earth |
| Earth's magnetic field catches them and steers them toward the North and South Poles | NASA Space Place; SWPC: the auroral ovals sit around the magnetic poles |
| They hit the air about 80 to 500 kilometers up and make it glow | SWPC, verbatim: "The aurora typically forms 80 to 500 km above Earth's surface." (electrons colliding with oxygen and nitrogen) |
| Oxygen glows green or red; nitrogen glows blue and purple | NASA Space Place: "Oxygen gives off green and red light. Nitrogen glows blue and purple." |
| The Sun has an 11-year cycle and reached its busy peak in 2024 | NASA/NOAA announcement, Oct 15, 2024: the Sun has reached its solar maximum period, which "will last another year or so" |
| In May 2024 the strongest storm in two decades pushed auroras over places that rarely see them | NASA/NOAA announcement (the May 2024 storm, the strongest in two decades); Earth Observatory: G5 storm of May 10-11, 2024, the most extreme since 2003, aurora seen unusually far south |
| In 2024 it hit its peak, and the years just after a peak still bring big storms; so check the aurora forecast | NASA/NOAA announcement (Oct 15, 2024): solar maximum reached, the maximum period "will last another year or so", and large storms remain possible as the cycle declines; SWPC publishes the aurora forecast |

Matched to the source exactly: the altitude is SWPC's 80-500 km, spoken in kilometres (a NASA page stating "60 to 250 miles" could not be found). Written to be true on a Nov 2026 posting day: the peak is described in the past (2024), and the claim is only that the years just after a peak still bring big storms. No claim about where auroras will be visible on a given night.
