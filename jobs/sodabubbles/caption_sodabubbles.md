Why do soda bubbles rise in lines? A new bubble is too tiny to survive on its own: the liquid crushes it back. It needs a pocket of gas that's already there. Champagne scientist Gérard Liger-Belair filmed these bubble sites up close, and most were tiny hollow cellulose fibers from towels, paper or dust stuck to the glass, not scratches (the glass's own bumps are far too small). Each fiber traps a gas pocket when you pour; gas seeps in, a bubble breaks off, and part of the pocket stays behind to start again, up to about 30 bubbles a second. That's your line. The bubbles grow as they rise, so the line spreads out near the top. Some glasses are laser-etched to give bubbles a place to start.

Sources: Liger-Belair, Scientific American (2015); Liger-Belair & Jeandet, Europhysics News (2002); Liger-Belair et al., Langmuir (2004) and J. Phys. Chem. B (2006, "champagne and carbonated beverages"); Physics Today (2018).
