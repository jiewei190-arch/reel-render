# Sources: firstsnowsmell ("Can you really smell snow coming?")

| Claim in the script | Source | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Snow is frozen water, and pure water has no smell | Common chemistry (water is odourless); Science Friday "Why Snow Has That Crisp, Clean Smell" (Feb 7, 2025) on snow and odour (https://www.sciencefriday.com/segments/snow-crisp-smell/) | general knowledge; science radio |
| Cold slows odor molecules down, so fewer reach your nose; "fewer smells to smell" in winter | Pamela Dalton, Monell Chemical Senses Center, via Discovery News / Environmental News Network, Jan 14, 2014 (https://enn.com/articles/46900-why-cold-air-smells-different): "We're not as sensitive to odors in winter," "And odors aren't as available to be smelled"; The Children's Museum of Indianapolis, "Why does cold air smell different?" (https://childrensmuseum.org/stories/why-cold-air-smell-different): "odor molecules moving more slowly when temperatures drop", "there are simply fewer smells to smell" | research institute (via news); museum |
| Before snow, the air often gets more humid | Mental Floss, "What's Really Happening When You 'Smell' Snow?" (Jan 16, 2025): "Right before a snowstorm, the air is more humid than usual"; FOX Weather "Can you smell snow?" (rising humidity ahead of a storm) | secondary |
| In one lab study, noses were more sensitive in humid air than dry air | Kuehn M, Welsch H, Zahnert T, Hummel T, "Changes of pressure and humidity affect olfactory function", Eur Arch Otorhinolaryngol 265:299-302 (2008), PMID 17901967: "thresholds were lower in humid, compared to relatively dry conditions" | peer-reviewed |
| A later study found temperature and humidity barely changed smell | Drews T, Nehring M, Werner A, Hummel T, "The sense of smell is not strongly affected by ambient temperature and humidity", Eur Arch Otorhinolaryngol 278:1465-1469 (2021), PMID 33094385: "neither temperature nor humidity had a significant effect on olfactory test results" | peer-reviewed |
| Nerve endings carry a cold sensor, TRPM8, the same one menthol switches on | Ordás P et al., J Comp Neurol 529:234-256 (2021), PMID 30942489: "The cold- and menthol-activated ion channel ... TRPM8 is the principal detector of environmental cold in mammalian sensory nerve endings" | peer-reviewed |
| They belong to the trigeminal nerve; it feels, it doesn't smell | The Weather Company, "The 'smell of snow' isn't really a smell at all" (weather.com, Oct 1, 2026): "What you're actually sensing is your trigeminal nerve"; FOX Weather explainer: the trigeminal nerve "is not responsible for smells, but provides certain sensations" | secondary |
| Monell researchers found the fresh, open feeling of breathing comes mostly from the nose lining cooling down | Zhao K, Blacker K, Luo Y, Bryant B, Jiang J (Monell Chemical Senses Center), "Perceiving nasal patency through mucosal cooling rather than air temperature or nasal resistance", PLoS One 6(10):e24618 (2011), PMID 22022361: "mucosal cooling (heat loss) being the most heavily weighted predictor" | peer-reviewed |
| Payoff: partly; mostly your nose feeling cold, damp air with the usual smells turned down | Synthesis of the above, hedged as the explainers do ("may account for the perceived smell of snow", FOX Weather) | synthesis |
| Your brain files that crisp feeling under "snow" | Alan Hirsch (neurologist) via the Children's Museum article: "What you think a smell will be impacts whether you like it and what you perceive it to be" | expert via museum |

## Softened / left out
- No ozone or geosmin claims (no source ties them to snow; geosmin is the smell of rain).
- The humidity clue is presented as "shaky", because the two lab studies disagree.
- "Partly", not "no": snow can carry odours (porous, traps smells, per Science Friday), so we don't say snow never smells.
- The "olfactory receptors bury deeper in winter" idea (Dalton) is left out: only paraphrased, no primary source.
