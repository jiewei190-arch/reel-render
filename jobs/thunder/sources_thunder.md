# Sources: thunder (why does thunder rumble instead of just bang?)

Institutional: NOAA National Severe Storms Laboratory (NSSL) and the National Weather Service (NWS).

- NSSL, Severe Weather 101, "Lightning Basics": https://www.nssl.noaa.gov/education/svrwx101/lightning/
- NWS, "Understanding Lightning: Thunder": https://www.weather.gov/safety/lightning-science-thunder

| Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|
| A bolt heats the air to about 50,000 °F | NSSL: "Energy from a lightning channel heats the air briefly to around 50,000 degrees Fahrenheit"; NWS: "may reach as high as 50,000 degrees Fahrenheit" |
| About five times hotter than the surface of the Sun | NWS: "5 times hotter than the surface of the sun" |
| The air expands so fast it explodes outward, then cools and snaps back; that blast is the sound wave, thunder | NWS: "The lightning discharge heats the air rapidly and causes it to expand. Immediately after the flash, the air cools and contracts quickly. This rapid expansion and contraction creates the sound wave that we hear as thunder." |
| A bolt is a long, crooked channel; every piece makes its own boom; the closest part reaches you first and farther parts arrive seconds later | NSSL: "the first thunder you hear is from the closest channels"; "A few seconds later, you hear a sharp click or loud crack from lightning channels a little farther away"; "several tens of seconds later the thunder from the most distant part of a flash has quieted to low frequency rumbling" |
| Sound travels about one kilometer every three seconds | NSSL: "Sound travels approximately one fifth of a mile per second or one third of a kilometer per second" |
| Up close, a tearing crack; far away, the air soaks up the high notes and only a low rumble survives | NSSL: nearby thunder "contains high frequencies" ("a tearing sound"); "higher frequencies are more quickly absorbed by the air"; NWS: "a sharp crack or click will indicate that the lightning channel passed nearby. If the thunder sounds more like a rumble, the lightning was at least several miles away." |
| Count from flash to bang: every five seconds is about one mile, every three seconds about one kilometer | NSSL: "dividing the number of seconds by 5 gives the number of miles to the flash, and dividing by 3 gives the number of kilometers"; NWS: "5 seconds = 1 mile" |
| If you can hear thunder, you're close enough to be struck: head indoors | NWS lightning safety ("When thunder roars, go indoors"; if you can hear thunder, the storm is close enough that lightning could strike your location) |

Matched to the sources: the temperature is NWS/NSSL's 50,000 °F (not the ~30,000 °C conversion), and the Sun comparison is to its surface only. "Lots of booms in a row" (not a count) for the many channel segments.
